On the evening of Thursday, October 29, 2026, roughly $6.3 billion in crypto was taken from Binance, the world's largest crypto exchange. It is the biggest crypto theft on record, by a wide margin. Within three days the story went from "possible security issue" to withdrawals suspended, frozen funds, an FBI statement and a restart plan.

This post is a snapshot **as of the morning of Sunday, November 1, 2026 (UTC)**. The story was still moving as it was written, so some details below will change. It explains what is known, who is saying it, what is still unclear, and what the episode means for anyone who keeps crypto on an exchange. It makes no predictions about prices and recommends no coins or exchanges.

## Who this is for, and who it isn't

**It's for:** people who hold crypto on an exchange (Binance or any other), people who saw the headlines and aren't sure what they mean, and anyone deciding how much trust to place in a platform that holds their money.

**It isn't investment advice,** and it doesn't say whether to keep or move your funds. That depends on your situation. If you hold crypto and want the basics first, start with [what is cryptocurrency](/blog/what-is-cryptocurrency-how-it-works-and-the-risks/).

## What happened, step by step

All times are UTC, and the sources are named because they don't always agree.

- **Oct 29, evening.** According to Reuters, the bulk of the theft unfolded in around 20 minutes, beginning late Thursday. TRM Labs, a blockchain analytics firm, says funds left Binance wallets across ten blockchains and that further sweeps continued until close to midnight. Tether and Circle, the issuers of the USDT and USDC stablecoins, froze funds linked to the theft within hours, and other issuers and a blockchain's security council did the same, according to Cointelegraph's timeline.
- **Oct 29-30.** Binance said it was investigating a security incident and suspended withdrawals. It said user funds were safe and that it would cover the loss.
- **Oct 30.** Binance's early finding was that the break-in "may involve a compromised DevOps engineer's laptop," which let an attacker reach an internal service and submit unauthorized withdrawals. Co-CEO Richard Teng later described a "complex attack" and said investigators found additional points of entry.
- **Oct 31.** Binance reported that it had restored backing for about 90% of the stolen assets, up from about 80% a day earlier. Late that day the FBI said North Korea was responsible.
- **Nov 1.** Binance resumed withdrawals in phases, starting shortly after 06:00 UTC, and said about 95% of the stolen assets were now covered.

## What is confirmed, and who says so

| Question | What's said | By whom |
|---|---|---|
| How much was taken? | About $6.3 billion | Binance; TRM Labs says about $6.36 billion at the time of each transfer |
| Who did it? | North Korea's "TraderTraitor" unit | The FBI, in a statement on Saturday, Oct 31 |
| How did it happen? | A compromised engineer's laptop, plus other entry points | Binance's preliminary account; not fully established |
| Were private keys leaked? | No evidence of that | Binance |
| How much is frozen? | About $3 billion | Binance, as of Friday, Oct 30 |
| Are user funds covered? | Binance says it will absorb the loss | Binance |

A few notes on that table.

**The attribution is no longer only a private-sector view.** Blockchain analysts, including TRM Labs and Chainalysis, pointed to North Korea in the first 48 hours. The FBI then said North Korea was responsible, and said it observed members of the group converting the stolen assets into ether and expected "further laundering." Reuters also reported that President Trump said Saturday that the US was still not certain North Korea was involved. North Korea has long denied using state-linked cyber units for theft, according to Reuters.

**The cause is still unsettled.** CNBC noted on Saturday that it "still isn't clear" how the attack happened. TRM's analysis, published October 29, said the attacker used an Ethereum feature called EIP-7702 delegation to take control of a Binance hot wallet, and described a likely compromise of the systems that sign transactions as its own assessment, not a confirmed finding. Binance's co-CEO said the attacker used software designed to delete traces. A fuller report is promised, so treat every cause you read, including this one, as preliminary.

**"Frozen" is not "recovered."** Roughly $3 billion was reportedly frozen, which is about 48% of the amount taken. Frozen funds have not necessarily returned to Binance, as one report noted.

## How Binance says it is covering the loss

This is the part most users care about, and the wording matters.

On Saturday, Binance said it had replenished about 90% of the stolen assets, and on Sunday about 95%. It said ten major tokens (BTC, ETH, USDT, BNB, SOL, USDC, XRP, USD1, RLUSD and ZEC) were fully backed again, and that the remaining shortfall is in less liquid tokens that "may take longer to source." It said it would keep buying until every affected asset is 100% covered.

CNBC made a point worth repeating: this does **not** mean Binance recovered 90% of the money from the attacker. It means Binance bought replacement tokens with its own money. According to Binance and Reuters, that money came from its **Secure Asset Fund for Users (SAFU)**, a reserve Binance keeps for security incidents, part of its futures insurance fund, and its corporate treasury. Reuters reported the SAFU holds about $1 billion, that a co-CEO said half of it would be used, and that Binance has committed to refilling it to about $1 billion. Binance has also said it set aside about $6.34 billion of company funds.

Using the reported percentages, here is what they imply on a $6.3 billion loss. These are rounded and illustrative.

| Reported coverage | Replaced | Still to replace |
|---|---|---|
| About 80% (Oct 30) | About $5.0 billion | About $1.3 billion |
| About 90% (Oct 31) | About $5.7 billion | About $0.6 billion |
| About 95% (Nov 1) | About $6.0 billion | About $0.3 billion |

The point isn't the exact figures. It's that **the safety net here is a company's own money and its own promise.** It is not a government guarantee like bank deposit insurance, and it worked because Binance had very large reserves. A smaller platform may not.

## Why this event is bigger than most

CNBC's chart of past incidents shows the scale. These are the amounts reported there.

| Incident | Date | Amount |
|---|---|---|
| Binance | Oct 2026 | $6.3 billion |
| Bybit | Feb 2025 | $1.5 billion |
| Ronin Network | Mar 2022 | $625 million |
| Poly Network | Aug 2021 | $611 million |
| BNB Chain bridge | Oct 2022 | $570 million |

The Binance figure is about four times the previous record, the Bybit theft, which the FBI also attributed to North Korea. TRM Labs said several features of this attack match earlier North Korea-linked thefts: small Tornado Cash withdrawals funding an operator wallet, preparation that began well before the theft, and signatures obtained in advance then used in one burst.

## What it means if you keep crypto on an exchange

This is general education, not a call to move your money. The lessons apply to every exchange, not only Binance.

**1. An exchange balance is a claim on a company.** When your coins sit on an exchange, the exchange holds the keys. You are trusting its security, its honesty and its finances. This is the custody risk described in [what is cryptocurrency](/blog/what-is-cryptocurrency-how-it-works-and-the-risks/): coins left on an exchange are only as safe as the company holding them.

**2. Deposit insurance generally doesn't apply.** Crypto held on an exchange is generally not covered by bank deposit insurance. Some platforms make their own promises, like Binance's SAFU. Read exactly what is promised, by whom, and what backs it.

**3. Reserves and proof matter.** Binance says it published a fresh proof-of-reserves snapshot, taken at 00:00 UTC on October 31, with a full zero-knowledge proof, which lets users check their own balances, due on Tuesday, November 3. It also said it moved all withdrawals to new wallet addresses that it added to that page. A proof of reserves is the exchange's own publication, and a snapshot is a moment in time, so it is useful evidence but not an independent guarantee.

**4. Keep only what you need on an exchange.** Yahoo Finance's guide for Binance users suggested keeping on the exchange only what you need for trading and moving long-term holdings to a wallet where you control the keys. Self-custody has its own risks, including lost keys and fake wallets, so it isn't automatically safer. For how wallets compare, see [multi-currency wallets compared](/blog/best-multi-currency-wallets-compared-one-account-for-several-currencies/).

**5. Spread your risk.** If you use exchanges, splitting funds across more than one lowers the damage from any single failure, at the cost of more accounts to secure.

## Scams after a big hack

Big incidents bring scams with them. Expect:

- **Fake "support" messages** that claim to be from the exchange and ask you to "verify" or "recover" your account.
- **Fake compensation or recovery offers,** often by email, text or social media.
- **Fake screenshots and claims.** After the hack, claims circulated that it had been predicted, and Binance's founder Changpeng Zhao said a chat screenshot behind one of them was fake, adding that he couldn't verify the others. Screenshots and videos are easy to fake.
- **Urgency.** Anyone telling you to act within hours is a warning sign.

Basic habits help: use a unique password and two-factor authentication on every account, only use the official app or website you typed or bookmarked yourself, and never share a seed phrase or recovery phrase with anyone. [How to read crypto news without getting burned](/blog/how-to-read-crypto-news-without-getting-burned/) has more on spotting hype and scam patterns.

## What is still unknown

- **How the attacker got in,** and whether other platforms are exposed to the same weakness.
- **How much is recoverable.** Frozen funds still have to be returned, and the attacker is reported to hold a large amount beyond issuers' reach.
- **When every withdrawal phase will reopen.** Binance said withdrawals would resume in stages. Binance describes the first phase as covering 15 networks with selected assets (Reuters calls it 15 cryptoassets), and I couldn't find dates for the later phases in the sources I could read.
- **What the full report and proof of reserves will show.**

## Common mistakes

- **Reading "95% covered" as "95% recovered."** It means replaced with Binance's own money.
- **Treating one source as final.** Figures differed across Binance, TRM, Chainalysis and the news reports, and early numbers changed.
- **Clicking "recovery" or "compensation" links.** They are a common scam after a hack.
- **Moving everything in a panic.** A rushed move can mean mistakes, fees or a fake wallet. Decide calmly.
- **Assuming self-custody solves everything.** It swaps company risk for your own responsibility.

## Getting started

1. **Check where your crypto is.** List what you hold on exchanges and what you hold yourself.
2. **Decide how much you could afford to lose** on any one platform, and compare it with what's there.
3. **Secure your accounts:** unique passwords, two-factor authentication, and a note of the official URL.
4. **Read the platform's own statements,** not forwards or screenshots, and expect them to change.
5. **Check back after the full report** and the proof of reserves, then decide what, if anything, to change.
