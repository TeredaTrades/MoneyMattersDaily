A TFSA and an RRSP both let your investments grow without the government taxing that growth every year — but they shelter you from tax on opposite ends of the timeline, and mixing up which one does what is the single most common mistake Canadians make with either account. An RRSP gives you a tax break the year you contribute and taxes you when you withdraw. A TFSA gives you no break up front and never taxes you at all. That one difference is what everything else below is built on.

## How each one actually works

**TFSA (Tax-Free Savings Account):** you contribute after-tax dollars — no deduction — and everything the account earns, plus every withdrawal, is completely tax-free, for any reason, at any time. For 2026, the annual contribution limit is **$7,000**, unchanged from 2025 and 2024. If you've been a Canadian resident and 18 or older since the TFSA launched in 2009 and have never contributed, your cumulative room is **$109,000**. Unused room carries forward indefinitely, and withdrawals get added back to your room — but only on January 1 of the *following* year, not immediately.

**RRSP (Registered Retirement Savings Plan):** contributions are tax-deductible, reducing your taxable income for the year you make them, and the investments inside grow tax-deferred. New room each year is the lesser of 18% of your prior year's earned income or the annual dollar cap — **$33,810 for 2026**, up from $32,490 in 2025. (You'd need roughly $187,800+ in 2025 earned income before that 18% figure exceeds the cap.) Belonging to a workplace pension plan reduces your new room via a pension adjustment reported on your T4. Withdrawals are fully taxed as ordinary income in the year you take them out, and the account must convert to a RRIF, annuity, or lump-sum withdrawal by the end of the year you turn 71.

## The actual decision: tax rate now vs. tax rate later

An RRSP deduction is worth the most when your marginal tax rate today is *higher* than the rate you'll pay when you eventually withdraw — the classic case is your peak-earning years, when a deduction saves tax at your highest bracket and you expect a lower-income retirement. A TFSA makes more sense when your current rate is low (early career, a lower-income year, parental leave) or when you might need the money before retirement, since there's no future tax bill waiting and no penalty for pulling it out early.

The gap that catches people off guard: RRSP/RRIF withdrawals count as taxable income, which can also trigger clawback of income-tested benefits like Old Age Security. TFSA withdrawals don't count as income anywhere — they don't affect OAS, GIS, or other benefit calculations at all. That flexibility is worth more the closer you get to retirement.

## Who this is for — and who it isn't

**It's for:** anyone deciding whether to prioritize a TFSA or RRSP, or how to split contributions between them, based on their own income and timeline.

**It's not for:** first-time home buyers trying to work out the Home Buyers' Plan or the First Home Savings Account (FHSA) in detail — both interact with an RRSP but follow their own separate rules, covered only briefly below. If you're earlier in the process of just getting money invested at all, [how to start investing with little money](/blog/how-to-start-investing-with-little-money/) and [index funds explained for beginners](/blog/index-funds-explained-for-beginners/) cover what actually goes inside either account.

## A worked example: same $7,000, two different outcomes

Say someone earns $80,000 and is deciding what to do with $7,000 this year. Their 18% RRSP room ($14,400) comfortably covers it, so room isn't the constraint — the tax treatment is:

- **RRSP path:** the $7,000 contribution is deducted from taxable income. At a illustrative combined marginal rate of ~35% for that income level, that's roughly **$2,450 back as a tax refund or reduced tax owing** this year. That $7,000 then grows tax-deferred, but every dollar withdrawn later — contributions and growth alike — is taxed as income in the year it comes out.
- **TFSA path:** no refund this year, but the $7,000 grows completely tax-free, and every dollar withdrawn later — again, contributions and growth alike — comes out with zero tax, regardless of the person's income at the time.

Neither path is simply "better" — the RRSP path banks a tax saving today in exchange for a future tax bill; the TFSA path skips today's saving in exchange for no future bill at all. Which one wins depends entirely on whether this person's tax rate today is higher or lower than their tax rate whenever they eventually withdraw.

## What to actually do

- **Higher income now than you expect later (peak career, especially with a workplace pension):** prioritize the RRSP for the deduction, then use a TFSA for anything beyond your RRSP room or for money you might need sooner.
- **Lower income now, or you might need the money in the next few years:** prioritize the TFSA — no future tax bill, and no cost to accessing it early. See [how much you should actually have in an emergency fund](/blog/how-much-emergency-fund/) if part of this money is really an emergency buffer rather than long-term savings.
- **Saving for a first home:** the RRSP's Home Buyers' Plan lets a first-time buyer withdraw up to **$60,000 tax-free** (up from $35,000, as of April 2024), repaid over 15 years starting the second year after withdrawal. The newer First Home Savings Account (FHSA) stacks on top of that with its own separate limits and never needs repayment — worth researching specifically if a first home is the goal, since it isn't covered in depth here.
- **Room for both:** most financial planners land on "use both, in whichever order matches your tax situation" rather than picking one permanently — they solve different problems (a tax deduction now vs. tax-free flexibility later), not competing versions of the same thing.

## Common mistakes

- **Recontributing to a TFSA the same year you withdrew.** Withdrawn room isn't restored until January 1 of the *next* year — recontributing in the same calendar year can push you over your limit and trigger a 1%-per-month CRA penalty on the excess.
- **Assuming RRSP withdrawals are tax-free like a TFSA's.** Outside the Home Buyers' Plan and Lifelong Learning Plan (both of which must be repaid to avoid being taxed), every RRSP/RRIF withdrawal is fully taxable income in the year you take it.
- **Not accounting for a pension adjustment.** Workplace pension plan members get their new RRSP room reduced by the pension adjustment on their T4 (box 52) — assuming the full 18% figure applies can lead to an accidental over-contribution.
- **Treating TFSA and RRSP as an either/or choice.** If you have room in both and money to contribute, the real question is which one to prioritize first, not which one to abandon.

## Getting started

The dollar limits above ($7,000 TFSA, $33,810 RRSP for 2026) are the maximums, not necessarily your personal room — RRSP room in particular depends on your own prior-year income and any pension adjustment. Your actual available room for both accounts is on your latest CRA Notice of Assessment and in CRA My Account, and that number is the one to plan around, not the headline figure.
