You've decided to invest in a fund instead of picking individual stocks, and now you're looking at two options that sound almost identical: a mutual fund and an ETF. They both hold a basket of investments, and both can track an index. So which one do you pick, and does it matter?

It can matter, but usually less than people expect. The funds work differently in how you buy and sell them, how they're priced, and how they're taxed in some accounts. This post explains each difference in plain terms, so you can choose with your eyes open.

## Who this is for, and who it isn't

**It's for:** beginners comparing the two fund types, and anyone who's seen "ETF" in an app and wondered how it differs from the mutual funds in their workplace plan.

**It's not for:** anyone looking for a recommendation on a specific fund. This is general education, not personalized investment advice, and investments can lose value. The examples use US rules; account types, taxes, and fund names differ in other countries.

## The short version

- **Both** pool money from many investors into a basket of stocks, bonds, or other assets, run by a professional manager or built to follow an index.
- **A mutual fund** is bought from the fund company (or through a broker) at a price calculated once per business day.
- **An ETF** (exchange-traded fund) trades on a stock exchange, like a stock, at a price that moves throughout the day.
- **The funds inside** can be the same. A "total US stock market" fund exists as both a mutual fund and an ETF at many providers. The wrapper differs, not necessarily the investments.

## What they have in common

The SEC's investor education site describes an ETF as a fund registered with the SEC that pools money from many investors, with an investment adviser managing the portfolio, much like a mutual fund. Each share represents a small ownership stake in everything the fund holds.

Two more similarities are worth knowing, because they clear up a common myth:

- **Neither is automatically "active" or "passive."** Both types can follow an index or try to beat one. Most people think ETFs are always index funds and mutual funds are always actively managed. That isn't true; check what the specific fund does.
- **Both charge an annual fee called an expense ratio**, taken out of the fund's assets, so you never get a bill for it. You see it as slightly lower returns.

For what an index fund is and why many beginners choose one, see [index funds explained for beginners](/blog/index-funds-explained-for-beginners/).

## Where they differ

| | Mutual fund | ETF |
|---|---|---|
| **How you buy** | From the fund company or through a broker | On a stock exchange, through a brokerage account |
| **Price you get** | The fund's net asset value (NAV), calculated at the end of the business day | The market price at the moment you trade, which moves all day |
| **Know the price before you trade?** | No, only after the day's NAV is set | Yes, you see a live price |
| **Trading costs** | Some funds charge fees to buy, sell, or exchange shares | Possible brokerage commission, plus the bid-ask spread |
| **Taxes in a taxable account** | Can pay out capital gains you owe tax on | Typically fewer capital gains payouts |
| **Taxes in an IRA or 401(k)** | No tax difference | No tax difference |
| **Minimum to start** | Some funds set a minimum | Often the price of one share, if your broker doesn't offer part-shares |

The rest of this post unpacks the rows that matter most.

## Pricing: once a day versus all day

A mutual fund calculates its **net asset value (NAV)**, the total value of everything it holds divided by the number of shares, typically at the end of each business day. When you place an order, you get that day's NAV, whatever time you placed it. You won't know the exact price until after the market closes.

An ETF also calculates a NAV each business day, but you buy and sell at the **market price**, which moves throughout the trading day. That price can sit slightly above or below the fund's NAV, called trading at a premium or a discount. For widely traded ETFs, the SEC says the gap generally stays small, but it can vary over time and, in rough markets, can be wider.

**What this means for you:** if you invest for years, the difference between a trade at 10am and one at 3pm is rarely meaningful. The all-day pricing helps people who want to choose the moment of a trade or use order types, and it can tempt others into trading more than they should. Being able to trade every minute is not a reason to do it.

## Trading costs: the spread

When you buy an ETF, you buy at the **ask** price and, if you sell right away, you'd sell at the lower **bid** price. The gap between them is the **spread**. The SEC calls this a hidden cost, since it reduces your return.

Here's a made-up example. An ETF is quoted at $59.95 bid and $60.00 ask. Buy one share and sell it back immediately and you'd lose $0.05, about 0.08%. For a popular fund that's often small, but the SEC notes that ETFs that trade more heavily typically have tighter spreads, and a thinly traded ETF can have a wider one.

Add the other possible cost: a **brokerage commission** on each trade, if your broker charges one. Some brokers do and some don't, so check yours before you assume. A $5 commission on a $200 purchase is 2.5% of what you invested, which would swamp the fund's annual fee. If you plan to invest small amounts every month, commission and spread matter more than they do for one big purchase. The commission-free route, or a mutual fund bought directly with no transaction fee, can be the better fit in that case.

Mutual funds have their own version of this. Some charge fees on purchases, sales, or exchanges (often called loads), and some charge periodic account fees, though many index mutual funds charge none of these. Read the fund's fee table before you buy.

## Taxes: where ETFs often have an edge

This only matters in a **taxable account**. In a tax-advantaged account like a 401(k) or IRA, the SEC says there's no tax difference between the two, so skip this section if that's where you're investing.

In a taxable account, both can pay out **capital gains distributions**, which are taxable to you, even if you haven't sold anything and even if you reinvest them. They happen when the fund sells holdings at a profit inside the fund. The SEC explains that ETFs typically have fewer of these than mutual funds, because many ETFs use in-kind exchanges rather than selling holdings for cash. FINRA adds that, with ETFs, you generally have more control over when you realize gains, since that's decided by when you sell.

"Typically fewer" is not "never." An ETF can still pass on taxable gains, and tax rules differ by country. If taxes on a fund are a big concern, look at the fund's own history of distributions, which providers publish.

## A worked example: costs for a small monthly investor

These numbers are made up to show the logic, not to predict anything.

Say you invest $200 a month, 12 times a year, $2,400 in total.

- **Fund A, a low-cost mutual fund with no purchase fees:** you pay only the expense ratio. At 0.05% a year, that's about $1.20 on $2,400.
- **Fund B, an ETF at the same expense ratio, with a $5 commission per purchase:** commissions alone add up to $60 a year, which is 2.5% of what you put in. Same expense ratio, much higher cost.
- **Fund C, the same ETF at a broker that charges no commission:** your cost is the expense ratio plus a small spread each time, a few cents on a $200 buy.

The lesson isn't "mutual funds win" or "ETFs win." It's that the way you invest, how often, in what amounts, and through which broker, can matter more than the label on the fund. For a bigger lump sum bought once, the commission shrinks to nothing and the comparison flips.

## Why fund costs still matter most

Whichever you choose, the expense ratio compounds against you every year. On $10,000, a 0.05% expense ratio costs about $5 a year, while a 0.75% one costs about $75, and that gap grows with the balance and the years. For more on how small amounts compound, see [compound interest explained simply](/blog/compound-interest-explained-simply/). Compare the expense ratio of any two funds before you compare their wrappers.

## So which fits you?

There's no universal answer, but these situations point in different directions:

- **Investing through a workplace plan (401(k), 403(b)):** you usually choose from the plan's menu, and many plans offer only mutual funds. Pick the lowest-cost option that matches your goals. You usually can't choose ETFs there anyway.
- **Investing small amounts on a regular schedule:** look for automatic investing and low or no transaction costs, whether that's a mutual fund or a commission-free ETF. Check whether your broker lets you buy part of a share if an ETF's price is high relative to your monthly amount.
- **Investing in a taxable account and caring about taxes:** an ETF's typical edge on capital gains payouts is worth weighing.
- **Investing in an IRA or other tax-advantaged account:** tax differences don't apply, so decide on cost, convenience, and the specific fund.
- **Wanting a specific niche (a sector, a region, a theme):** ETFs cover a lot of these, but narrow funds are riskier and often cost more. A niche fund holds fewer things, so a bad stretch hits harder.
- **Not sure:** a broad, low-cost index fund in either form is a reasonable default for many people. See [how to start investing with little money](/blog/how-to-start-investing-with-little-money/).

## Where people actually go wrong

- **Assuming ETFs are always cheaper.** Many are, but commissions, spreads, and the fund's own fee all count.
- **Assuming mutual funds are always actively managed.** Plenty are index funds.
- **Buying a niche or leveraged ETF without reading about it.** Leveraged and inverse ETFs are complex products with their own risks, and the SEC has issued a separate bulletin on them. They are not a simple version of a regular fund, so if you don't understand how one works, don't buy it.
- **Trading more because you can.** Intraday trading doesn't make long-term returns better, and every trade can carry a cost.
- **Using a market order on a thinly traded ETF.** A limit order lets you set the most you'll pay.
- **Overlooking account type.** The tax edge only exists in a taxable account.
- **Picking on the label instead of the details.** Two funds with the same name style can hold very different things. Check what's inside and what it costs.

## Getting started

1. **Decide the account first.** A workplace plan, an IRA, and a regular brokerage account each limit your choices and change the tax picture.
2. **Pick the goal and time frame.** Money you need in a year or two belongs in safer places, not stock funds.
3. **Compare expense ratios and trading costs** for two or three broad funds that cover the same market.
4. **Check how the fund is bought:** minimums, commissions, whether part-shares are available.
