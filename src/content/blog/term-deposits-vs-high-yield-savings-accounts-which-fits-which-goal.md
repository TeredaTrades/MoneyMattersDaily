When you have cash you do not need right away, two options come up again and again: a high-yield savings account, and a term deposit. A term deposit is called a certificate of deposit, or CD, in the United States, and goes by other names in other countries. Both are ordinary bank products, and both usually pay more than a basic current account. The difference is what you give up to get the rate.

This post explains how the two differ, shows the arithmetic with a worked example, and helps you match each to a goal. It quotes no current rates, because they change often and differ by bank and country. It is general education, not a recommendation of any bank or product.

## Who this is for, and who it isn't

**It's for:** anyone with savings sitting in a low-interest account, who is deciding between flexibility and a locked-in rate.

**It isn't for:** people who have not yet built a basic cushion. Before locking money away, make sure you have an [emergency fund](/blog/how-much-emergency-fund/) you can reach at any time. If you want a flexible account, start with [high-yield savings accounts explained](/blog/high-yield-savings-accounts-explained/).

## The core difference: flexible rate or locked rate

According to GOBankingRates (April 2026), a CD holds your money for a fixed term, typically anywhere from three months to five years, in exchange for a fixed rate. A savings account has no end date, lets you add and withdraw money, and usually pays a variable rate.

Vanguard's comparison of high-yield savings, CDs and money market funds (June 2025) lays out the main differences:

| | High-yield savings | CD / term deposit |
|---|---|---|
| Rate | Variable; can change at any time | Fixed for the whole term |
| Access to your money | Withdraw any time, though some banks limit monthly transactions | Locked until the term ends |
| Early withdrawal | Generally no penalty | Bank CDs usually charge a penalty, often a few months' interest |
| Deposit insurance (US) | FDIC or NCUA, up to $250,000 per depositor, per bank or credit union, per ownership category | The same coverage |

The same Vanguard article notes that brokered CDs, sold through a brokerage account, have no early withdrawal penalty, but selling before maturity can mean a gain or a loss.

## What "fixed" and "variable" really mean for you

- **A fixed rate protects you if rates fall.** If you lock in a rate for a year and rates drop after three months, you keep the higher rate until the term ends.
- **A variable rate helps if rates rise.** A savings account's rate can move up, and you can leave at any time if a better offer appears. It also means the rate can fall. GOBankingRates says savings account rates can move with the federal funds rate.

Nobody can say in advance which will turn out better. The choice is about what you need, not a bet on rates.

## A worked example

These numbers are made up and simplified (no compounding, no tax) to show the mechanics.

You have 10,000 to set aside for a year. A one-year CD pays a fixed 4%. A high-yield savings account also pays 4% today, but the rate can change.

- **CD, held to the end:** 10,000 × 4% = **400** in interest, guaranteed.
- **Savings, rates fall after six months to 3%:** 200 for the first half, 150 for the second half = **350**. The CD wins by 50.
- **Savings, rates rise after six months to 5%:** 200 for the first half, 250 for the second half = **450**. The savings account wins by 50.

Now add an early exit. GOBankingRates uses a penalty of 90 days of interest on a one-year CD as an example. At 4% on 10,000, 90 days of interest is about 400 × 90 ÷ 365, or **about 99**. If you need the money after four months, you have earned about 133 in interest and lose about 99 of it, leaving about **34**. With the savings account you would have kept all 133.

The takeaway: the CD only comes out ahead if you leave it alone and rates are flat or falling. If you might need the money, the flexible account is worth its lower certainty.

## Matching each to a goal

| Goal | Better fit | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Emergency fund | High-yield savings | You may need it any day, and a penalty would hurt |
| Money for a known date (tuition, a deposit on a flat) | CD or term deposit sized to that date | A fixed rate and a fixed end date line up |
| Money you might use soon but are not sure | Savings, or a no-penalty CD | Flexibility matters more than the last bit of rate |
| Cash you are waiting to invest | Savings | You may move it at short notice |
| Extra cash you will not touch for a year or more | CD | You gain certainty and give up nothing you would use |

If your goal is a [sinking fund](/blog/how-to-build-a-sinking-fund-for-irregular-expenses/) for a bill in nine months, a nine-month term deposit is a natural match. A flexible account is still better for a fund you dip into regularly.

## Options in between

- **No-penalty CDs** let you withdraw the full amount before maturity without a fee, but GOBankingRates says their rates are typically lower than traditional CDs.
- **CD ladders** split your money across several CDs with staggered end dates, for example six months, one year and two years, so part of your money becomes available regularly while still earning CD rates. As each one matures you can renew it at the then-current rate or move the money elsewhere.
- **Money market accounts** sit between savings and checking and are explained in [money market accounts explained](/blog/money-market-accounts-explained-how-they-differ-from-savings-accounts/). Note that Vanguard distinguishes bank money market deposit accounts from money market funds, which are not FDIC insured.

## The maturity date: a quiet trap

GOBankingRates says CD funds are typically available without a penalty for a short period after maturity, often 7 to 10 days. Many banks automatically renew a CD into a new term if you do nothing. Whether yours does, and at what rate, is in your terms. Put the maturity date in your calendar a few weeks ahead, so you decide on purpose.

## Where people actually go wrong

- **Locking up money they may need.** An early withdrawal penalty can eat most of the interest, as in the example above.
- **Chasing the highest rate without reading the terms.** Check the minimum deposit, the penalty and whether the CD renews automatically.
- **Putting everything in one term.** A ladder or a mix of accounts spreads the timing risk.
- **Ignoring insurance limits.** The US figure of $250,000 applies per depositor, per bank, per ownership category. If you hold more than that at one bank, consider where the rest sits.
- **Forgetting taxes and inflation.** Interest is usually taxable, and a fixed rate lower than inflation loses buying power. Rules differ by country, and this post does not give tax advice.
- **Assuming "term deposit" works the same everywhere.** Terms, penalties and protection schemes differ by country, so check your own bank and regulator.

## Getting started

1. **Write down what the money is for and when you will need it.**
2. **Keep your emergency fund in a flexible account.**
3. **For money with a firm date,** compare a term that ends just before that date with a savings account.
4. **Read the early withdrawal rule** before you commit.
5. **Check deposit insurance** for your country and your balance.
6. **Set a reminder** a few weeks before any maturity date.
