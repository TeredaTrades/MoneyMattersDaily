The holidays cost more than the gifts. Gifts are the part everyone remembers, but travel, hosting, food, decorations, wrapping and postage all land in the same few weeks, and a budget that only counts presents is usually the one that ends in January credit card debt. The fix is not to spend nothing. It is to decide the total before the season starts, divide it up, and pay for it with money you have already set aside.

This guide is general and works in any currency. The examples are invented and the numbers are round.

## Who this is for, and who it isn't

**It's for:** anyone who feels the holidays sneaking up on their budget, or who has paid off a December bill into spring.

**It isn't** a reason to feel guilty about generosity. If you carry a high-interest balance or have no emergency fund, the honest answer may be a smaller budget this year, and that is a valid plan. See [how much you should keep in an emergency fund](/blog/how-much-emergency-fund/) first if that is you.

## Step 1: Set the total from what is left, not from what you want to spend

The US Consumer Financial Protection Bureau's holiday planning advice starts here: cover your regular bills first, estimate the extra costs such as hosting or travel, and treat whatever remains as the gift budget. Start early, save toward it, and be realistic (CFPB, 2017, an archived page but still sound advice).

If you want a rule of thumb for a first number, Kiplinger suggested in November 2025 setting aside roughly 1% to 1.5% of annual income for gifts, and said you can spend less if money is tight. That is one writer's guideline, not a standard. For scale only, Kiplinger also cited a National Retail Federation survey in which Americans planned to spend an average of $890.45 per person on holiday items in 2025, including $627.93 on gifts for family, friends and coworkers. Those are survey averages, not targets, and your situation may be very different.

## Step 2: Count the non-gift costs first

Before you split anything, subtract the known holiday costs: travel, hosting and food, decorations, wrapping and postage, and any events. What is left is what you can spend on gifts. Kiplinger calls this a zero-based method, because every unit of the holiday budget gets a job. If you like the idea, see [zero-based budgeting explained](/blog/zero-based-budgeting-explained/).

## Step 3: Make a list and give each name a cap

The CFPB's advice is to list the gifts you need to buy, check the list before you shop, and cross items off as you go. A personal rule such as "nothing that is not on the list" stops impulse buys. Putting a cap next to each name turns a vague budget into something you can follow in a shop.

## Step 4: Cut costs without cutting meaning

Both sources suggest ways to spend less without losing the point of the gift:

- **Homemade gifts**, such as jar mixes or a picture book of favorite recipes (CFPB).
- **Secret Santa or a group gift cap**, such as agreeing nobody spends more than a set amount per person (Kiplinger).
- **Experience gifts**, such as a shared meal or a day out.
- **Price tracking** for online shopping, so you notice a real discount rather than a fake one.

## Step 5: Decide how you will pay

This is the step that decides whether the budget turns into debt.

- **Cash or an envelope.** Take out only what you planned to spend and stop when it runs out. See [the envelope budgeting method](/blog/envelope-budgeting-method/).
- **A credit card, with a plan.** The CFPB points out that credit cards offer more consumer protections, such as when an item arrives broken. Its advice is to have a specific plan to pay the bill and to set the money aside in your budget first.
- **Gift cards** can be useful, but they have their own fees and scam risks. See [gift cards and your budget](/blog/gift-cards-and-your-budget-fees-rules-and-scams/).

Whichever you choose, the test is the same: could you pay the whole holiday bill from the money you have set aside, without touching your emergency fund?

## Step 6: Track what you spend

Write down every purchase, check regularly whether you are on budget, and note where you spent more or less than expected (CFPB). A simple note on your phone is enough. The notes are also what make next year's budget easy.

## A worked example

This example is invented, with round numbers in no particular currency.

Someone earns 48,000 a year. Using 1.5% as a first guide gives 720, but their regular bills are covered and they have some room, so they choose a total holiday budget of **900**.

| Item | Amount |
|---|---|
| Total holiday budget | 900 |
| Travel to family | -250 |
| Hosting and food | -150 |
| **Left for gifts** | **500** |

They split the 500 across five names: partner 150, two parents 80 each (160), sibling 70, a friend 60 and a coworker or teacher 60. That adds up to 500 exactly.

If they start six weeks before the holiday, saving 900 takes 150 a week. If they start two weeks out, it takes 450 a week, which is why starting early matters.

## Common mistakes

- **Counting only gifts.** Travel, food and extras are usually the bigger surprise.
- **Setting the total from what you want to buy.** Set it from what is left after bills.
- **Paying by credit card with no repayment plan.** The protections are real, but so is interest if you carry a balance.
- **Skipping the list.** Without names and caps, the season turns into a string of small purchases.
- **Forgetting January bills.** Annual fees, insurance and subscriptions often renew right after the holidays. A [sinking fund](/blog/how-to-build-a-sinking-fund-for-irregular-expenses/) spreads those out.

## Getting started

1. **Work out what is left** after regular bills, and decide your total.
2. **Subtract travel, hosting and extras** to find your gift budget.
3. **List names and set a cap for each.**
4. **Pick how you will pay,** and set that money aside now.
5. **Keep a running note of what you spend,** and keep it for next year.
