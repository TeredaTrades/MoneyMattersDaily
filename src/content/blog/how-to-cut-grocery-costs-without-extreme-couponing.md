Rent and loan payments are fixed, but groceries are a line you decide on every time you shop. That makes them one of the easiest places to find savings, and also one of the easiest to overshoot. This guide shows what the US Department of Agriculture (USDA) says households spend on food, then walks through changes that lower the bill without spending hours on coupons.

## Who this is for, and who it isn't

**It's for:** anyone whose grocery bill feels higher than it should be, and anyone setting a food budget for the first time.

**It isn't** medical or dietary advice, and the USDA figures below are US averages for food prepared at home. Prices in your country and city will differ.

## What the USDA says a household spends

The USDA publishes monthly cost-of-food figures for four plans, from Thrifty to Liberal. The latest report at the time of writing is October 2026 (the page was updated on 13 November 2026). The plans assume all meals are prepared at home.

For a reference family of four (two adults and two children), the Thrifty plan is $1,024.60 a month, or $236.50 a week. The page does not list the other plans for the family of four. For individual adults aged 20 to 50, monthly costs are:

| Plan | Female | Male |
|---|---|---|
| Thrifty | $254.20 | $319.10 |
| Low-Cost | $277.00 | $318.50 |
| Moderate-Cost | $337.00 | $398.90 |
| Liberal | $430.20 | $486.80 |

To adjust for household size, add up the individuals and then apply the USDA factor: one person plus 20%, two people plus 10%, three plus 5%, four no change, five or six minus 5%, and seven or more minus 10%. These plans are reference points. They are not rules, and your own food needs, diet and local prices decide your real number.

## A worked example

This uses the USDA figures above for a household of two adults, one female and one male, aged 20 to 50.

- **Thrifty:** ($254.20 + $319.10) × 1.10 = **$630.63** a month.
- **Moderate-Cost:** ($337.00 + $398.90) × 1.10 = **$809.49** a month.
- **Difference:** $178.86 a month, about **$2,146 a year**.

So the gap between a careful shopping approach and a middle-of-the-road one is real money, even before you count a single coupon. If your own bill is above the Moderate figure for your household, the next sections are where to look. If it is below, you may already be spending carefully.

## Where grocery money leaks

- **Food you buy and throw away.** A University of Nebraska-Lincoln extension article (written in 2024) says confusion over date labels is behind an estimated 20 percent of consumer food waste. Wasted food is money you paid for and never used.
- **Shopping without a plan.** Unplanned trips mean impulse buys and duplicates.
- **Buying bulk you can't finish.** The same source notes that bulk works for staples you use regularly, like pasta, but not for short-life perishables.
- **Convenience.** Pre-cut, pre-washed and ready meals cost more per serving than doing it yourself. Whether the time saved is worth it is your call.

## Practical ways to cut the bill

These tips draw on the Nebraska extension guidance above, plus general budgeting practice.

1. **Plan meals around what you already have.** Check the fridge, freezer and pantry before you shop, then build meals around those items, including planned leftovers.
2. **Write a list with quantities.** Amounts on the list help you avoid buying too much of anything.
3. **Buy bulk only for things you will finish** before they spoil or go stale.
4. **Consider "ugly" produce.** Imperfect fruit and vegetables are often cheaper, and the Nebraska article says they are safe to eat if not bruised or damaged and have the same nutrition.
5. **Learn what date labels mean.** Many are about quality, not safety. Check the product's storage guidance, or the USDA FoodKeeper app for storage times.
6. **Keep the fridge at 40°F (4°C) or lower and the freezer at 0°F (-18°C) or lower** so food lasts as long as it should.
7. **Compare unit prices,** the price per kilo, litre or 100 grams, rather than the shelf price. This is general shopping practice, not from a specific source.
8. **Cook once, eat twice.** Plan one or two batch meals a week and use them as leftovers or a second dinner.
9. **Take a fixed amount to the store.** A cash or prepaid limit makes the budget visible. See [envelope budgeting](/blog/envelope-budgeting-method/) for how this works.
10. **Review the receipt once a month** for the three items that cost you most, and ask whether each is worth it.

## How to set a grocery budget

1. **Track a month of food spending,** including takeaway and snacks. See [how to make a budget for beginners](/blog/how-to-make-a-budget-for-beginners/).
2. **Compare your total with the USDA figure** for your household size, as a rough guide rather than a target.
3. **Pick a number** slightly below your current spending and use it for a month.
4. **Adjust.** If you keep overshooting, the budget or the plan needs changing, not your willpower.
5. **Use [the 50/30/20 rule](/blog/50-30-20-budget-rule-explained/)** if you want a simple check on whether food and other essentials take too much of your income.

If prices are rising where you live, [what inflation reports mean for your budget](/blog/what-inflation-reports-mean-for-your-budget/) explains how to tell whether your own costs are rising faster than the headline number, and [how to protect your savings in a high-inflation country](/blog/how-to-protect-your-savings-in-a-high-inflation-country/) covers the savings side.

## Common mistakes

- **Cutting too hard.** A budget so tight you give up in two weeks saves nothing. Aim for a change you can keep.
- **Shopping hungry,** which tends to add unplanned items to the cart. This is general advice, not from the sources above.
- **Chasing a deal on something you wouldn't buy anyway.** A discount on a product you don't need is still a cost.
- **Ignoring waste.** Buying less and wasting less usually beats finding a cheaper price.
- **Treating the USDA plans as a target.** They are averages for US households, not a rule for yours.

## Getting started

1. **Write down what you spent on groceries last month.**
2. **Compare it with the USDA figure for your household size.**
3. **Plan the next week's dinners** from what you already have, then make one list with quantities.
4. **Set a weekly amount** and check it on the same day every week.
5. **Revisit in a month** and decide what to keep.
