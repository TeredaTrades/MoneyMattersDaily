Saving for a home feels huge because the number is huge. The way through is to turn it into a monthly figure you can automate. This guide works through the target, the costs people forget, and a simple plan to get there. It uses US sources, and rules in other countries differ.

## Who this is for, and who it isn't

**It's for:** anyone planning to buy a first home or move up, and anyone who wants to know what "saving for a down payment" actually involves.

**It isn't** mortgage advice. Lenders, programs and rates change, and what you qualify for depends on your income, credit and location. Talk to a lender or housing counselor before deciding.

## What a down payment is, and how much you need

A down payment is the part of the price you pay up front. The rest is the mortgage. The size matters in two ways, according to the US Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB): it affects how much you can afford, and low-down-payment options "can mean higher costs over the life of the loan." The CFPB also notes that if you put down less than 20% of the price, you will likely need mortgage insurance, which adds to your monthly costs.

Common minimums in the US:

- **FHA loans:** as low as 3.5% down with a credit score of 580 or higher, and at least 10% down for scores of 500 to 579, according to NerdWallet's 2026 FHA guide. Individual lenders can ask for more. FHA mortgage insurance is built into every loan, and with under 10% down it lasts the life of the loan.
- **Conventional low-down-payment programs:** Fannie Mae's HomeReady program allows as little as 3% down for eligible borrowers, with homeownership education required for first-time buyers. Eligibility rules apply.
- **VA and USDA loans** can allow zero down for eligible buyers (check the agency's own pages for the current rules).
- **Putting 20% down** generally avoids the mortgage insurance described above.

NerdWallet, citing the National Association of Realtors' 2025 survey, reports that first-time buyers put down about 10% on average. That is a data point, not a target.

## The cost people forget: closing costs

The CFPB says closing costs typically run 2% to 5% of the purchase price, not counting the down payment. They depend on the price, the loan type, your lender and where you live. Plan for them separately, because they are due at closing on top of the down payment.

## A worked example

This is invented, for a $300,000 home. Totals ignore interest earned on savings and any assistance, and the monthly figures divide the total by 60 months (five years).

| Down payment | Down payment cash | Closing costs (2%-5%) | Total cash needed | Monthly saving over 5 years |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 3.5% | $10,500 | $6,000 - $15,000 | $16,500 - $25,500 | $275 - $425 |
| 10% | $30,000 | $6,000 - $15,000 | $36,000 - $45,000 | $600 - $750 |
| 20% | $60,000 | $6,000 - $15,000 | $66,000 - $75,000 | $1,100 - $1,250 |

Two things to notice. First, the closing costs are a big share of the total at the low end. Second, a smaller down payment is not free: the CFPB's point about higher lifetime costs and mortgage insurance applies, so the cheapest way to start is not always the cheapest way to own.

## A step-by-step plan

1. **Set a realistic target price.** Look at what homes you would actually buy. A target price drives every other number.
2. **Pick a down payment percentage** from the table above, based on the loan type you are considering.
3. **Add closing costs.** Use the CFPB's 2% to 5% range until you have a lender's estimate.
4. **Divide by the number of months** until you want to buy. The result is your monthly saving goal.
5. **Open a separate account for the goal.** Keeping it apart from your daily money makes it harder to spend. See [high-yield savings accounts explained](/blog/high-yield-savings-accounts-explained/), [money market accounts explained](/blog/money-market-accounts-explained-how-they-differ-from-savings-accounts/) and [term deposits vs high-yield savings accounts](/blog/term-deposits-vs-high-yield-savings-accounts-which-fits-which-goal/) for the options.
6. **Automate the transfer** for the day after payday. See [best ways to automate your savings](/blog/best-ways-to-automate-your-savings/).
7. **Check for assistance.** The CFPB says you may qualify for down payment assistance through a state, local or nonprofit program. A local housing counselor can tell you what exists near you.
8. **Review every few months.** Prices, rates and your income change, so adjust the target and the monthly amount.

## Keep these separate

- **Your emergency fund.** A down payment is not an emergency fund. Buying a home leaves you with new costs (repairs, taxes, insurance), so keep a cash cushion that is not part of the down payment. See [how much emergency fund do you need](/blog/how-much-emergency-fund/).
- **Your debt payoff.** High-interest debt usually costs more than a savings account earns. See [the financial order of operations](/blog/financial-order-of-operations/) and [how credit card interest works](/blog/how-credit-card-interest-works-apr-grace-periods-and-how-to-avoid-paying-it/).
- **Your credit.** A better score can mean better loan terms, and a mortgage lender will look at it. See [what makes up a credit score](/blog/what-makes-up-a-credit-score-and-what-doesnt/).

## Common mistakes

- **Forgetting closing costs** and saving only the down payment.
- **Draining the emergency fund** to hit the down payment, then having nothing for the first repair.
- **Stretching for the smallest possible down payment** without checking the mortgage insurance cost over the loan's life.
- **Investing the money in something volatile** when the purchase date is close. A short timeline usually calls for stable savings, not stocks or crypto.
- **Not checking assistance programs.** They have eligibility rules, but many buyers never look.

## Getting started

1. **Pick a target price and a down payment percentage.**
2. **Add 2% to 5% for closing costs.**
3. **Divide by your months** to get a monthly number.
4. **Open a separate savings account** and automate that amount.
5. **Book a call with a housing counselor or lender** to check loan types and assistance in your area.
