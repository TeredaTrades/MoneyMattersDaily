Nigeria's Dangote Petroleum Refinery has just closed what is billed as Africa's biggest share sale. The offer ran from September 14 to October 27, 2026, applications came from about 120 countries, and the company called it "The People's IPO" because shares could be bought for as little as about $4.

The shares are not trading yet, and several of the headline numbers still come from unnamed sources rather than the company. This post covers what has been reported as of November 3, 2026, what is still unknown, and, since most readers will never buy into an IPO this size, how an IPO works for ordinary investors in general. It is not a recommendation to buy or avoid anything, and it makes no prediction about the share price.

## What was offered

| Item | Reported detail |
|---|---|
| Shares on offer | About 4.1 billion ordinary shares |
| Fixed price | 525 naira per share (about $0.40 at announcement) |
| Base size | About 2.15 trillion naira (about $1.6 billion) |
| Smallest order | 10 shares, or 5,250 naira (about $4), then in multiples of 10 |
| Share of the company sold | About 3.3% |
| Extra shares (greenshoe) | Up to 30% more if demand allows, subject to regulator approval |
| Where it will list | The Nigerian Exchange (NGX) in Lagos |

The offer opened on the NGX on September 14. The Nigerian SEC approved a two-week extension from October 13 to October 27, which Bloomberg reported was meant to add more countries. Reports put the company's value at the IPO price at roughly $47 to $49 billion, which one outlet said would make it the largest market value in Africa. Treat that as press-reported: the tracker site Daba, which sells access to the offer, notes its own figures for the valuation do not fully reconcile.

## What has been reported about demand

- **Orders.** Reuters, citing two people with knowledge of the sale, reported orders of about 6.4 trillion naira ($4.78 billion) from Nigerian retail and institutional investors. Divided by the 2.15 trillion naira base, that is about three times the offer.
- **People.** The same report said more than 2 million investors had subscribed through digital channels, with more expected once other African countries' orders were counted. Aliko Dangote said all 54 African countries took part.
- **The greenshoe.** One source said Dangote was expected to use the extra-share option, which could lift the deal to about $2.1 billion. A company spokesperson declined to comment. This is not confirmed.
- **A different read.** A Moroccan outlet, Le360, called the result a "relative success" because the stated goal was 10 million African retail investors and about 2 million took part. It also noted that a $2.5 billion private placement to institutions in July may have drawn off some demand.

None of these figures has been published by the company or the regulator yet. Nairametrics, citing Bloomberg's sources, described about $1.5 to $1.6 billion in fully funded commitments from domestic and retail investors, which is a different measure from total orders. Until official numbers come out, treat all of them as indications.

## What happens next, and where sources disagree

After an IPO closes, nothing trades right away. The usual sequence for this offer, as described in the prospectus and press reports:

1. **Basis of allotment.** The company proposes who gets how many shares, and the SEC must approve it. One report says the draft goes to the SEC on November 10, with approval expected around November 17.
2. **Refunds.** Money for shares you did not receive is due back within five business days.
3. **Shares credited.** Shares should reach investors' accounts at the Central Securities Clearing System within 15 business days.
4. **First trading day.** The NGX listing follows. Reuters said "later in November." Counting 15 business days after a mid-November allotment points to early or mid-December. No first trading day has been announced, so both could be wrong.

The other open items are the final amount raised, whether the greenshoe is used, the allotment rules for retail versus institutional investors, and whether any shares are sold by existing holders.

## How an IPO works, in plain terms

An initial public offering is the first time a company sells shares to the public. Whatever the size, the pieces are the same:

- **The prospectus** is the legal document describing the business, the risks, the price, the number of shares and the rules of the sale. It is where retail rules such as minimum orders and refunds live.
- **Fixed price or book building.** Here the price was fixed at 525 naira. In other IPOs, investors bid within a range and the price is set once orders are counted.
- **Oversubscription** means orders exceed the shares available. Everyone cannot get everything they asked for.
- **Basis of allotment** is the rule for cutting back. Small orders are often filled in full and large ones scaled down, but the details differ by deal, and this one has not been published as a final rule in the sources I could open.
- **A greenshoe** lets the company sell extra shares, usually up to 15%, but here up to 30%, if demand is strong.
- **Lock-ups** stop some existing holders from selling for a period. The July private placement carries a 365-day lock-up, according to Daba. I did not find a stated lock-up for shares sold in the IPO itself.
- **Free float** is the share of the company actually available to trade. A small float can mean prices move sharply on small volumes.

## A worked example: what oversubscription does to your order

The numbers below are invented to show the mechanics. The real allotment basis has not been published.

Say you apply for 1,000 shares at 525 naira each and pay 525,000 naira up front. If demand is about three times supply and your order is scaled back to 40%, you would receive 400 shares worth 210,000 naira and get 315,000 naira back within about five business days.

Two points follow. First, a big headline order is not the same as a big allocation. Second, your money can be tied up for weeks even when you get far fewer shares than you wanted. If you borrowed to apply, the interest keeps running while the refund is pending.

## Risks worth knowing about

These come from the prospectus-based summaries and press coverage, not from a view on whether the shares are cheap or expensive.

- **Thin float.** Only about 3.3% of the company is being sold, which the sources say can make trading volatile after listing.
- **Margins.** The company's strong first-half 2026 profit was helped by refining margins that have since eased, according to a Cowrywise summary. Le360 reported net profit of $4.73 billion for the first nine months of 2026. The same Cowrywise summary says the company lost money in 2024 and 2025 and only recently turned profitable. Check the prospectus for the actual figures.
- **Currency.** The summary says about 56% of revenue is in naira, while roughly 70% of feedstock costs are in foreign currency. A weaker naira can hurt costs, and also changes what shares are worth to someone holding dollars or birr or shillings.
- **Execution.** Expansion plans, including a target of 1.4 million barrels a day by 2029 that needs further funding, may not go to schedule.
- **Policy.** Fuel pricing, crude supply and the 15% import duty on refined products are set by government decisions that can change.
- **Dividends.** The prospectus says dividends are intended to be declared in US dollars, subject to approvals. None has been declared. A proposed bonus-share scheme for holders who keep their shares for 12 months also still needs approvals.

## If you are outside Nigeria

The offer is closed, so the question now is about buying after listing. Reporting on the IPO shows how fragmented cross-border access still is:

- Investors outside Nigeria generally needed a broker or platform connected to the Nigerian market. Daba, for example, described a route through a Nigerian licensed broker, with eligibility depending on the investor's country of residence.
- Le360 reported that some regions have rules that require approval to invest abroad or limit how money and dividends move, and that a few countries, including Kenya, Ethiopia and Uganda, set up depositary receipts in time as a workaround.
- Naira exposure and the cost of converting money are part of the price you pay. Currency fees can quietly eat returns, which is why our guides on [multi-currency wallets](/blog/best-multi-currency-wallets-compared-one-account-for-several-currencies/) and [dynamic currency conversion](/blog/dynamic-currency-conversion-why-to-decline-it-and-how-to-avoid-it/) are worth a look if you move money across borders.

Rules on investing abroad, tax and reporting differ by country, so check your own before sending money anywhere.

## What this means if you are an ordinary investor

- **A hot IPO is not a guaranteed win.** Oversubscribed offers can still trade below their price, and weak ones can jump. Nobody can promise which.
- **One company is one bet.** If you want exposure to energy or African markets without picking a single stock, compare it with a fund. Our post on [ETFs vs. mutual funds](/blog/etfs-vs-mutual-funds-whats-the-difference-and-which-fits-you/) explains the trade-off.
- **Know what is in your index.** A very large listing can change what broad market indices hold over time. See [stock market indices explained](/blog/stock-market-indices-explained-sp-500-dow-jones-and-nasdaq/).
- **Only commit money you can leave alone.** Between payment, allotment and the first trading day, cash can be locked up for weeks.
- **Read the prospectus, not the headline.** Minimums, refunds, lock-ups, dividends and risks are all there.
