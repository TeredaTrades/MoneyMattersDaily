If you own crypto, there is a question that matters almost as much as what you bought: who holds the keys? Where your crypto is stored decides who can lose it, who can freeze it, and what happens if something goes wrong. Recent exchange hacks, including the one covered in [The Binance Hack Explained](/blog/the-binance-hack-explained-what-happened-and-what-it-means-for-you/), have made that question hard to ignore.

This post explains the basics in plain language: what a wallet really is, how keeping crypto on an exchange differs from holding it yourself, and which scams follow big headlines. It is not a recommendation to buy crypto, to hold it, or to use any particular product. Crypto can lose most or all of its value, and nobody can promise you a price.

## Who this is for, and who it isn't

**It's for:** anyone who already holds crypto, or is thinking about it, and wants to understand the storage trade-offs before moving money.

**It isn't for:** people looking for a "safest wallet" pick. This post names no products on purpose. Which option fits depends on how much you hold, how technical you are, and how much responsibility you want. If you are new to the basics, start with [what is cryptocurrency](/blog/what-is-cryptocurrency-how-it-works-and-the-risks/).

## What a wallet actually is

The word "wallet" is misleading. The US Securities and Exchange Commission's investor education office, in a bulletin on crypto custody published December 12, 2025, puts it directly: crypto wallets "do not store crypto assets themselves." What a wallet stores is keys.

- **A private key** works like a password. It authorizes transactions. The SEC notes that once created, "a private key cannot be changed or replaced," and losing it can mean losing access to everything the wallet controls.
- **A public key** works like an email address for your wallet. People can use it to send you crypto, but it cannot authorize anything.
- **A seed phrase** is a list of words that can restore a wallet if the device is lost or damaged. Whoever has the seed phrase can usually take everything, so it has to be kept private.

Whoever controls the private key controls the crypto. Every storage choice below is really a choice about who holds that key.

## Option 1: leave it on an exchange (third-party custody)

When you keep crypto in an exchange account, the exchange typically holds the keys for you. This is called third-party custody.

**What you get:** convenience. You log in with a password, you can usually recover the account if you forget it, and trading is one click away.

**What you give up:** direct control. The SEC bulletin says it plainly: "If the third-party custodian is hacked, shuts down, or goes bankrupt, you may lose access to your crypto assets."

A few specific risks the bulletin names:

- **Hacks.** The exchange's own systems can be breached, as large incidents have shown.
- **Insolvency.** If the company fails, you may be an unsecured creditor waiting in line rather than an owner pulling assets out.
- **Commingling.** Some custodians pool customer assets instead of holding them separately.
- **Rehypothecation.** Some custodians use deposited assets as collateral or lend them out, so check whether they do and whether they need your consent.

One more point that surprises people: balances held on a crypto exchange are generally **not covered by bank-style deposit insurance** in the way a bank account is. Check the exchange's own terms and your local regulator's page for what protection, if any, applies where you live.

## Option 2: hold it yourself (self-custody)

In self-custody, you hold the private keys, usually in a wallet app or a dedicated device.

**What you get:** control. No exchange can freeze, lend out or lose your crypto because it failed.

**What you take on:** all of the responsibility. The SEC bulletin warns: "If your crypto wallets are lost, stolen, damaged, or hacked, you may permanently lose access to your crypto assets." There is no help desk that can reset a lost seed phrase.

Self-custody wallets come in two broad types:

- **Hot wallets** are connected to the internet (a phone app, desktop app or browser wallet). They are convenient, but the SEC says they "expose your crypto assets to cyberthreats."
- **Cold wallets** are kept offline, for example on a hardware device or a paper backup. They are generally harder to attack remotely, but they can be lost, damaged or stolen, and they are less convenient to use.

## The trade-offs side by side

| | Exchange (third party holds keys) | Self-custody hot wallet | Self-custody cold wallet |
|---|---|---|---|
| Who holds the keys | The exchange | You | You |
| Convenience | High | High | Lower |
| Main risks | Hack, failure, commingling, lending | Malware, phishing, lost phone | Lost or damaged device, lost seed phrase |
| If you forget your login | Often recoverable | Only with your seed phrase | Only with your seed phrase |
| Protection if it fails | Depends on the firm and local law; do not assume | None | None |

There is no option with zero risk. The point is to choose which risks you would rather carry.

## A simple worked example

The amounts below are made up. They only show the logic of spreading risk.

Suppose you hold the equivalent of 1,000 in crypto. You use about 100 for occasional trading and payments, and you can afford to lose it. You keep the other 900 as a long-term holding you rarely touch.

- **Trading amount (100):** keep it somewhere convenient, such as an exchange account or hot wallet, knowing it carries hack and failure risk.
- **Long-term amount (900):** you decide whether to hold it yourself in a cold wallet, which removes exchange risk but makes you responsible for the seed phrase.

Many people split holdings this way, but it is a way of thinking, not advice. If the long-term amount is large and you are not comfortable guarding a seed phrase, that is a reason to think harder about whether you should hold crypto at all. The general rule behind all of this is the one in [what is cryptocurrency](/blog/what-is-cryptocurrency-how-it-works-and-the-risks/): only hold what you could afford to lose.

## Questions to ask any exchange or custodian

The SEC bulletin suggests asking these before you trust a third party with your keys:

1. **Who is it and who regulates it?** Look at its background and any complaints.
2. **What happens if it fails?** Ask whether there is insurance and what it actually covers.
3. **How does it store keys?** Hot or cold wallets, and whether it uses subcontractors.
4. **Does it lend or pool your assets?** And does it need your consent?
5. **What are the fees?** Trading, withdrawal, transfer and account closure fees can differ widely.
6. **Does it sell your data?**

If you cannot find clear answers, treat that as an answer.

## Scams that follow the headlines

After a large hack or price swing, scammers move fast. These patterns come up again and again:

- **Fake "recovery" or "compensation" offers.** A message says you can claim funds back from a hack if you connect your wallet or share your seed phrase. Real exchanges and wallets do not ask for your seed phrase.
- **Seed phrase and "support" scams.** Someone posing as support asks you to "verify" your wallet. No legitimate support needs your private key or seed phrase.
- **Impersonators and fake giveaways.** The US Federal Trade Commission has warned about scams promising that a celebrity will multiply any crypto you send. In a 2021 release it reported that consumers had lost more than $80 million to crypto investment scams since October 2020, with a median reported loss of $1,900. That data is old, but the patterns have not gone away.
- **Fake investment sites.** These often begin with "tips" or "secrets" in online forums and lead to sites that look real but only take your money.
- **Phishing.** Fake emails, texts and ads that copy an exchange or wallet and lead to a lookalike login page.

A general rule from the FTC's guidance: it will never demand money, make threats, tell you to transfer money or promise you a prize. The same goes for any real exchange.

## Where people actually go wrong

- **Photographing or emailing a seed phrase.** Anything stored online can be stolen along with the account it sits in.
- **Keeping one copy of the seed phrase.** A fire, a flood or a lost note can end access for good.
- **Keeping everything on one platform.** A single hack or failure then takes it all.
- **Rushing a transfer.** Crypto transfers generally cannot be reversed. Sending to a wrong address usually means the funds are gone.
- **Trusting a stranger who offers help.** Unsolicited "help" after a loss is usually the second scam.
- **Skipping two-step login.** The SEC bulletin recommends strong passwords and multi-factor authentication.
- **Treating storage as a one-time decision.** Review where your crypto sits whenever the amount, the platform or the news changes.

## A basic safety checklist

1. **Decide how much you could afford to lose,** and hold no more than that.
2. **Choose a storage method on purpose,** knowing who holds the keys.
3. **Turn on multi-factor authentication** for every exchange account.
4. **Write the seed phrase on paper,** keep more than one copy in separate safe places, and never store it online.
5. **Send a small test transfer first** whenever you move crypto to a new address.
6. **Never share a private key or seed phrase,** with anyone, for any reason.
7. **Treat any unsolicited message about your crypto as suspect,** especially after big news.
8. **Read how to spot hype and fraud** in [how to read crypto news without getting burned](/blog/how-to-read-crypto-news-without-getting-burned/).
