Superannuation ("super") is Australia's compulsory retirement savings system. Your employer is legally required to pay a percentage of your wages into a super fund on top of your salary — not deducted from it — where it's invested and locked away until you reach a set age. It's not optional, and it's not the same as a bank account: you can't withdraw it early just because you want to.

## The mechanic: employer contributions, not payroll deductions

The Superannuation Guarantee (SG) is the rate employers must pay. It's currently **12% of your ordinary time earnings (OTE)** — your normal wages, not overtime or most bonuses — and has been since 1 July 2025, after nearly a decade of scheduled 0.5% annual increases. There's no further legislated increase on the books; 12% is where it stays.

**Worked example:** someone earning $90,000 a year in OTE gets $90,000 in their bank account across the year, plus a separate **$10,800** ($90,000 × 12%) paid by their employer straight into their super fund. That $10,800 never touches their payslip as take-home pay — it's on top of the $90,000, not a slice of it. Over a 30-year career at a flat $90,000 (ignoring raises, job changes, and investment growth entirely, just to isolate the mechanic), that's $324,000 in employer contributions alone before a single dollar of investment return is added.

Most employees don't need to do anything to receive this — it's automatic once you're over 18 and earning wages, with narrow exceptions (some visa holders, contractors paid mainly for labour, and a few other edge cases the ATO lists explicitly).

## Where the money actually goes

Contributions land in a super fund, not a savings account. Funds invest the money — typically in a mix of Australian and international shares, property, bonds, and cash — and the balance grows or shrinks with those investment returns, minus fees. Most people are defaulted into their fund's "MySuper" product, a low-cost, diversified option designed for someone who never actively chooses an investment mix. You can usually switch to a different investment option (more growth-focused, more conservative, or fully self-directed) inside the same fund, or move to a different fund entirely — but the contributions keep coming from your employer regardless of which fund holds them.

## The trade-off: you can't touch it until "preservation age"

This is the part that trips people up coming from a country without compulsory super: the money is genuinely locked away. You generally can't access it until you reach your **preservation age — 60 for anyone born after 30 June 1964** — and even then, only once you've also met a "condition of release," most commonly retiring. There's no early-withdrawal-with-penalty option for a house deposit or a medical bill the way a 401(k) hardship withdrawal works in the US; the exceptions that do exist (severe financial hardship, compassionate grounds, terminal illness) are narrow and specifically defined, not a general early-access door.

That illiquidity is the actual price of the tax treatment super gets: employer contributions are taxed at 15% going in, rather than at your marginal income tax rate, and investment earnings inside the fund are also taxed concessionally. The system is built around the assumption that this money is genuinely untouchable until retirement — which is what makes the concessional tax rate worth it as a policy trade, not a loophole.

## Putting in more than the minimum

Beyond the compulsory 12%, two more contribution types exist:

- **Concessional (before-tax) contributions** — salary sacrifice or personal contributions you claim a tax deduction for. These are capped at **$32,500 for the 2026–27 financial year** (up from $30,000 the year before), and the 12% SG your employer already pays counts toward that cap, not on top of it.
- **Non-concessional (after-tax) contributions** — money you put in that's already been taxed as income. Capped at **$130,000 for 2026–27**, with a "bring-forward" rule letting eligible people contribute up to three years' worth (currently $390,000) in a single year if they want to move a lump sum — an inheritance or the proceeds of selling an asset — into super at once.

Salary sacrificing into super only makes sense once the calculation actually favours it: contributing at the 15% concessional tax rate beats contributing from take-home pay taxed at your marginal rate, but only for the money you can genuinely afford to lock away until 60.

## Who this is for — and who it isn't

This matters for **anyone earning wages in Australia**, whether or not they think of themselves as "an investor" — the contributions happen automatically, and the fund and investment option chosen (or left as the default) meaningfully affects the final balance over a multi-decade career. It especially matters for anyone who's changed jobs several times, since duplicate accounts across old employers are one of the most common ways people quietly lose money to multiple sets of fees (more on that below).

It's less immediately relevant for someone not currently working in Australia, or for a short-term visitor on a visa where SG doesn't apply — though even short-term working visa holders who did accumulate super may be able to claim a **Departing Australia Superannuation Payment (DASP)** after they leave.

## Common mistakes people make with super

- **Losing track of old accounts.** Every job change can create a new super account unless you actively tell a new employer to pay into your existing fund. Multiple accounts mean multiple sets of fees and, often, multiple insurance premiums being deducted from balances you've forgotten about. The ATO's online services (via myGov) can find and consolidate lost or unclaimed super in a few minutes.
- **Ignoring fees because the money feels abstract.** A fund charging 1.5% in fees versus one charging 0.5% doesn't feel urgent when the balance is $8,000 — it's a much bigger deal at $400,000, decades later, because fees compound against the balance the same way returns compound for it.
- **Never checking or choosing an investment option.** Staying in a default MySuper option isn't a mistake by itself — it's a reasonable, deliberately-designed default. The mistake is not knowing that's what's happening, and not revisiting it as circumstances change (a more growth-oriented option generally suits someone decades from preservation age; a more conservative one usually suits someone close to it).
- **Treating the 12% SG as the only lever.** Someone behind on retirement savings, or who took years out of the workforce, has concessional and non-concessional contribution room sitting unused every year that simply expires — it isn't something that can be "made up" retroactively beyond a limited five-year carry-forward rule for unused concessional caps.
