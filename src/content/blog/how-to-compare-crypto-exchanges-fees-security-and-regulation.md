A crypto exchange is a platform where you can buy, sell and often hold digital assets. It is not a bank, and a balance on an exchange is not the same as money in an insured account. Exchanges differ a lot in how they are regulated, how they hold your assets, what they really charge and what happens if something goes wrong. This guide gives you a way to compare them on criteria, because naming a "best" one would not hold up: availability, licensing and fees differ by country and change often. It does not recommend any exchange or any coin, and it makes no price predictions.

Crypto assets can lose most or all of their value, and platforms have been hacked, have frozen withdrawals and have failed. If you want to understand the asset first, start with [what is cryptocurrency](/blog/what-is-cryptocurrency-how-it-works-and-the-risks/).

## Who this is for, and who it isn't

**It's for:** someone who has already decided, for their own reasons, to buy or hold some crypto and wants to pick a platform carefully, or to check one they already use.

**It isn't** an invitation to buy crypto, and it is not a ranking. If you cannot afford to lose the money, the safest choice is not to put it there.

## The risks come first

FINRA's guidance on crypto trading platforms, written with the BBB Institute (April 2019, so older), makes points that still frame the problem:

- These platforms are often called "exchanges," but in the US they typically are not registered as national securities exchanges and may face little or no regulatory oversight. Platforms registered abroad may face limited oversight too.
- **FDIC insurance does not cover digital assets held by a platform,** and SIPC does not protect crypto losses. Dollar deposits at an FDIC member bank may be insured, but that is different from the crypto.
- Platforms have been hacked. FINRA cites Mt. Gox, which lost 750,000 bitcoins in a 2014 attack. More recent events, such as the one covered in [the Binance hack explained](/blog/the-binance-hack-explained-what-happened-and-what-it-means-for-you/), show the risk has not gone away.
- A platform failure or technical problem could mean losing your entire investment, and newer platforms may not handle high volumes, so you might not be able to withdraw or sell when you want to.
- Fake platforms and impersonators target investors.

## The criteria to compare

Use the same checklist on every platform you consider.

**1. Licensing and oversight.** Is the company licensed or registered in your country, and can you find that on the regulator's own site? A claim on the exchange's page is not proof. Note which legal entity you would actually contract with, as large exchanges often run separate local entities.

**2. Custody and keys.** FINRA says to find out what safeguards protect your assets and whether the platform holds your private keys. If the platform holds them, you rely on its security and honesty. Ask whether customer assets are kept separate from company funds and whether the company publishes proof of reserves, and treat a proof of reserves as a signal, not a guarantee.

**3. The true cost of a trade.** The fee shown is rarely the whole cost. Compare:

- the **trading fee,** which may differ between "simple buy" screens and advanced order books,
- the **spread,** the gap between the buy and sell price, which can be larger than the fee,
- **deposit and withdrawal fees,** including network fees when you send crypto out,
- **currency conversion** if you fund in a different currency,
- and any **inactivity or other account fees.**

FINRA notes that buy and sell prices and exchange rates can differ by platform, so prices are worth comparing at the same moment.

**4. Security features.** Two-factor authentication using an app or hardware key (not SMS if you have the choice), withdrawal allow-lists, login alerts and a clear record of past incidents and how the company handled them.

**5. Insurance and protection.** Ask exactly what is covered, by whom and for what events. Any private insurance has limits and exclusions. "Insured" in marketing can mean very little.

**6. Withdrawals and access.** Can you move your assets out to your own wallet, how long does it take, and are there limits? A platform that is easy to deposit into but hard to withdraw from is a warning sign. See [how to store crypto safely](/blog/how-to-store-crypto-safely-wallets-exchanges-and-common-scams/).

**7. Supported assets, currencies and payment methods.** The right platform for someone paying by local bank transfer or mobile money may not be the right one for someone using a card. Check which currencies and local payment methods your country supports, and whether stablecoins and local fiat are both available. See [bitcoin vs. ethereum vs. stablecoins](/blog/bitcoin-vs-ethereum-vs-stablecoins-whats-the-difference/).

**8. Support and complaints.** Can you reach a human, and is there a regulator or ombudsman to complain to if things go wrong?

**9. Taxes and records.** Many countries tax crypto gains, and some require reports from platforms. Can you export a transaction history?

## Kinds of platform you will meet

- **Large centralized exchanges.** More assets and liquidity, and more targets for attackers. You trust the company.
- **Regional or local exchanges.** Often better for local currency and payment methods, with varying standards of oversight.
- **Broker-style apps.** Simple buying inside an app you may already use, often with higher spreads.
- **Decentralized exchanges.** You keep your own keys and connect a wallet, which removes some company risk and adds others: you carry the security burden, there is no support desk and scams are common.

None of these is automatically safer. They simply trade one risk for another.

## A worked example

This uses invented numbers. Someone buys 1,000 of crypto and compares two platforms:

| | Trading fee | Spread (hidden in the price) | Cost of the buy |
|---|---|---|---|
| Platform A | 0.1% | 0.9% | 1,000 × 0.001 + 1,000 × 0.009 = **10** |
| Platform B | 0.5% | 0.1% | 1,000 × 0.005 + 1,000 × 0.001 = **6** |

Platform A looks cheaper by its headline fee, 0.1% against 0.5%, but costs more once the spread is included, 10 against 6. If they also pay a flat 5 to withdraw to their own wallet on one of them, the ranking can change again. The lesson is to add up **every** cost for the way you will actually use the platform.

## Common mistakes

- **Choosing by sign-up bonuses or advertising.**
- **Leaving everything on the exchange.** A balance on a platform is a claim on the company.
- **Trusting "insured" or "secure" without details.**
- **Comparing only the headline fee.**
- **Using SMS-based two-factor authentication** where something stronger is available.
- **Clicking links in messages or ads** to log in. Type the address yourself or use a saved bookmark.
- **Starting with a large amount.** FINRA suggests starting with a small trade to test the cost, how long it takes, whether you can withdraw easily and whether you can reach someone.
- **Not checking local rules and taxes.**

## Getting started

1. **Decide first whether crypto belongs in your plan at all,** and set an amount you could lose.
2. **List two or three platforms available in your country** and verify each one's licensing on the regulator's site.
3. **Compare the full cost of one buy and one withdrawal** on each, using their own fee pages on the day.
4. **Turn on the strongest two-factor authentication** and withdrawal protections.
5. **Test with a small amount,** including a withdrawal, before putting in more.
6. **Move what you will not trade** to storage you control, and keep records for tax.
