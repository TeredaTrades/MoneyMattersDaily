At the till, a credit card, a debit card and a prepaid card all do the same thing: you tap or insert, and the payment goes through. The difference is **whose money moves** and **what happens when something goes wrong**. That decides how much a fraud problem could cost you, whether you pay interest, and which card suits which job. This guide explains the three, compares them side by side, and ends with a simple way to choose.

The rules quoted below are US rules, from US government and state pages. In other countries the protections differ, sometimes a lot, so check your own card issuer's terms and your national consumer agency.

## Who this is for, and who it isn't

**It's for:** anyone who uses more than one type of card and wants to know which to use for online shopping, travel, a deposit or a budget.

**It isn't** a case for any one card. Each of the three is the right tool in some situations.

## The core difference: where the money comes from

- **Credit card:** you spend the issuer's money and repay it later. The Wisconsin Department of Financial Institutions puts it plainly: "a credit card is a loan." If you do not pay the full balance by the due date, interest may apply.
- **Debit card:** the purchase is taken directly from your linked bank account. You are not borrowing, so there is no interest. The US Federal Trade Commission (FTC) warns that overdraft protection can still bring fees and interest.
- **Prepaid card:** you can generally spend only what you have loaded onto it. There is no loan and no direct link to a bank account, though some cards can be topped up from one.

## What happens if the card is stolen or used without permission

This is where the cards differ most. The figures below come from the Wisconsin DFI page and the FTC.

| | Credit | Debit | Prepaid |
|---|---|---|---|
| Whose money is at risk first | The issuer's, while it is investigated | Yours, straight from your account | The money loaded on the card |
| Liability limit for unauthorized use (US) | Capped at $50 | $50 if reported within two business days; after that, losses can reach $500 or more | Depends on the card type |
| If you wait too long | Cap still applies | The FTC warns you could lose all the money in your account and more | Check the issuer's terms |

The practical point is cash flow. With a debit card, the stolen money has already left your account while the bank investigates, so rent or bills could bounce. With a credit card, it is the issuer's money in dispute while your own account stays untouched.

**Prepaid cards are the least uniform.** The Wisconsin page says payroll cards follow debit-card rules, while general-purpose reloadable and gift cards had no federal liability limit at the time it was written. It shows no date. A US federal rule on prepaid accounts has since extended debit-style protections to many registered prepaid accounts, so older advice can be out of date. Check the current terms for your card and your country, and ask the issuer whether the card is registered and what protections apply before you load a large amount.

## Fees and rules to check

- **Credit:** issuers must disclose the APR and fees. In the US, rates and fees generally cannot go up in the first year, with some exceptions, and issuers must give 45 days' notice of significant changes (Wisconsin DFI).
- **Debit:** banks must disclose fees and give 21 days' notice of fee changes (Wisconsin DFI). Overdraft and ATM fees are the ones to watch.
- **Prepaid:** fees vary widely by card and what the card is. Look for monthly, reload, ATM and inactivity fees. Gift cards must disclose any expiry date and dormancy fees. See [best prepaid cards compared](/blog/best-prepaid-cards-compared-fees-limits-and-who-they-suit/) and [gift cards and your budget](/blog/gift-cards-and-your-budget-fees-rules-and-scams/).

## Which one for which job

- **Online shopping and big purchases:** credit is usually the safest, because the issuer's money is in dispute if something goes wrong. Pay in full each month so the interest never applies.
- **Everyday spending you want to keep to your cash:** debit keeps you inside what you actually have. Turn on alerts, and keep a buffer so a fraud hold cannot bounce your bills.
- **Travel:** carry more than one. A credit card for most spending, a debit card for cash, and a prepaid or backup card kept separately. See [prepaid travel cards vs travel debit cards](/blog/prepaid-travel-cards-vs-travel-debit-cards-which-to-carry-abroad/) and [travel credit cards explained](/blog/travel-credit-cards-explained-points-miles-and-foreign-transaction-fees/).
- **Hard budgeting:** a prepaid card with a fixed load can work as a spending cap, if its fees are low.
- **Building credit:** only a credit-type card reports a borrowing history. A [secured card](/blog/secured-credit-cards-explained-how-they-work-and-when-to-graduate/) is the usual starting point, and [how credit scores are made up](/blog/what-makes-up-a-credit-score-and-what-doesnt/) explains what to do with it. Debit and ordinary prepaid cards generally do not build credit.
- **Deposits and holds** (hotels, rental cars): a credit card is usually kinder. A debit hold ties up real money in your account until it is released.

## A worked example

This uses invented numbers in no particular currency. Someone finds 400 in charges on a card that they did not make.

- **Credit card:** the loss they can be held to is capped at 50 (FTC, Wisconsin DFI), and the other 350 is the issuer's to investigate. Their own bank balance does not change.
- **Debit card, reported within two business days:** the cap is 50, but the whole 400 left their account first, and they may wait for the bank to restore it. If rent is due that week, that gap is the real cost.
- **Debit card, reported much later:** the Wisconsin page says losses can reach 500 or more, and the FTC says you could lose all the money in the account and more.
- **Prepaid card:** the most they can lose is the money loaded on the card, and the protections depend on the card.

The same event, a very different week.

## Common mistakes

- **Using a debit card for risky or unfamiliar online purchases.** A problem hits your own cash first.
- **Assuming a prepaid card has the same protection as a credit card.** It may not. Check before you load a large amount.
- **Carrying a credit balance and paying only the minimum.** Interest can erase the protection benefit.
- **Letting prepaid cards sit.** Inactivity fees and expiry rules can quietly take the balance.
- **Using one card for everything while travelling.** If it is lost, blocked or compromised, you have no backup.
- **Not reading the issuer's current terms.** Protections and fees are the issuer's and your country's, not a universal rule.

## Getting started

1. **Look at the cards you carry** and write down the type of each.
2. **Check your issuer's terms** for fraud liability and how quickly you must report.
3. **Set alerts** on every card, so you see a charge you do not recognise within a day.
4. **Pay credit cards in full** if you can, and use them for online and larger payments.
5. **Keep a second card** in a different place from your main one, especially when travelling.
