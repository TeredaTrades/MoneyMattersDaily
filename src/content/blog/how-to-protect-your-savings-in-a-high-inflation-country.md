When prices rise 20% a year, money sitting in a drawer or an account paying 5% is losing buying power every month. In some countries the local currency also falls against the dollar and the euro, so imported goods, rent tied to a foreign currency and school fees get more expensive even faster. People in this situation have a real problem, and there is a lot of confident advice online that does not mention the risks.

This guide lays out the main options, what each one costs and risks, and how to compare them. It does not name investments to buy, and it cannot tell you what is legal where you live. Rules about foreign currency and crypto vary and change, so check them locally first.

## Who this is for, and who it isn't

**It's for:** people living with fast-rising prices or a weakening currency who want to keep what they have saved from shrinking.

**It isn't** a promise that any option "beats inflation." Every option below has a way to lose money, and the right mix depends on your country, your rules and how soon you need the money.

## Start with the real rate

Whatever you do, compare the **real** return, meaning the interest you earn minus how fast prices are rising. Monevator, a UK investing site, makes the same point about debt: what matters is the real interest rate, and rates can rise as authorities fight inflation.

Here is a made-up example. You put 1,000 in a deposit paying 12% a year, while prices rise 20%.

- After a year you have 1,000 × 1.12 = **1,120**.
- To keep your buying power, you would need 1,000 × 1.20 = 1,200.
- In today's money, your 1,120 buys about 1,120 ÷ 1.20 = **933** worth of goods, a loss of about 6.7%.

You earned interest, and you still lost buying power. If the deposit paid 25%, you would come out ahead. So the first question for any option is: what is the real rate after inflation, and after fees and tax?

## The main options, with their trade-offs

**1. Interest-paying accounts and deposits.** Check whether any local account pays at or above the inflation rate, and read the withdrawal terms. See [high-yield savings accounts explained](/blog/high-yield-savings-accounts-explained/) and [term deposits vs. high-yield savings](/blog/term-deposits-vs-high-yield-savings-accounts-which-fits-which-goal/) for how to compare. Risk: rates can lag inflation, and locking money in can hurt if prices jump.

**2. Inflation-linked bonds, where your country sells them.** Monevator notes that index-linked bonds adjust for inflation, but their traded prices fluctuate, so you can lose money unless you hold to maturity. Corporate versions carry default risk. Many countries have no such product.

**3. Foreign-currency savings.** People often move savings into a more stable currency such as the US dollar. Providers describing this approach note that governments may limit it. GetPlu, a payments company, says Argentina's central bank caps dollar purchases at $200 a month, Pakistan restricts foreign exchange access, and banks in Egypt cannot provide dollar accounts. Those are one company's claims that I could not independently confirm, and rules change. Check what is legal and what your bank allows.

**4. Stablecoins.** A stablecoin is a crypto token designed to track a currency, almost always the US dollar. The IMF's Dan Katz said in August 2026 that nearly 99% of stablecoins are dollar-denominated and that the market is about $300 billion in size. He said reserves are mostly short-term US Treasury bills and repurchase agreements, but some issuers use less liquid, riskier assets. GetPlu adds that stablecoins are not government-insured like bank deposits, and advises using reputable issuers, diversifying and keeping balances small. Katz also warned that stablecoins can amplify dollarization and help people get around capital controls, and said regulators need to supervise exchanges and the on- and off-ramps. Practical risks include a token losing its peg, an exchange failing, a scam, and difficulty turning it back into local money. See [mobile money in Africa](/blog/mobile-money-in-africa-m-pesa-and-digital-banking-basics-explained/), [bitcoin vs. ethereum vs. stablecoins](/blog/bitcoin-vs-ethereum-vs-stablecoins-whats-the-difference/) and [what is cryptocurrency](/blog/what-is-cryptocurrency-how-it-works-and-the-risks/). This blog does not recommend any token or platform.

**5. Real assets such as gold or property.** Monevator says property and other real assets can pass on rising costs but are illiquid and hard to price, and that gold can rise in inflation but has had long stretches of poor returns, so timing matters. See [how to invest in commodities](/blog/how-to-invest-in-commodities-the-main-ways-to-get-exposure-and-the-risks/) for the general picture.

**6. Shares in a diversified fund, where accessible.** Monevator calls shares the best long-term defence in its view, but notes they are volatile and may lag inflation for years. That is one writer's opinion, and it depends on what your market offers.

**7. Spending timing and debt.** Monevator notes that inflation erodes the real value of fixed debt, but only if the interest rate stays below inflation. Floating-rate loans can get more expensive. Do not borrow to speculate on inflation.

## A second made-up example: a falling currency

Say your local currency goes from 100 to 125 per dollar.

- 100,000 in local currency was worth $1,000 at 100 per dollar.
- At 125 per dollar it is worth $800, a drop of 20% in dollar terms.
- Someone who held the $1,000 in dollars instead now has 125,000 in local currency, 25% more than before.

That is the case for holding a stronger currency. The risks are the ones above: rules, access, fees, and the chance that the "safe" asset has its own problem. A currency that rises can also fall back.

## Keep some money simple

Whatever mix you choose, keep enough in something you can reach quickly to cover essential bills for a few months. See [how much you should keep in an emergency fund](/blog/how-much-emergency-fund/). Money you might need next month should not be in anything that can swing 10% in a week.

## Common mistakes

- **Chasing a promised return.** Anyone guaranteeing a high yield is a warning sign. See [how to read crypto news without getting burned](/blog/how-to-read-crypto-news-without-getting-burned/).
- **Putting everything in one place.** One exchange, one coin or one bank is a single point of failure.
- **Ignoring local rules.** A product that is allowed in one country can be restricted in another, and penalties vary.
- **Forgetting the exit.** Before you buy, ask how you will turn it back into spendable local money, and at what cost.
- **Borrowing to buy a hedge.** If the hedge falls, you still owe the loan.

## Getting started

1. **Write down the inflation rate you are living with,** and what your accounts actually pay.
2. **Work out your real rate** on each place your money sits.
3. **Check local rules** on foreign currency and crypto before moving anything.
4. **Keep a few months of essentials in an accessible form.**
5. **Spread what remains across more than one option,** and start small with anything new.
