"Latin America" is not one money system. It is a dozen countries with different currencies, different rules about who can open a bank account, and one shared habit: ATMs and card terminals will happily convert your money at a bad rate unless you tell them not to. The good news is that a small, boring setup, a way to receive money, a way to spend it, and a few habits at the machine, works almost everywhere in the region. Here is how to put it together.

## Who this is for, and who it isn't

**It's for:** remote workers and freelancers paid in dollars, euros, or pounds who are spending a few months to a couple of years in places like Mexico, Colombia, Brazil, Argentina, or Costa Rica.

**It's not for:** people building a long-term life in one country, who will need local tax and residency advice for that country. Nothing here is legal or tax advice. For the tax side, start with [tax residency basics for digital nomads](/blog/tax-residency-basics-for-digital-nomads/).

## Start with the setup, not the country

Most nomads who stay out of trouble in the region use the same three pieces, whatever country they're in:

- **A multi-currency account for receiving and converting money** (Wise is the common example). Client payments land here and you convert to local currency when you need it, at a rate close to the mid-market one, instead of letting a bank or ATM pick the rate for you.
- **A card with no foreign-transaction fee** for day-to-day spending.
- **An ATM-friendly debit card** that reimburses or waives withdrawal fees. Charles Schwab is the one US-based nomads usually mention.

Two or three accounts sounds like a lot, but each does one job. For more on choosing them, see [best banking apps for digital nomads](/blog/best-banking-apps-for-digital-nomads/) and [multi-currency budgeting for remote workers](/blog/multi-currency-budgeting-for-remote-workers/).

## The ATM habit that saves the most money

Reports from expats in Mexico describe three layers of cost on a foreign card: your own bank's foreign-ATM fee, the machine operator's fixed fee (often tens of pesos), and a currency-conversion spread. The easiest layer to avoid is the last one. When a machine or card terminal offers to charge you in your home currency instead of local pesos, **decline and choose local currency.** That prompt is called dynamic currency conversion, and critics note its markups are usually higher than what your own card issuer would charge. Choosing local currency lets your card network and issuer do the conversion instead.

Withdraw larger amounts less often, since many fees are flat per withdrawal, and prefer ATMs attached to bank branches, which tend to have clearer screens and are safer to use.

## Can you open a local bank account?

Usually only once you have residency, and a tourist or visitor stay generally isn't enough. Mexico is a typical example. Guides describe banks such as BBVA, Banorte, and Santander wanting a temporary or permanent resident card, plus a CURP (national ID number), often an RFC (tax ID), and proof of address. Some sources say a CURP alone can open a limited basic account, but that depends on the bank and product. Because this varies, ask the specific bank what it requires for your visa category before you go.

For a few months of travel, most nomads skip the local account entirely and rely on the setup above. If you plan to stay a year or more and will have rent, utilities, and local transfers to handle, a local account starts to pay for itself.

## A few countries, in brief

**Argentina.** This is where older advice is most out of date. For years, a wide gap between the official exchange rate and the unofficial "blue dollar" meant carrying cash dollars was far better than using a card. Currency controls on individuals were lifted in April 2025, and 2026 guides describe the gap between official, blue, and card rates as only a few percent. Those same guides say foreign Visa and Mastercard payments now convert at a much friendlier rate than they once did, though sources differ on the details and rates can move quickly, so check a current rate before assuming cash still wins.

**Colombia, Brazil, Costa Rica, and Panama.** All four have some form of remote-worker visa. Reported income thresholds range from roughly US$750 to $1,500 a month in Colombia and Brazil to around US$3,000 a month in Costa Rica and Panama, and sources disagree on exact figures and durations, so confirm with the official immigration site before relying on any number here. Several of these countries tax residents on local income only, which is part of their appeal, but how that applies to you depends on how long you stay and where you're otherwise a tax resident.

**Mexico.** There is no dedicated nomad visa, so people typically stay on the visitor permit (up to 180 days) or apply for temporary residency, which is also what unlocks a normal bank account.

## A worked example

A freelance editor paid in US dollars plans six months split between Mexico City and Medellín. Before leaving, she opens a Wise account and a fee-free-ATM debit card, and she keeps one no-foreign-fee credit card as backup. Client invoices land in Wise, and she converts roughly a month's spending at a time. She pays with the credit card where cards are accepted, takes out larger cash withdrawals from bank-branch ATMs, and declines the home-currency option every time a machine asks. She never opens a local account, since neither stay is long enough to justify the paperwork.

## Where people actually go wrong

- **Accepting "charge in dollars" at the ATM or terminal.** It feels convenient and usually costs more.
- **Following old Argentina advice.** The cash-dollar strategy was built for a gap that has largely closed.
- **Assuming a visa guide's income number is current.** Thresholds change and sources disagree, so verify.
- **Counting on one account.** One frozen card or blocked transfer in a country you don't know well is much easier to survive with a second option.

## Getting started

Open the receiving and spending accounts before you leave, test each with a small transaction, and tell your banks where you're traveling. Make declining home-currency conversion an automatic habit. Then check each country's current visa and tax rules directly with the official source before committing to a long stay.
