A money market account (MMA) is a deposit account at a bank or credit union that usually pays a savings-style interest rate and often comes with a debit card or checks. That mix is why people call it a hybrid between savings and checking. The name also causes confusion, because a very different product, the **money market fund**, shares part of it. This post sorts out the three.

Rates in this post are snapshots from mid-2026. They change, so treat any number here as an example, not an offer.

## Who this is for, and who it isn't

**It's for:** people with an emergency fund or a short-term goal (a car, a tax bill, a down payment) who want their cash to earn interest and stay easy to reach.

**It's not for:** money you won't need for many years, which usually belongs in investments instead; see [how to start investing with little money](/blog/how-to-start-investing-with-little-money/). It's also not a way to earn more than a good high-yield savings account in every case, as the next section shows.

## What a money market account actually is

An MMA is a regular deposit account. At a bank, the type is formally called a money market *deposit* account. The FDIC lists it alongside checking, savings and CDs as covered by deposit insurance, up to $250,000 per depositor, per insured bank, for each account ownership category. At a credit union, the equivalent protection comes from the NCUA.

What tends to set an MMA apart from a basic savings account is access. Bankrate notes that MMAs often include a debit card and check-writing, which most savings accounts don't. The interest rate is set by the bank, so it can move with the wider rate environment; see [what the Fed does and how it affects your money](/blog/what-the-federal-reserve-does-and-how-rate-decisions-affect-your-money/) for why.

## Money market account vs. savings account

| | Savings account | Money market account |
|---|---|---|
| **Insurance** | FDIC or NCUA, up to $250,000 | Same |
| **Interest** | Yes, rate varies widely by bank | Yes, rate varies widely by bank |
| **Checks or debit card** | Rarely | Often |
| **Minimum balance** | Often low or none | Sometimes higher, to open or to earn the best rate |
| **Transfer limits** | Check your bank's terms | Check your bank's terms |

In plain terms: the safety is the same, and the main practical difference is access. Whether the *rate* is better depends entirely on the bank. Some MMAs pay more than a savings account at the same bank, and some online high-yield savings accounts beat a local bank's MMA.

## A note on withdrawal limits

You may read that savings and money market accounts are limited to six withdrawals or transfers a month. That was a federal rule (Regulation D) for years. On April 24, 2020, the Federal Reserve deleted the six-per-month limit from the rule, which let banks stop enforcing it. It did not require them to. According to a law firm's summary, some institutions may keep a numeric limit.

So the safe move is to read your own account's terms. Look for a monthly transaction cap and for the fee charged if you go over it. Many articles online still describe the old rule as if it were current.

## Money market account vs. money market fund

The names are almost identical. The products are not.

| | Money market **account** | Money market **fund** |
|---|---|---|
| **What it is** | A deposit at a bank or credit union | A mutual fund bought through a brokerage |
| **Insurance** | FDIC or NCUA, up to $250,000 | Not FDIC-insured |
| **Can you lose money?** | Not from market moves, within insured limits | Yes, though losses are uncommon |
| **Return** | A stated rate set by the bank | Tracks short-term interest rates, minus fund costs |

The SEC's investor education site (Investor.gov) describes a money market fund as a mutual fund investing in short-term, liquid debt. It says the money isn't guaranteed by the FDIC, so you could lose some or all of it, and that most funds aim to hold a steady $1.00 share price but can fall below it. Bank money market deposit accounts, it points out, *are* FDIC-insured within legal limits.

**Where this goes wrong:** you open an account at a brokerage, see "money market" on the screen and assume it's insured like a bank deposit. It might be a fund. Check which one you have.

## A worked example

Say you keep $10,000 in an emergency fund. These rates are illustrative, based on Bankrate's mid-2026 figures: roughly 0.45% for the national average money market account, and top accounts in the range of about 3.5% to 3.9%.

- **At 0.45%:** about **$45** in interest over a year.
- **At 3.5%:** about **$350** in interest over a year.

(Simple one-year math, ignoring compounding and rate changes.) The gap is about $305 on the same money with the same insurance. The type of account didn't make the difference. The *bank* did. That's the main reason to shop the rate rather than the label.

For how interest builds over longer periods, see [compound interest explained simply](/blog/compound-interest-explained-simply/).

## When an MMA makes sense

- You want your emergency fund to earn interest but also want a debit card or checks as a backup.
- You want savings and bill-paying in one place, kept apart from your everyday checking.
- Your bank's MMA rate beats its savings rate, and you meet any minimum balance.

## When a plain high-yield savings account may be better

- It pays a higher rate with no minimum balance. See [high-yield savings accounts explained](/blog/high-yield-savings-accounts-explained/) for how to compare them.
- You don't need checks or a debit card.
- You'd rather have a bit of friction so the money isn't easy to spend.

## Common mistakes

- **Picking by label, not rate.** Compare the actual APY and its conditions.
- **Missing the minimum.** Some accounts pay the top rate only above a balance threshold, or charge a monthly fee below it.
- **Confusing an account with a fund.** Only the account is a bank deposit.
- **Going over a transaction limit.** If your bank still has one, fees can eat interest.
- **Going past insurance limits.** Coverage is per depositor, per insured bank, per ownership category. If you hold far more than $250,000 at one bank, check how it's covered.
- **Assuming the rate stays put.** It can change at any time. Check every few months.

## Getting started

1. **Confirm the bank is FDIC-insured** (the FDIC's BankFind tool shows this) or the credit union is NCUA-insured.
2. **Read the terms:** APY, minimum balance, monthly fees, transaction limits, and whether the top rate has conditions.
3. **Compare against a high-yield savings account** from a bank you'd also consider.
4. **Decide if you need checks or a debit card.** If not, you may not need an MMA.
5. **Set up a recurring transfer** into it, so it actually fills. See [best ways to automate your savings](/blog/best-ways-to-automate-your-savings/).
