In the space of a week, two governments told their citizens to keep emergency supplies at home. The UK launched a "Be Ready" campaign on October 22, 2026, asking every household to hold about three days of essentials. Japan's disaster minister said he would make an "urgent request" on November 2, the day the country's new disaster management agency launches, asking households to keep at least three days of food, water and toilet supplies, and a week if possible.

Headlines about this have been alarming. The official explanations are much calmer. This post sticks to what governments actually say, and then does the part that belongs on a money blog: how to build a basic kit slowly, cheaply and without buying more than you need. It is not predicting any event.

## Who this is for, and who it isn't

**It's for:** anyone who saw the headlines and wants a sensible, low-cost way to be ready for a power cut, a storm, a cyber outage or a disrupted shop delivery. Those are the everyday risks the guidance is mostly about.

**It isn't for:** people looking for a survival plan. A basic kit covers a few days at home. It is not a bunker, and it doesn't need a big budget. If money is tight right now, start with a small cash buffer, covered in [how much emergency fund you need](/blog/how-much-emergency-fund/), and add supplies a little at a time.

## What governments are actually saying

- **UK.** The Cabinet Office's Be Ready campaign launched on October 22, 2026, as the first phase of a three-year effort, according to press coverage of the launch. The advice is to keep about three days of essentials, built up gradually over ordinary shopping trips. Ministers have said no specific threat prompted it and that it is not a warning of an imminent crisis. They pointed to storms, power cuts and cyber outages as the kind of events it is meant for.
- **Japan.** Disaster Management Minister Goshi Hosono said he planned an emergency request on November 2 for at least three days of supplies, and a week if possible. The reasons given were earthquakes and other natural hazards, such as a major quake near Tokyo or a Nankai Trough quake. Relief supplies can take more than three days to arrive after a big disaster. Press reports say the request is about disaster disruptions rather than an imminent war.
- **Switzerland.** The federal office for national economic supply has run its own campaign for years. It offers an online calculator that builds a shopping list from your household size and diet, rather than one fixed list for everyone.
- **United States.** City and state emergency offices commonly advise at least one gallon of water per person per day for at least three days, plus non-perishable food and a few other basics.

Some reports have linked the UK and US announcements to talk of war with a hostile state. Neither government has said a conflict is imminent, and some of the wilder online claims are unverified. The reasons that are actually stated, again and again, are weather, power and cyber disruptions, and earthquakes in the case of Japan.

## The basic kit, and what each part costs you

The lists from different governments overlap heavily. Here is the common core, with a note on where the money goes.

| Item | How much | Money note |
|---|---|---|
| Drinking water | About 3 litres per person per day in the UK guidance, or about 1 US gallon (3.8 litres) in US guidance, for three days | The cheapest item per day. Washing and cooking need extra |
| Food that needs no cooking | Three days of meals | Buy things you already eat, so nothing is wasted |
| Light and power | A torch with spare batteries, a power bank | Low cost; check batteries every few months |
| Radio | A battery or hand-crank radio | Basic models can be found cheaply, sometimes second-hand |
| First aid and medication | A basic kit and any regular prescriptions | Ask your pharmacist about keeping a small spare supply |
| Cash | A mix of small notes | See below |
| Warmth and documents | Blankets, warm clothing, copies of key papers and phone numbers | Mostly things you already own |
| Pets and babies | Food, formula, nappies, anything specific | Add to the list only if it applies to you |

For a household of four, three days at the UK's 3 litres per person per day comes to 36 litres. That is a lot of bottles, which is why storage and cost both matter.

## How to build it for less

1. **Spread it over weeks, not one shop.** The UK guidance itself says to build the kit gradually. Adding one or two items to each regular shop barely changes the weekly bill. A city preparedness guide from the US makes the same point: write a list, then buy as sales come up, because patience can lower prices a lot.
2. **Buy food you already eat.** The easiest rotation system is the one you don't think about. Buy a couple of extra tins, pasta or rice, and use the oldest first, replacing as you go. Check dates, avoid items you won't actually eat, and discard any tin that bulges or leaks.
3. **Shop sales and store brands.** Store brands are usually fine for basics such as pain relievers, provided the active ingredient matches. Discount shops often sell batteries, torches and basic first aid supplies cheaply.
4. **Store water in clean containers.** The same US guide suggests reusing clean plastic soft-drink bottles, not milk or juice containers, filling them with tap water, labelling the date and storing them somewhere cool and dark. It advises replacing water that wasn't commercially bottled about every six months. Rules for treating tap water vary by country, so check your local authority's page.
5. **Use what you have.** Blankets, warm clothes, a torch and a charger often already live in your home. A kit is partly just knowing where they are.
6. **Skip the gadgets.** You don't need a pre-packed branded kit with a premium price. A shelf or a box and a list does the same job.

| 2 | Extra tins or packets of food you already eat | 10 |

## Cash: the part people forget

The UK guidance includes cash in a mix of denominations in case card or payment systems stop working. That is a money point as well as a safety one. A power cut or a network outage can stop card readers and ATMs for a while.

You don't need a large sum. Think of enough for a few days of essentials, in small notes, kept in a safe place and separate from your normal spending. If that sounds like an emergency fund, it is a small, physical slice of one. Keep it out of the account you check every day, or you'll spend it.

## Don't panic-buy or hoard

Retail reports in the UK said shelves of water, tinned soup, batteries and radios ran low or sold out after the campaign launched, although storms may explain part of that. Buying far more than you need has three problems:

- **It costs more.** Prices and stock are worst exactly when everyone is buying.
- **It leaves others short.** Emptying a shelf can leave a neighbour who needs one case of water with none.
- **It goes to waste.** A pile of food you don't eat will expire, and that's money thrown away.

A three-day kit, built slowly, doesn't need any of that. The point of the government messages is a calm, modest buffer, not a flood of buying.

## Where people actually go wrong

- **Buying a pre-packed kit at a premium** when the same items cost less bought separately.
- **Stocking food nobody likes,** then never rotating it.
- **Forgetting water,** the item you can't live without for even three days.
- **Forgetting regular medication and glasses.**
- **Treating the kit as finished.** Check it twice a year: dates, batteries, water, and who lives in the house now.
- **Letting fear drive the spending.** The guidance is about everyday disruptions. Spending beyond your budget on it adds financial stress you don't need.

## Getting started

1. **Check your local official guidance** (your government's preparedness page or your local emergency office) for quantities that fit where you live.
2. **Write a short list** for your household: people, pets, medication, anything specific.
3. **Add one or two items to each shop** over the next six weeks, starting with water and food you already eat.
4. **Set aside a small cash float** in small notes, kept separate from everyday money.
5. **Put a reminder in your calendar** for six months from now to check dates and batteries.
6. **Pair it with money habits** such as a [sinking fund](/blog/how-to-build-a-sinking-fund-for-irregular-expenses/) for the costs you can see coming, so the kit never has to come out of your rent money.
