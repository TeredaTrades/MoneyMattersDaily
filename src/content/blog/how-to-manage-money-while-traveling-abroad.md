Managing money abroad isn't about how much you brought — it's about whether you can actually get to it when something goes wrong. The four habits below cost nothing to set up and solve the problems that actually derail trips: a blocked card, a bad exchange rate you didn't need to accept, and no idea how much you've spent until the trip's over.

## Who this is for — and who it isn't

**It's for:** anyone on a trip of a week or longer who wants to stop guessing at exchange rates and stop finding out their card is frozen at the worst possible moment.

**It's not for:** figuring out your overall trip budget or choosing a travel-specific bank account — see [how to budget for long-term travel](/blog/how-to-budget-for-long-term-travel/) and [the best banking apps for digital nomads](/blog/best-banking-apps-for-digital-nomads/) for those. This post is about what you do with the money and cards you already have once you've landed.

## Split your money across three places before you leave

Never travel with access to your money through a single card or account. If it's lost, cloned, or flagged as fraud by your bank, you want a working backup that doesn't depend on the first one.

A simple three-way split:
- **Primary card** — the one you use for most purchases day to day.
- **Backup card**, kept in a separate bag or pocket from the primary — a second debit or credit card from a different bank, so a single bank's fraud freeze or a single lost wallet doesn't cut off both at once.
- **Emergency cash**, in the local currency or USD/EUR (both are widely exchangeable), stored separately from both cards — enough to cover 2–3 days of essentials if every card stops working at once.

This isn't about carrying more money overall. It's about making sure no single point of failure — one lost card, one bank's fraud algorithm — cuts off all your access at the same time.

## Tell your bank before you go, or expect a freeze

Card networks flag sudden foreign-country charges as likely fraud by default, and a triggered freeze usually means a phone call to a number you may not be able to easily call from abroad. Most banks let you log a travel notice in-app or online in under five minutes — do it for every card in your three-way split, not just the primary, and list every country on your route, not just the first stop.

If a bank doesn't offer a travel notice feature at all (some smaller banks don't), that's worth knowing before you go, not after a decline at a register — it means that card is more likely to need an active fraud call mid-trip if a charge looks unusual.

## Always decline dynamic currency conversion

At many card terminals and ATMs abroad, you'll be asked: pay in [local currency] or in your home currency? Choosing your home currency is called dynamic currency conversion (DCC), and it almost always uses a worse exchange rate than your card network's own conversion — the merchant or ATM operator sets that rate themselves, with no obligation to match the market rate.

**Always choose to pay in the local currency.** Your card network then converts it at its own rate, which is typically much closer to the real (mid-market) exchange rate.

A worked example: buying something priced at 100 EUR on a card with no foreign transaction fee, with a real mid-market rate of 1 EUR = $1.08 USD.
- **Decline DCC, pay in EUR**: card network converts at close to the real rate → roughly **$108–$109** charged.
- **Accept DCC, pay in USD**: the terminal might quote something like $114 for the same 100 EUR — a markup of roughly **5%**, or about **$5–$6** lost on this one purchase for no reason.

That's one purchase. Over a week of meals, transport, and shopping, accepting DCC by default can quietly cost $50–$100 more than declining it every time — for a choice that takes zero extra effort once you know to look for it.

## Check actual spending weekly, not just before and after the trip

Budgets set before a trip are estimates. The only way to know if you're on track is to look at actual spending partway through — not just when you get home and the trip is already over and unchangeable.

A workable habit: once a week, pull up your card and cash spending, add it up, and compare it to (total budget ÷ total trip days × days elapsed). If you're meaningfully over that pace in week one, you have five, six, or more weeks left to adjust — cut back on one category, extend a cheaper leg of the trip, or accept the higher pace deliberately. Finding out on day 45 of a 60-day trip that you're 40% over budget leaves you far less room to fix it than finding out on day 7.

## Common mistakes

- **Carrying all cards in one wallet.** If it's lost or stolen, your entire backup plan goes with it. Split the primary and backup physically.
- **Accepting DCC because saying "no" feels awkward.** The terminal or ATM screen makes home-currency conversion look like the convenient default. It's a worse rate, not a favor — decline it every time.
- **Withdrawing cash in small amounts repeatedly.** Many ATMs abroad charge a flat fee per withdrawal regardless of amount, so five $50 withdrawals can cost far more in fees than one $250 withdrawal. Check your card's ATM fee structure and withdraw accordingly, balanced against not carrying more cash than you're comfortable with.
- **Assuming your home bank's app will work everywhere.** Some banking apps restrict login from foreign IP addresses as a fraud measure. Test that your app logs in before you leave home, or at minimum know your account number and a phone number that works internationally.

## Getting started

Before you leave: set up the three-way split, log a travel notice for every card and every country on your route, and put a recurring weekly reminder in your phone to check spending against pace. None of this requires opening a new account or changing how you already manage money at home — it's entirely about being ready for the two things that actually go wrong on trips: a card that stops working, and a rate you didn't need to accept.
