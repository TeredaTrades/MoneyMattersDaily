Freelance and gig income isn't just irregular — it's also *unprotected* in ways a regular payroll job isn't. In almost every country, an employer normally withholds tax and pays part of your social contributions before the money even reaches you. When you're self-employed, none of that happens automatically: every dollar, pound, or euro that lands in your account looks fully spendable, even though a chunk of it already has a job waiting. A freelancer budget has to account for that, not just the income swings — see [how to budget on an irregular income](/blog/how-to-budget-on-an-irregular-income/) for the income-timing side; this post is about the parts specific to working for yourself, regardless of which country's system you're filing under.

## Open a second account before you do anything else

The single highest-leverage move is separating business money from personal money, even if you're a sole trader with no legal requirement to. Every payment from a client or platform goes into the business account first. From there, you move yourself a "paycheck" — a fixed, modest amount you can actually live on — into your personal account on a set schedule (weekly or biweekly works well). Everything else — tax and contributions owed, business expenses, the buffer — stays in the business account until it's needed.

This does two things a single shared account can't: it stops a large invoice from *looking* like spending money when a third of it is already earmarked for tax, and it gives you a real number — what you actually pay yourself — to budget against, instead of budgeting against whatever happens to be sitting in the account that day.

## The tax-and-contributions piece a payroll job used to handle for you

A payroll employer typically does two things you don't notice until you're self-employed: it withholds income tax automatically, and it pays (and withholds) social insurance contributions — the system that funds state pension, healthcare, or unemployment benefits, depending on the country. Self-employed people are usually on the hook for both, calculated and paid themselves, and the *combined* rate is almost always higher than employees assume, because the "employer half" that used to be invisible is now theirs too.

The specifics vary enormously by country — a few examples of how differently this is structured:

- **United States:** self-employment tax (15.3%, covering Social Security and Medicare) on top of ordinary federal and state income tax, paid via quarterly estimated payments.
- **United Kingdom:** Class 2 and Class 4 National Insurance contributions on top of income tax, reported through Self Assessment.
- **Many EU countries:** mandatory self-employed social security contributions (pension, health insurance) calculated separately from income tax, often with a minimum contribution regardless of how little you earned.

Rather than centering a budget on any one country's exact formula, the useful habit is universal: **set aside a fixed percentage of every payment, the moment it's paid, into a separate tax-and-contributions holding account** — before it's counted as spendable at all. A commonly used starting point is 25–30% of gross freelance income, adjusted up or down once you know your own country's actual combined rate (check with a local accountant or your tax authority's self-employed guidance).

**Worked example:** say you hold back 28% of every payment. On a $2,000 invoice, that's $560 moved to the holding account immediately, leaving $1,440 as the real, spendable amount — not $2,000. Do this on every payment, all year, and the amount sitting in the holding account when your tax bill or contributions payment comes due is close to correct, because it was never mentally "your" money to begin with. Skip this step and the same $2,000 tends to get spent as though the tax portion doesn't exist — because nothing physically stopped it from being spent, unlike a payroll job where it was never in your account in the first place.

Find your own percentage once, properly — most countries have an official self-employed/sole-trader tax and contributions calculator or guidance page — then treat it as a fixed rule, not a guess you re-litigate every time you get paid.

## Track deductible expenses like it's part of the job, not an afterthought

In almost every tax system, legitimate business expenses — equipment, software subscriptions, a portion of your phone or internet bill, mileage for gig driving, a home office if you qualify — reduce the income your tax and contributions are calculated on. Skipping this doesn't just cost you the deduction; it means paying tax and contributions on money that was never really profit. Keep receipts and a simple mileage or expense log as you go — reconstructing a year of expenses from memory and bank statements at filing time is where most of the deduction gets lost, not from any rule against claiming it.

## Gig-platform pay has its own quirks

If your income comes through platforms (rideshare, delivery, marketplace freelancing) rather than direct client invoices, budget around a few platform-specific realities:

- **Instant payout fees.** Cashing out same-day instead of waiting for the standard payout schedule usually costs a percentage or flat fee. It's a legitimate tool for a genuine cash-flow gap, but treat it as a fee you're paying for speed, not as free money.
- **The tax/earnings summary a platform issues won't match your bank deposits exactly.** Platform fees are often deducted before the payout hits your account, but the earnings report may show the gross amount before fees — the difference is a deductible business expense, not unreported income, but only if you track it.
- **Multiple platforms means multiple, staggered payout schedules.** If you drive for one app and deliver for another, your "paycheck" isn't one number on one day — build your personal-account transfer around a weekly or biweekly total across all of them, not around chasing each individual payout.

## No employer benefits means you're self-insuring

A payroll job usually bundles in things that don't show up as a separate expense until you're the one buying them: health coverage, paid sick time, and often unemployment protection. How much of this is provided publicly regardless of employment status varies a lot by country — some have strong universal healthcare that makes this section matter far less than it would elsewhere — but wherever it isn't automatic, it has to be budgeted in deliberately.

- **Health coverage costs**, if not provided publicly, are usually the single largest line item freelancers underestimate — price it early rather than discovering the number when you actually need a plan.
- **A sick-day buffer** functions like an emergency fund but is sized around lost income, not just expenses — a few weeks of your baseline personal "paycheck," held separately, covers the gap if you can't work for a stretch.
- **Little or no unemployment protection** if a client relationship or platform dries up means your regular emergency fund needs to lean toward the higher end of any range you'd use for a steady payroll job.

## Who this is for — and who it isn't

**It's for:** freelancers, independent contractors, and gig-platform workers anywhere in the world, where no employer is withholding tax/contributions or providing benefits on your behalf.

**It's not for:** someone with a single steady payroll job looking for a first budget — start with [how to make a budget for beginners](/blog/how-to-make-a-budget-for-beginners/) instead, since the withholding and benefits gaps this post covers don't apply to you. If your main challenge is the month-to-month income swing rather than the self-employment-specific pieces, [how to budget on an irregular income](/blog/how-to-budget-on-an-irregular-income/) is the more direct fit.

## Common mistakes

- **Spending the full payment as if it's all yours.** The tax-and-contributions portion was never really spendable, even though nothing stopped you from spending it.
- **Guessing at a holdback percentage instead of checking your own country's actual rate.** 25–30% is a reasonable starting point, not a substitute for confirming your real combined rate.
- **Mixing business and personal spending in one account.** It doesn't just make bookkeeping harder — it makes it much easier to accidentally spend tax money before it's time to pay it.
- **Skipping expense tracking because a year "wasn't that complicated."** Even modest freelance income leaves real deductions on the table without a running log.
- **Treating health coverage and a sick-day buffer as an afterthought.** Wherever they're not provided publicly, both are real, ongoing costs of self-employment — building them into the budget from month one is cheaper than discovering the gap during an emergency.

## Getting started

Open the second account, set your personal "paycheck" amount, confirm your own country's real combined tax-and-contributions rate, and start moving that percentage of every payment into holding before you touch the rest. That one habit — done from the very next payment, not retroactively — prevents the single most common freelancer money problem: a tax bill you technically earned the money for months ago but no longer have.
