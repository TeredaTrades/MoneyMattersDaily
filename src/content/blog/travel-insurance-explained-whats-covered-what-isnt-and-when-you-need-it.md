Travel insurance is easy to buy at checkout and hard to understand afterwards. It does not protect you from "anything that goes wrong." It repays specific losses, for specific reasons, up to specific limits, and the list of reasons that are not covered is often as important as the list that are. This guide explains the main types of cover, what policies commonly exclude, what it costs, and how to decide whether a given trip needs it.

The sources here are mostly US insurance regulators and consumer pages. Policies, rules and prices differ by country and insurer, so treat this as a map of the questions to ask, not a guarantee of what any policy pays.

## Who this is for, and who it isn't

**It's for:** anyone paying for a trip they could not easily afford to lose, travelling somewhere their normal health insurance may not apply, or heading to a place where medical care is far away or expensive.

**It isn't a reason to buy cover for every weekend away.** For a cheap, refundable trip close to home, you may be paying for protection you do not need. The point of the exercise is to match the policy to the size of the possible loss.

## The main types of cover

The US National Association of Insurance Commissioners (NAIC), in a June 2026 consumer article, describes these main parts. Many policies bundle several of them.

- **Trip cancellation, interruption or delay.** Repays prepaid trip costs if you cancel or cut a trip short for a reason the policy lists. It can also help with a hotel if a delay forces an overnight stay.
- **Travel medical.** Covers short-term medical care if you get sick or injured while travelling.
- **Medical evacuation.** Covers emergency transport to the nearest suitable hospital and, where needed, transport home. The NAIC notes it matters most in remote places with limited medical care.
- **Accidental death and dismemberment.** Pays you or your beneficiaries after a serious accident during the trip.
- **Baggage loss or delay.** Covers lost or delayed bags and personal items.
- **Cancel for any reason (CFAR).** An optional add-on, covered below.

An Arizona state insurance page also points out that travel medical cover can matter abroad, where standard health plans and, for older travellers, government plans such as Medicare often do not apply. It adds that some countries require proof of medical insurance for a visa.

## What policies commonly leave out

The same sources list exclusions that come up again and again:

- **Pre-existing health conditions.** Whether they are covered, and under what conditions, varies by policy. Ask directly.
- **Pandemics.**
- **Civil or political unrest at the destination.**
- **Pregnancy and childbirth.**
- **Certain activities,** such as bungee jumping, backcountry skiing and snowboarding (NAIC).

Two other practical limits are worth knowing. First, the **reason** matters: trip cancellation pays only for reasons the policy names, such as a doctor saying you cannot travel, a family member's hospitalisation or death, severe weather, or certain airline or cruise disruptions. Changing your mind is not on the list. Second, **timing** matters: if a storm has already been named before you buy, the NAIC says claims related to it are likely not covered.

## Cancel for any reason, and what it really means

CFAR lets you cancel for a reason a standard policy would not accept. The NAIC lists the usual catches:

- It typically must be bought within a set period after you book.
- It costs more than standard cover.
- It usually refunds only a portion of your trip costs, not all.
- You must usually cancel within a set window before departure.
- You generally have to insure all your prepaid, non-refundable trip expenses to qualify.

If you cannot afford to lose the trip for any reason at all, CFAR is worth pricing. If you can absorb the loss, it may not be.

## What it costs

The NAIC says policies usually cost about 5% to 10% of the total trip price, depending on the season, the destination, the traveller's age and other factors. That is a rough range, not a quote, and it can be higher for older travellers, longer trips or CFAR. Many booking sites sell cover at checkout, so compare it with a policy you buy separately.

## Check what you already have

Before paying, look for overlapping cover:

- **Your health insurance.** Does it apply outside your home country, and does it pay for evacuation? Often it does not.
- **Your credit card.** Some cards include trip cancellation or baggage cover. Check the limits and the conditions, such as paying for the whole trip with that card. See [travel credit cards explained](/blog/travel-credit-cards-explained-points-miles-and-foreign-transaction-fees/) for how card benefits work.
- **Your employer or school,** for work or study trips.

If you find some cover, you may only need to top it up, for example by adding medical evacuation.

## A worked example

This is invented and uses a made-up currency. Someone books a trip for 3,200 (non-refundable flights and hotels), and a quote comes back at 5% to 10% of the trip price.

- At 5%: 3,200 × 0.05 = **160**.
- At 10%: 3,200 × 0.10 = **320**.

Is that worth it? Ask two questions. If the trip were cancelled for a covered reason, could they afford to lose 3,200? And are there risks the policy would not actually cover, such as a pre-existing condition flare-up, that make the cover less valuable than the price suggests? If the answer to the first is "no, that would hurt," the 160 to 320 buys a clear benefit. If it is "yes, I could absorb it," they might take a cheaper policy or skip it. Either way, the number to focus on is the **loss they cannot afford**, not the premium.

## Common mistakes

- **Buying on the price alone.** A cheap policy with big exclusions can pay nothing when you need it.
- **Not reading the exclusions.** Look for pre-existing conditions, pandemics, unrest and your planned activities first.
- **Buying late.** Cover bought after a problem is foreseeable, such as a named storm, may not apply to it.
- **Assuming your credit card covers everything.** Card cover often has low limits, secondary status and strict conditions.
- **Ignoring medical limits and evacuation.** For trips abroad, a small medical limit can run out fast. Check it, and check whether evacuation is included.
- **Not keeping records.** Save receipts, medical reports and written confirmations from airlines or hotels, because claims depend on proof.

## Getting started

1. **List the non-refundable costs** and decide how much you could afford to lose.
2. **Check your existing cover:** health insurance, credit card and any employer policy.
3. **Compare two or three policies** on medical limit, evacuation, cancellation reasons and exclusions, not just price.
4. **Ask about pre-existing conditions and your activities** before you pay.
5. **Buy soon after booking,** keep the policy documents on your phone, and note the claims contact and the insurer's emergency number.
