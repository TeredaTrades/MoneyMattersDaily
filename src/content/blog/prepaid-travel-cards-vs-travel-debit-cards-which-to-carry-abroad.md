Two cards often get lumped together as "travel cards": a prepaid travel money card you load before you go, and a debit card chosen because it handles foreign spending well. They are not the same product. One keeps your trip money in a separate pot and sometimes locks in an exchange rate. The other draws on your own account and usually converts at the card network's rate when you pay. Which one is better depends on the fees, how you like to budget, and what happens if the card is lost.

This guide is general. Fees, protections and rules differ a lot by country and provider, so use it as a checklist to compare real cards, not as a ranking.

## Who this is for, and who it isn't

**It's for:** travelers deciding what to put in their wallet for a trip abroad, and anyone surprised by a foreign-fee line on a card statement.

**It isn't** a list of named cards with current fees. Those change often, and the sources used here do not support quoting them. For account types that hold several currencies, see [multi-currency wallets compared](/blog/best-multi-currency-wallets-compared-one-account-for-several-currencies/).

## What each card is

**A prepaid travel money card** is a card you load with money, which you can exchange into one or more currencies, then use like a debit card for purchases and ATM withdrawals overseas. Most run on Visa or Mastercard networks and are offered by banks, airlines and currency retailers, according to the Australian consumer group CHOICE (September 2025). Wise's own guide (January 2026) describes it as a dedicated account you top up in advance, separate from your everyday account. Wise sells a competing card, so its comparisons lean its way.

**A travel debit card** is a normal debit card linked to an account, picked because it charges little or nothing for foreign spending. CHOICE says low-fee debit cards from smaller banks generally convert at the Visa or Mastercard rate on the spot, with no conversion fee and no ATM fee from the bank, while cards from the largest banks often charge heavy overseas fees.

## Where the costs hide

CHOICE lists the typical fees on prepaid travel cards. Check each one before you buy:

- **Issue, account opening and closing fees.**
- **Loading (reload) fees,** which can depend on how you pay.
- **Currency conversion or exchange fees,** and how close the rate is to the mid-market rate Wise suggests comparing against.
- **Overseas ATM fees,** plus whatever the machine's own operator charges.
- **Cash-out or transfer-off fees** when you want your unspent balance back.
- **Inactivity fees** if the card sits unused. Wise notes these can drain a balance.
- **Minimum load amounts,** which CHOICE found ranged from nothing to A$350 depending on the card.

For a debit card, the main things to check are the foreign transaction fee, the bank's own ATM fee and whether the card charges a flat fee per withdrawal. Whichever card you use, [decline dynamic currency conversion](/blog/dynamic-currency-conversion-why-to-decline-it-and-how-to-avoid-it/) at the terminal. CHOICE flags it as a way to lose money on prepaid cards too.

## What a prepaid card does better

- **A separate pot.** Wise points out that the card is not linked to your everyday account, which limits what a thief can reach.
- **Budgeting.** You load a fixed amount, so you can see what you have left (CHOICE).
- **Locked-in rates on some cards.** Wise describes loading your home currency and converting into the destination currency in advance, which fixes the rate. That helps you plan, but a locked rate can be worse than the day's rate, so it is not automatically cheaper.
- **Less cash to carry** (CHOICE).

## What a prepaid card does worse

- **Costs can be higher.** CHOICE says rates are often less competitive than low-fee debit cards, and loading, cash-out and inactivity fees add up.
- **Some currencies cannot be preloaded,** and not every card works at every ATM or merchant (CHOICE).
- **Hotels and car rental desks may not accept it** as security for a deposit (CHOICE). That is a real reason to carry a second card.
- **Protection varies.** Wise says to check card-loss and dispute protection with the provider directly, and I could not verify protections from the sources I read.

## What a travel debit card does better and worse

**Better:** often the lowest cost, since a low-fee card converts at the network rate with no extra charge (CHOICE). There is nothing to load, and you do not need to guess the amount in advance.

**Worse:** it is linked to your real account, so a lost card or a fraud problem puts that balance at risk. It can also be less useful for budgeting, because there is no fixed pot.

## A worked example

These fees are invented, to show how to compare. Say you spend **2,000** over a trip and make two ATM withdrawals.

| Card | Loading | Conversion | ATM fees | Total | Share of spend |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Prepaid card | 1% = 20 | 1.5% = 30 | 2 × 2 = 4 | **54** | 2.7% |
| Low-fee debit card | none | none | 2 × 4 = 8 | **8** | 0.4% |
| Big-bank debit card | none | 3% = 60 | 2 × 5 = 10 | **70** | 3.5% |

In this made-up case, the low-fee debit card is cheapest, the prepaid card sits in the middle and the big-bank card costs most. Change the numbers (a prepaid card with no loading fee, or a debit card with a high flat ATM fee) and the order changes. The method matters more than the result: add up every fee for your trip, then compare.

## Which to choose

- **Prepaid** can fit if you want a fixed budget, a separate pot, and you have checked the full fee list.
- **A low-fee debit card** often fits best if cost matters most and you are comfortable with the card being linked to your main account.
- **Carry both,** or one of either plus a credit card. [Travel credit cards explained](/blog/travel-credit-cards-explained-points-miles-and-foreign-transaction-fees/) covers when a credit card is the safer option.

Some app-based accounts and card products also hold several currencies, and some draw on crypto or stablecoin balances. They can be worth comparing, but check who holds your money, how conversion is priced and what protection exists if something goes wrong. The same fee checklist applies.

## Common mistakes

- **Comparing only the headline exchange rate.** Fees can add more than the rate does.
- **Ignoring inactivity and cash-out fees.** They appear after the trip.
- **Bringing only one card.** A blocked, lost or unaccepted card with no backup is a bad way to spend a holiday.
- **Accepting the merchant's currency.** Pay in the local currency.
- **Using a card that charges per ATM withdrawal for many small withdrawals.** See [how to use ATMs abroad without overpaying](/blog/how-to-use-atms-abroad-without-overpaying-in-fees/).

## Getting started

1. **List the cards you already have** and check their foreign fees.
2. **For any prepaid card, read the full fee table** and the protection terms.
3. **Estimate your trip spending and ATM use,** then add up the fees as in the example.
4. **Pick a primary card and a backup,** ideally from different accounts.
5. **Tell your bank you are traveling** if it asks, and keep its contact number offline.
