In many countries, one of the oldest ways to save is to do it with other people. A group of friends, relatives or colleagues agrees to put the same amount into a pot every month. Each month, one member takes home the whole pot. The rotation continues until everyone has had a turn. In Ethiopia this is called an ekub (also spelled iqqub or equb), in Kenya a chama, in West Africa a susu, and researchers call the general model a rotating savings and credit association, or ROSCA.

It is simple, it needs no bank account, and it works for millions of people. It also has risks that are easy to miss when everyone in the group is friendly. This guide explains how it works and what to check before you join.

## Who this is for, and who it isn't

**It's for:** anyone invited to join a savings group, anyone thinking of starting one, and anyone curious how informal saving compares with a bank account.

**It isn't** legal advice. Rules about informal groups differ by country, and this post does not describe the law anywhere in particular. It is also not a recommendation to join or avoid one.

## How a rotating savings group works

A World Bank chapter on informal finance describes the typical shape: a small group, usually between six and forty people, chooses a leader who collects a fixed amount from every member each period. The pot goes to one member, and the turn rotates. In the simplest version the money is an interest-free loan to the person who receives it early, repaid through their later contributions. More sophisticated groups let members bid for an earlier turn, and some pay the organiser a commission.

For Ethiopia, a University of Nottingham research paper on equbs (using survey data from roughly the year 2000, so the amounts are very old) reports that most equbs decide the order by random draw, that a typical urban group has monthly contributions and about 24 members, and that a cycle runs around two years. The same paper found that many members join to buy household goods or to stock a small business, for example inventory for street vending.

## Why people use them

- **Forced discipline.** The World Bank chapter notes that in one Bolivian survey, over 90 percent of members joined mainly to save more, saying that membership forced them to do it. The group's expectation is harder to ignore than a bank balance.
- **A lump sum sooner than you could save alone.** If you draw an early turn, you can buy something now that would have taken months of saving.
- **No fees and no paperwork.** Most groups run on trust, and a bank account is not required.
- **Social support.** The group is often made up of people who know each other, which is part of how it works, and also part of how it can go wrong.

## The risks, in plain terms

**1. Someone stops paying after they receive the pot.** This is the main risk. The Nottingham paper describes it as depletion: early winners may stop contributing, which lowers payouts for later members. The World Bank chapter says informal arrangements have no access to legal sanctions and rely on local social pressure and standing instead. It also says such groups generally work only for small circles of people who know one another well.

**2. The order matters.** Early turns get the money first. Late turns take the risk. The Nottingham paper notes that an early payout can fund a purchase that earns a good return, while a late payout loses that opportunity, and in the worst case the return over the whole cycle is zero in nominal terms. Our own addition: if prices are rising quickly where you live, a payout in month 20 buys less than the same amount in month 2.

**3. The organiser holds the cash.** In some groups the leader collects and passes on the money. The sources above do not say how often organisers fail, so we cannot give a figure, but it is a point of trust you should be able to see. In a commission group the World Bank chapter says the leader may take on liability for defaults, which is a reason to ask exactly what the leader promises.

**4. No deposit insurance.** Money held in a group is usually not covered by the deposit-insurance schemes that cover bank accounts. Check this locally before relying on it.

**5. Pressure to join or to cover someone.** Social pressure keeps members paying, and it can also push you to stay in when you can no longer afford the contribution.

## A worked example

This is invented. Ten people each pay 1,000 a month into a group, so the pot is 10,000 a month for ten months. Each person receives it once.

| Your turn | You pay in total | You receive | What it means |
|---|---|---|---|
| Month 1 | 1,000 in month 1, then 9,000 more over nine months | 10,000 in month 1 | You have borrowed 9,000, interest-free, and must keep paying |
| Month 5 | 5,000 by your turn | 10,000 | You are partway through paying back |
| Month 10 | 9,000 before your turn, 1,000 in month 10 | 10,000 in month 10 | You have saved for ten months and get your own money back |

Over the whole cycle everyone pays 10,000 and receives 10,000, so nobody earns interest.

Now suppose the member who drew month 3 pays only the first three months and then disappears. They paid 3,000 and received 10,000, so they are 7,000 ahead. From month 4 on, only nine people are paying, so each pot is 9,000. The seven people who still have turns to come (months 4 to 10) each receive 1,000 less than they should, which adds up to the 7,000 that went missing. Members who took turns 1 and 2 are unaffected. The members with the late turns carry the loss.

The group may decide to share the loss or chase the missing member. Without a written agreement, you may find out only when it happens.

## How to join one with your eyes open

- **Know every member.** The model works best with a small group of people who know and can find one another.
- **Ask who holds the money and how it is handed over.** A named organiser, a written list of members and turns, and a record of each payment (a notebook, a message thread or a mobile-money receipt) are basic protections.
- **Ask what happens if someone defaults** before you join, and get the answer in writing, even a text message. Does the group cover it, does the organiser, or does the loss fall on the last people in line?
- **Choose your turn for your reason.** If you need a lump sum for a known cost, an early turn helps, as long as you will be able to keep paying. If you want help saving, a later turn builds a habit with less risk of being the one left owing money, but carries default risk.
- **Pay only what you can afford every month**, including in months when your income is low. If your income is irregular, see [how to budget on an irregular income](/blog/how-to-budget-on-an-irregular-income/).
- **Pay by a method that leaves a record,** such as mobile money. See [mobile money in Africa](/blog/mobile-money-in-africa-m-pesa-and-digital-banking-basics-explained/) for how those systems work.
- **Do not put your whole emergency fund into one.** A group payout is not available when you need it, because your turn may be months away. See [how much emergency fund do you need](/blog/how-much-emergency-fund/).
- **Be wary of anything that promises returns.** A rotating group returns your own money. If someone calls it an "investment" with a profit, or asks you to recruit new members to be paid, that is a different thing and a warning sign.

## Common mistakes

- **Joining without knowing who else is in it.**
- **Taking an early turn and spending the money without a plan to keep paying.**
- **Treating it as an emergency fund,** when the money is locked until your turn.
- **No written record of payments or the turn order,** which makes any dispute a matter of memory.
- **Staying in a group you can no longer afford** because leaving feels embarrassing. Tell the group early, and ask whether someone can take over your turn.

## Getting started

1. **Decide what the money is for** and when you need it.
2. **Meet or message the full member list** and agree the amount, the order and the payment method.
3. **Write down the rules,** including what happens on a late or missed payment.
4. **Keep your own record** of every payment you make and every payout you receive.
5. **Keep a separate small emergency fund** so one missed month does not force you to borrow. A simple way to build one is [automating your savings](/blog/best-ways-to-automate-your-savings/).
