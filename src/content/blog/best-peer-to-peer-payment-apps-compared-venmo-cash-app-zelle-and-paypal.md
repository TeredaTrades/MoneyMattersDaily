Splitting a dinner bill, paying a friend back, sending rent to a flatmate: peer-to-peer (P2P) payment apps make it easy. In the US, four names dominate: Venmo, Cash App, Zelle and PayPal. They look alike but work differently, and the differences matter most when you are in a hurry, sending a larger amount, or sending to someone you do not know well.

This post compares them on criteria rather than crowning a "best," because the right one depends on how you use it. It quotes no fee amounts or limits, because those change and differ by account, bank and country, and I could not open the providers' own fee pages. It also covers options beyond the big four, for readers who live outside the US or deal in other currencies. It is general information, not a recommendation of any app.

## Who this is for, and who it isn't

**It's for:** people choosing how to pay friends, family or small sellers, and anyone who wants to understand the scam risks.

**It isn't for:** people who need to run a business. Business accounts have their own fees and rules, so read the provider's current business terms.

## The core difference: where your money sits

According to a 2026 comparison from WealthVieu (published March 2026, updated October 2026), **Zelle** moves money directly between enrolled bank accounts. It has no wallet, and it runs through your bank's app or website. **Venmo, PayPal and Cash App** hold money in an in-app balance until you move it to your bank.

That one difference drives most of the rest:

- **Zelle** is tied to your bank, so limits and some features depend on your bank.
- **The balance-based apps** act like small accounts, so you can leave money in them, spend it, or cash it out.
- **PayPal** also handles online shopping and merchant payments, and **Cash App** adds a debit card and investing features, according to a Primeway Federal Credit Union comparison.

## Comparing them on the things that matter

| Criterion | Zelle | Venmo | Cash App | PayPal |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| How it works | Bank-to-bank, no wallet | In-app balance, social feed | In-app balance, debit card | In-app balance, shopping and merchant payments |
| Speed to the recipient's bank | Typically minutes | Standard transfer takes days; instant costs extra | Standard transfer takes days; instant costs extra | Standard transfer takes days; instant costs extra |
| Where fees tend to appear | Usually none for consumers, but your bank may set rules | Paying by credit card, instant cash-out, seller payments | Paying by credit card, instant cash-out | Card payments, instant cash-out, goods and services |
| Limits | Set by each bank | Low until you verify your identity | Low until you verify your identity | Low until you verify your identity |
| Buyer protection | None | Opt-in on eligible goods and services payments | Payments usually final | On goods and services payments, not friends and family |
| If you send to a scammer | Generally cannot be reversed | Generally not refunded | Generally final | Generally not refunded for personal payments |

These patterns come from the WealthVieu and Primeway comparisons and a third 2026 comparison on eco.com (October 2026). Specific amounts differ between those sources, and the Primeway page itself says to confirm current fees and limits with each provider, so check each app's own fee page before relying on any number.

## A worked example: where the cost shows up

These numbers are invented, to show how fees work, not what any app charges.

You owe a friend 200 and pay with a credit card in an app that charges a funding fee of 3%. The fee is 200 × 3% = **6**, so the friend gets 200 and you pay 206. If you pay from your bank account or debit card, the same app might charge nothing.

Now suppose the friend cashes out 500 from an app balance using an instant transfer that costs 1.5% (also invented). The fee is 500 × 1.5% = **7.50**. Waiting for the standard transfer, which takes a few days, usually costs nothing.

The pattern to remember: sending from a bank account is usually free, and the costs appear when you use a credit card, want money instantly, or take payments for goods and services.

## Protection: the part people get wrong

Protection depends on whether the payment was authorized.

- **Unauthorized transactions** (for example, someone hacks your account) get the strongest protection. The eco.com comparison, citing the US Consumer Financial Protection Bureau, says P2P apps generally must investigate these under federal Regulation E.
- **Payments you send yourself, including to a scammer,** are generally not refunded. According to WealthVieu, Zelle payments to enrolled users cannot be canceled or reversed, though Zelle says qualifying imposter scams may be eligible for reimbursement, and eco.com says participating banks reimburse confirmed fraud and certain imposter scams. Rules for scams vary by provider and change, so ask your bank or app what applies.
- **Buying from a seller** is the case where you can get extra protection. PayPal's goods and services payments carry purchase protection and allow disputes, while friends and family payments do not. Venmo protects eligible goods and services purchases, but generally not personal payments. Zelle offers no buyer protection.

A simple rule: treat a P2P payment like handing over cash. Only send to people you know, and check the name, phone number or email before you confirm.

## Taxes

Payments between friends are not income, but money you take for goods or services can be. The WealthVieu article notes that business income is taxable either way, and that reporting thresholds for payment apps apply to goods and services payments. Those thresholds have changed in recent years, so check the current rules with your tax authority.

## Where people actually go wrong

- **Sending by credit card out of habit** and paying a funding fee for no reason.
- **Using instant transfer every time** when a standard transfer would be free.
- **Leaving a large balance in an app.** A balance in a P2P app is generally not the same as money in a bank account. Check what protection applies.
- **Paying a stranger or a seller with a friends and family option** and giving up buyer protection.
- **Falling for payment scams,** like "I sent you too much, send it back" or an urgent request from a "bank" asking you to move money to yourself first.
- **Not checking the recipient.** A typo in a phone number or email can send money to the wrong person.
- **Ignoring the app's privacy settings.** Some apps show payments in a social feed by default.

## Getting started

1. **Decide what you mostly do:** pay friends, buy from sellers, or move money abroad.
2. **Link a bank account or debit card** rather than a credit card, unless you want to pay a fee.
3. **Turn on two-step verification** and a PIN or fingerprint lock.
4. **Check privacy settings** before the first payment.
5. **Read the provider's current fee and protection pages,** since figures here are not quoted.
6. **Only send to people you know,** and double-check the recipient every time.
