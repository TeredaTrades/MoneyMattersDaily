An ISA — Individual Savings Account — isn't a type of savings account so much as a tax wrapper you put one inside. The money you hold in an ISA can sit in cash or be invested in stocks and funds; either way, the interest, dividends, and gains it produces are free of both Income Tax and Capital Gains Tax, with nothing to report on a tax return. For the 2026/27 tax year (6 April 2026 to 5 April 2027), UK residents aged 18 or over can pay up to £20,000 total across their ISAs.

## What the £20,000 allowance actually covers

The £20,000 is a combined annual subscription limit, not a per-account limit. You can split it across a Cash ISA, a Stocks and Shares ISA, and an Innovative Finance ISA in whatever proportion you want, or put the full amount into just one. Since April 2024, you can also hold and pay into more than one ISA of the same type in a single tax year — two different Cash ISAs with different providers, for example — as long as your total contributions across everything stay at or under £20,000.

The allowance doesn't roll over. If you only pay in £5,000 this tax year, you don't get £25,000 of room next year — the unused £15,000 is simply gone once 5 April passes. There's also no lifetime cap on how large an ISA can grow: pay in the maximum every year for a decade and the growth on all of it stays tax-free indefinitely, even though your total *contributions* over that decade would be capped at whatever you actually paid in each year.

## The four ISA types, and who each one actually fits

- **Cash ISA** — works like a savings account, pays interest, no investment risk. Fits money you might need within the next few years, where protecting the amount matters more than growing it. For the mechanics of comparing rates and providers, see [best high-yield savings accounts explained](/blog/high-yield-savings-accounts-explained/) — the ISA wrapper is about tax treatment, not about which account pays the best rate.
- **Stocks and Shares ISA** — holds funds, shares, or bonds instead of cash. Fits money with a longer horizon (typically 5+ years) where you're willing to accept short-term price swings for better long-run growth potential. If you're new to what actually goes inside one, [index funds explained for beginners](/blog/index-funds-explained-for-beginners/) covers the most common building block.
- **Lifetime ISA (LISA)** — for either a first home (up to £450,000) or retirement from age 60. You can pay in up to £4,000 a year, counted inside your overall £20,000, and the government adds a 25% bonus on top — up to £1,000 a year if you max it out. The catch: withdraw for anything else before 60 and a 25% exit charge applies, which claws back the full bonus plus a slice of your own money.
- **Junior ISA (JISA)** — opened by a parent or guardian for a child under 18, with its own separate £9,000 allowance that doesn't touch the adult's £20,000. The child can't access the money until they turn 18.

## Who this is for — and who it isn't

**It's for:** anyone in the UK trying to decide whether to use an ISA at all, or which type fits a specific goal — an emergency fund, a house deposit, or long-term investing.

**It's not for:** people outside the UK tax system, since ISAs are a UK-specific wrapper with no equivalent status abroad, or anyone already using their full £20,000 and wondering what comes next — that's a pensions/SIPP question, which works differently and isn't covered here.

## A worked example: splitting the allowance across two goals

Say you have £20,000 to save this tax year: £8,000 you want easy access to as an emergency fund, and £12,000 you're investing for a goal at least 10 years away.

- £8,000 → Cash ISA, held in a straightforward easy-access account, no investment risk
- £12,000 → Stocks and Shares ISA, invested in a diversified fund

Both portions sit inside the same £20,000 annual allowance, so this split uses the full amount without exceeding it. Whatever interest the Cash ISA portion earns and whatever the Stocks and Shares ISA portion grows by are both untouched by Income Tax or Capital Gains Tax — compare that to holding the same £20,000 outside an ISA, where interest above your Personal Savings Allowance and gains above your Capital Gains Tax allowance would both be taxable.

## What's changing from April 2027

In the Autumn 2025 Budget, the Chancellor announced that from 6 April 2027, the amount under-65s can pay into a **Cash ISA specifically** will drop from £20,000 to £12,000 a year. The overall £20,000 ISA allowance isn't changing — the remaining £8,000 would need to go into a Stocks and Shares ISA or Innovative Finance ISA instead if you want to use the full amount. Savers aged 65 and over keep the full £20,000 Cash ISA limit. Nothing changes for the current 2026/27 tax year, and existing Cash ISA balances built up before April 2027 aren't affected either way.

## Common mistakes

- **Assuming an ISA is itself an investment.** It's a tax wrapper around whatever you put inside it — a Cash ISA holding cash still only earns whatever interest rate that cash account pays, and a Stocks and Shares ISA still carries the risk of whatever it's invested in.
- **Letting the allowance reset pass unused.** Because it doesn't carry over, waiting until near the 5 April deadline to decide risks losing the year's allowance entirely if life gets in the way.
- **Opening a second Lifetime ISA or Junior ISA in the same tax year.** The same-type flexibility introduced in 2024 doesn't extend to LISAs or Junior ISAs — you're still limited to one of each per tax year.
- **Withdrawing from a LISA for something other than a first home or retirement.** The 25% exit charge doesn't just remove the government bonus — it takes a real bite out of your own contributions too.

## Getting started

If you don't already have an ISA, the first decision is simply Cash versus Stocks and Shares, based on when you'll actually need the money — not which one sounds more exciting. Money needed within a few years belongs in cash; money with a decade or more to grow can reasonably take on investment risk. From there, the £20,000 allowance is just a ceiling to split however your goals require, and it resets every 6 April whether you've used it or not.
