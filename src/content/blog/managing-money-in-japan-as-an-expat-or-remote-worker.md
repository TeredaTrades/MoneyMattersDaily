Japan is one of the more welcoming countries in the region on paper — ultra-fast internet, a genuine digital nomad visa, and a growing expat community — and one of the more confusing in practice once money is involved. The bank account rules differ by bank and change often enough that year-old blog posts are unreliable, the tax-residency test isn't the 183-day rule most nomads assume, and the country still runs on cash and bank transfers more than card taps. Here's what actually matters, and where the common assumptions go wrong.

## Who this is for — and who it isn't

**It's for:** remote workers and expats moving to Japan on a work visa, spouse visa, or the digital nomad visa, trying to get banking, tax status, and money transfers set up correctly from the start.

**It's not for:** short tourist-visa stays under 90 days — none of the bank-account or tax-residency mechanics below apply, since a Temporary Visitor status can't open a standard account or trigger tax residency. This also isn't a substitute for advice from a Japan-specific tax accountant (zeirishi) — cross-border tax rules are genuinely complicated and getting them wrong is expensive; what follows is the mechanic worth understanding before that conversation, same approach as [tax residency basics for digital nomads](/blog/tax-residency-basics-for-digital-nomads/).

## The digital nomad visa doesn't work like most people assume

Japan's digital nomad visa (formally "Designated Activities," in effect since April 2024) is narrower than the Portugal- or Spain-style visas it gets compared to. It requires proof of roughly ¥10 million (about $65,000–$70,000) in annual income from non-Japanese employers or clients, private health insurance with at least ¥10 million in treatment coverage, and citizenship in one of the roughly 50 countries with both a tax treaty and visa-waiver arrangement with Japan. It grants a single, generally non-renewable stay of up to six months — reapplying means spending time outside Japan first — and it doesn't come with a residence card, which matters a lot for the banking section below. It also doesn't lead to any path toward permanent residency on its own. It's a real option for someone who wants an extended stay without relocating, not a long-term residency strategy.

## Opening a bank account depends on one document, not how long you've been there

The single hard requirement across every Japanese bank is a **residence card (zairyū card)** showing a registered Japanese address — obtained by filing your address at the local ward office within 14 days of moving in, which also produces a jūminhyō (residence certificate) that most banks ask to see alongside it. Without both, no bank will open an account, full stop — which is exactly why the digital nomad visa (no residence card issued) doesn't pair well with local banking: most digital nomad visa holders can't open a standard Japanese account at all and should plan around Wise or a similar multi-currency account instead, covered in [best banking apps for digital nomads](/blog/best-banking-apps-for-digital-nomads/).

For everyone on a longer visa who does get a residence card, the old advice to expect a flat six-month waiting period is outdated — some megabank branches still apply it informally, but Japan Post Bank, Rakuten Bank, Sony Bank, and Seven Bank generally accept new arrivals with no minimum residency period. A Japanese phone number (postpaid, not prepaid) is required for SMS verification at most banks, and some still expect a registered personal seal (hanko/inkan) rather than a signature, though this is loosening at the online-first banks.

## Cash and bank transfers still run the country

Japan is still meaningfully more cash- and bank-transfer-dependent than Western media coverage of its tech scene suggests. Rent, utilities, and many recurring bills go out as direct bank debits tied to a Japanese account rather than a card — one more reason the account above isn't optional if settling in for more than a few months. IC transit cards (Suica, PASMO) and PayPay cover day-to-day convenience-store and transit spending well, and card acceptance has improved substantially in cities, but carrying cash for smaller shops, some restaurants, and rural areas is still normal, not a backup plan.

## Tax residency isn't the 183-day rule

This is the mistake carried over most often from other countries: Japan doesn't use a simple 183-day test. Classification instead runs off two categories — non-permanent resident and permanent resident, both distinct from immigration residency status — based on domicile (jūsho) and how long a five-year look-back period has been spent in Japan:

- **Non-permanent resident**: generally applies to someone without Japanese nationality who has lived in Japan for five years or less within the past ten years. Taxed on Japan-source income, plus any foreign income actually **remitted into Japan** — foreign income left in an overseas account is generally untaxed.
- **Permanent resident** (tax sense, not the immigration status): applies after living in Japan more than five years within the past ten. Taxed on worldwide income, same as a Japanese national.

A six-month digital nomad visa holder is very likely a non-resident for tax purposes and taxed only on Japan-source income, with tax-treaty relief — not a blanket exemption — usually why foreign income stays untaxed. The practical trap for longer-term non-permanent residents: wiring foreign income into a Japanese account for convenience can trigger tax on it that leaving it offshore wouldn't have.

## Sending money in and out

Standard SWIFT wire transfers into Japan work but are slow and fee-heavy on both ends; a multi-currency service like Wise is usually cheaper and faster for regular transfers, and several — Wise included — can issue Japanese bank details once a Japan-side account exists. Japan does not use IBAN; transfers route on bank code, branch code, and account number instead. Employer-side payroll into a Japanese account is the norm for local hires and the simpler path where it's available; contractors billing overseas clients directly usually route through Wise or a similar service rather than local banking rails.

## A worked example

A remote software contractor on a spouse visa moves to Japan, gets a residence card and jūminhyō within the first two weeks, and opens a Rakuten Bank account online the same week — no six-month wait, since Rakuten doesn't apply one. Client invoices, paid in USD to a Wise account, get converted and sent to the Rakuten account roughly twice a month to cover rent (paid by direct debit) and daily spending. In year one, with under five years of Japan residency in the past decade, this person is a non-permanent resident: US-source client income remitted to Japan is taxed there, and any income deliberately left in a US account is not — which is why keeping a buffer offshore rather than remitting everything immediately is worth planning for from the start, not adjusting after the fact.

## Where people actually go wrong

- **Assuming the digital nomad visa works like a residence visa.** No residence card means no standard bank account for most holders — plan on Wise or similar from day one, not as a fallback.
- **Applying the 183-day rule from other countries.** Japan's non-permanent/permanent split depends on domicile and a five-year look-back, not a day count in the current tax year — a stay well under 183 days can still create tax exposure once it's part of a broader remittance pattern.
- **Remitting everything into Japan out of convenience.** For non-permanent residents, moving foreign income into a Japanese account can convert otherwise-untaxed money into taxed money — worth a conversation with a Japan-side accountant before doing it automatically.
- **Skipping the residence card and jūminhyō step.** Both are needed before any bank will even start an application; getting the ward-office registration done in the first two weeks avoids weeks of delay on everything downstream.

## Getting started

Register the address at the local ward office within 14 days of arrival, get the jūminhyō, and use it plus the residence card to open an account at a bank known to accept new arrivals — Japan Post Bank, Rakuten Bank, or Sony Bank, rather than assuming a megabank branch will. Figure out which tax-residency category applies given time already spent in Japan over the past ten years, and treat any decision to remit foreign income as a deliberate one rather than an automatic transfer.
