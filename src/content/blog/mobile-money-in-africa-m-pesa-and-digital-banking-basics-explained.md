If you've only used banks and cards, mobile money can look like a workaround. In much of sub-Saharan Africa it is the main system. You hold a balance tied to your phone number, you put cash in and take it out at a nearby agent, and you pay friends, shops, and bills from the same wallet. The GSMA's 2026 industry report counted about 2.3 billion registered mobile money accounts worldwide and more than US$2 trillion in transactions in 2025, with sub-Saharan Africa accounting for the largest share. If you're visiting, working remotely, or sending money to family, understanding how it works saves you real hassle.

## Who this is for, and who it isn't

**It's for:** travelers and remote workers spending weeks or months in East or West Africa, and anyone sending money to someone who uses mobile money.

**It's not for:** people looking for investment or business-banking advice, or anyone trying to move large sums. Nothing here is legal, tax, or financial advice, and rules differ by country and change.

## How mobile money actually works

The basic model is simple:

1. **A wallet is linked to a SIM number.** Registration requires ID, and the wallet sits with a mobile network operator or a partner.
2. **Agents are the cash machines.** You hand cash to a nearby agent, who credits your wallet. To withdraw, you do the reverse and pay a fee.
3. **Payments run on codes.** You can send money to another number, pay a shop's till or paybill number, or buy airtime, usually through a phone menu or app. Many wallets work on basic phones through a USSD menu, with no internet needed.

The big brands are M-Pesa (Safaricom in Kenya, Vodacom in Tanzania, and others), MTN MoMo, Airtel Money, and Orange Money. M-Pesa is strongest in East Africa. MTN MoMo and Orange Money lead across much of West and Central Africa. In some countries, one local service dominates; Ethiopia's state-backed telebirr, for example, reported tens of millions of users while M-Pesa there had around 5 million active users by the end of 2025.

## Setting up as a foreign visitor

In Kenya, guides say foreign visitors can register a Safaricom line and M-Pesa with an original passport, and some say a foreigner certificate or similar ID also works. Registration reportedly has to happen at a Safaricom shop or care desk rather than at an ordinary agent, because agents may only be able to register with Kenyan ID. The SIM itself costs very little. Other countries follow a similar pattern but the specifics differ, so ask at the operator's shop on arrival.

Two practical tips. First, set your PIN yourself and never share it, including with hotel staff or "support" callers. Second, register on day one, not when you urgently need to pay for something.

## Limits and fees: what to expect

Published figures for Kenya put the standard M-Pesa caps at KSh 250,000 per transaction, KSh 500,000 per day, and KSh 500,000 as the wallet balance, set under central-bank rules. Some visitor guides describe lower caps for lightly verified accounts, so treat all of these as a ceiling and check at registration. Fees are tiered: sending small amounts is free in Kenya, with a capped charge on larger transfers, and cash withdrawals at agents carry a fee that rises with the amount.

In Ethiopia, the central bank's directive sets daily limits for all providers, and secondary sources list tiered caps by verification level. Again, your personal limit depends on how fully your account is verified, so confirm in the app.

## Don't expect your wallet to travel

This is the most common surprise. A wallet is tied to a country's regulator and its network. Reports say M-Pesa generally works only inside the country where it's registered, with limited cross-border links such as between Kenya, Tanzania, and a few others, and an M-Pesa wallet usually can't send directly to an MTN MoMo wallet. Interoperability is improving in places like Tanzania and Ghana, but if you cross borders, plan to register a new SIM and wallet in each country, or use a remittance service to reach a recipient's wallet.

For holding your own money across currencies, mobile money is not a substitute for a multi-currency account. ## Keep a backup, and watch for scams

Not every business takes mobile money. Reports from Kenya note that upscale lodges and camps often prefer international cards or bank transfers, and rural areas may lack agents or signal. Carry some local cash and one international card.

Fraud is the other risk. Operators regularly warn about people who claim to have sent money by mistake and ask you to "reverse" it, callers posing as customer care who ask for your PIN, and SIM-swap attacks that take over a number. The defenses are dull but effective: never share your PIN or one-time codes, check your balance yourself rather than trusting a message, and report a SIM that suddenly loses service.

## A worked example

A remote worker plans two months in Nairobi. Before leaving, she arranges a Wise account and a no-foreign-fee card. On arrival she buys a Safaricom SIM, registers M-Pesa with her passport at a shop, and sets her own PIN. She tops up about a week's spending at an agent, pays small vendors and rides from the wallet, and uses her card for bigger bills. When she takes a short trip to a neighboring country, she buys a local SIM there rather than expecting her Kenyan wallet to work.

## Where people actually go wrong

- **Waiting until they need it.** Registration takes time, and it's easier at a shop than at a roadside agent.
- **Assuming one wallet works everywhere.** It usually stays inside one country.
- **Trusting a limit number from a blog.** Check in the app or at the shop.
- **Sharing PINs or codes.** This is how most losses happen.

## Getting started

Register your local SIM and wallet early, set your own PIN, load only what you expect to spend, and keep cash and a card as backup.
