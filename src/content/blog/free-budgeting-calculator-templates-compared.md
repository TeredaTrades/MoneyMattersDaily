"Budgeting calculator" and "budgeting template" get used interchangeably, but they're two different tools solving two different problems. A calculator gives you a one-time answer — plug in your income, get a suggested split. A template is something you keep, fill in every month, and watch change over time. Neither is wrong, but grabbing a calculator when what you actually needed was an ongoing system (or vice versa) is why a lot of people try budgeting once and quietly stop. Here's an honest look at the free options in both categories.

## Google Sheets' built-in Monthly Budget — best for starting in the next five minutes

Google Sheets ships with a template gallery, and its Monthly Budget option is the fastest path from zero to a working budget: no download, no account beyond the Google account you already have, opens straight in the browser.

- **Cost:** Free.
- **Good fit for:** Anyone who wants to start today and refine later — it tracks income against expenses by category and produces a basic summary chart automatically.
- **Downside:** Genuinely basic. No savings-rate calculation, no multi-month comparison, no debt or goal tracking — you're extending the formulas yourself once you outgrow it.

## Vertex42 Personal Budget Spreadsheet — most polished free template

Vertex42 has maintained free Excel and Google Sheets templates since the mid-2000s, and it shows — the personal and family budget templates are the most professionally built formulas on this list, with a real annual view layered on top of the monthly one.

- **Cost:** Free.
- **Good fit for:** People who want more structure than Google's default template without moving to a full zero-based system — especially two-income households, since the family version splits income by earner.
- **Downside:** More categories and tabs to understand up front; customizing the category list means editing formulas that reference cell ranges, not just typing over labels.

## Aspire Budgeting — best free zero-based system

Aspire is a complete zero-based budgeting system built entirely in Google Sheets, free to copy, with no app, no account, and no subscription — the closest free equivalent to what YNAB charges for.

- **Cost:** Free.
- **Good fit for:** Anyone who wants to assign every dollar a job (the YNAB method) but doesn't want to pay for it, and is comfortable spending real setup time up front.
- **Downside:** The steepest learning curve here by a wide margin. It's a genuine system, not a fill-in-the-blanks sheet — expect an hour or more the first time, and manual transaction entry ongoing since there's no bank sync.

## Tiller's free Foundation Template — free template, separate from Tiller's paid product

This one causes real confusion: Tiller is best known as a paid service (roughly $79–99/year) that auto-imports bank transactions into a spreadsheet. Less known is that Tiller also hosts a genuinely free, static Foundation Template for Google Sheets on its own site — same spreadsheet-based philosophy, no subscription, but no automatic bank feed either.

- **Cost:** Free (the Foundation Template only — the auto-sync product is paid).
- **Good fit for:** Someone who likes the structure of a Tiller-style budget and is willing to enter transactions manually, or wants to try the format before ever considering the paid, automated version.
- **Downside:** Easy to land on this thinking you're getting Tiller's automation for free. You're not — that's the entire value the paid tier adds.

## NerdWallet's 50/30/20 Budget Calculator — best for a one-time gut check

This isn't a template at all — it's an instant online calculator. Enter your income, and it splits a suggested budget across needs, wants, and savings using the 50/30/20 framework, no spreadsheet or account involved.

- **Cost:** Free.
- **Good fit for:** A quick sanity check on whether your current spending is roughly in line with a common framework, or a starting point before you commit to building out a real template.
- **Downside:** Nothing to save or return to. It answers one question once — it isn't a system you track against next month.

## How to actually choose

The real question isn't which is "best" — it's whether you need an **answer** or a **system**. NerdWallet's calculator (and others like it) answers a question in thirty seconds and then it's done. Everything else here is a system you commit to filling in monthly, and the systems differ mainly in how much structure they hand you versus how much you build yourself: Google's default template hands you almost nothing and asks little in return, Vertex42 hands you more polish for more categories to learn, and Aspire hands you a complete zero-based method in exchange for real setup time.

If you're not sure which category you're in, start with the Google Sheets default or NerdWallet's calculator — both cost nothing but five minutes — and only move to Vertex42 or Aspire once you know you'll actually keep filling a template in. And if a spreadsheet template of any kind stops working for you — most people abandon manual entry within a few months once transaction volume picks up — that's the point where an app with real bank sync, not a fancier free template, is usually the fix; see [Best Budgeting Apps Compared](/blog/best-budgeting-apps-compared/) for that side of the decision.
