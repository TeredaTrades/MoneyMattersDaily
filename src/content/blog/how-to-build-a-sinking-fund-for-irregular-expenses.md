Some bills don't show up every month, and that's what makes them hard. Car insurance might be due twice a year, registration once, the holidays every December, and a dental check-up whenever you finally book it. None of these is a surprise. They're just lumpy, so a budget built around monthly pay keeps getting knocked off course. A sinking fund fixes that by turning one big, irregular bill into a small, regular saving.

## Who this is for, and who it isn't

**It's for:** anyone whose monthly budget looks fine until an annual or seasonal bill lands, and anyone who finds themselves reaching for a credit card for costs they could have seen coming.

**It's not for:** people who still need to build a starter cushion for real emergencies. Build that first, then add sinking funds. If you're behind on essential bills right now, deal with that before setting money aside for future ones. This is general information, not personalized financial advice.

## Sinking fund vs. emergency fund

They're easy to mix up, but they do different jobs.

- **Emergency fund:** for costs you can't predict, such as a sudden job loss or an urgent repair. See [how much should you have in an emergency fund](/blog/how-much-emergency-fund/).
- **Sinking fund:** for costs you know are coming but don't know the exact month-by-month timing of. You can name them, estimate them, and plan for them.

The term comes from corporate finance, where a similar idea is used to set money aside over time to repay a debt. In personal finance it simply means saving a little at a time toward a known future expense.

Keeping the two separate matters. If sinking-fund money and emergency money sit in one lump, the first big bill quietly eats your safety net.

## Step 1: List every irregular expense

Go back through the last 12 months of bank and card statements and pull out anything that didn't happen monthly. Common ones:

- Car insurance, registration, road tax, and servicing
- Home or renters insurance paid annually
- Holiday and birthday gifts, and travel
- Annual subscriptions and memberships
- Medical, dental, and optical visits
- Pet costs, such as vet check-ups
- School fees, supplies, and uniforms
- Replacing things that wear out, like tires, a phone, or a laptop

Also look ahead: is there something coming in the next year that wasn't in the last one?

## Step 2: Put a number on each one

For each item, write down the total you expect to spend in a year. If you're unsure, use last year's amount, and round up a little for price increases. Don't worry about being exact. You can adjust the amount whenever a bill comes in higher or lower.

## Step 3: Divide by the number of pay periods

Take each yearly total and divide it by how many times you save in a year. For monthly saving, that's 12. For paychecks every two weeks, it's 26. This is the amount to put aside each time.

### A worked example

All amounts are in your own currency.

| Irregular expense | Yearly total | Set aside per month |
|---|---|---|
| Car insurance | 720 | 60 |
| Vehicle registration | 120 | 10 |
| Car maintenance | 480 | 40 |
| Holiday gifts | 360 | 30 |
| Annual subscription | 96 | 8 |
| Dental | 240 | 20 |
| Vet | 180 | 15 |
| **Total** | **2,196** | **183** |

Instead of absorbing 720 in one hit for the car insurance, this person sets aside 60 a month, and the money is waiting when the bill comes due. The whole list costs 183 a month, which can go straight into the monthly budget as one line.

### The first-year gap

There's one catch in year one. If a bill is due soon, you haven't had time to save the full amount. Say the car insurance is due in six months. At 60 a month you'd have only 360 of the 720 by then.

Two ways to handle it:

- **Catch up faster for the first cycle.** Divide the bill by the months you have left (720 ÷ 6 = 120 a month), then drop to the normal 60 after it's paid.
- **Use any spare cash or a one-off windfall** to cover the gap, or ask whether the bill can be paid in installments.

Order your list by due date so the nearest bills get funded first.

## Step 4: Decide where the money lives

You have three reasonable options:

1. **One separate savings account** with a simple spreadsheet or note tracking how much belongs to each category.
2. **Several separate accounts or labeled sub-accounts,** if your bank offers them. This is the most visual option.
3. **A budgeting app's envelopes or goals,** which track each category for you. See [the envelope budgeting method](/blog/envelope-budgeting-method/) for the idea behind it.

Wherever you put it, keep it separate from your everyday spending account so it doesn't get absorbed, and consider an account that pays interest. For choosing one, see [high-yield savings accounts explained](/blog/high-yield-savings-accounts-explained/). Money you'll need within the next few years generally doesn't belong in stocks, since a market dip could leave you short right when a bill is due.

## Step 5: Automate it

Set up a recurring transfer on payday for the total amount, so you never have to remember. For options and a setup order, see [best ways to automate your savings](/blog/best-ways-to-automate-your-savings/). Treat the sinking fund contribution like any other bill, not like leftover money.

## Step 6: Spend from it when the bill arrives

When the expense comes, pay it from the sinking fund, not from your regular spending money. Then reduce that category's balance in your tracker. This is the whole point: the bill gets paid and your normal month isn't disrupted.

## Keeping it working over time

- **Review once or twice a year.** Compare what you saved with what you actually spent and adjust each category.
- **If a category runs short,** raise the monthly amount or borrow temporarily from another category, and write down that you need to repay it.
- **If a category runs over,** move the extra to another category, or to savings goals.
- **Refresh the list when life changes,** such as a new car, a pet, a child, or a move.
- **Fit it into your budget.** A sinking fund line works inside any method. See [zero-based budgeting explained](/blog/zero-based-budgeting-explained/) for how every unit of income gets a job, and [how to budget on an irregular income](/blog/how-to-budget-on-an-irregular-income/) if your pay also varies.

## Where people actually go wrong

- **Treating it like the emergency fund.** Keep them separate, or one will drain the other.
- **Forgetting the small ones.** A dozen small annual bills add up to a lot.
- **Not accounting for the first-year gap.** A bill due soon needs a faster catch-up.
- **Leaving the money in the spending account.** It disappears into everyday spending.
- **Never updating the amounts.** Prices change, and so do your needs.
- **Raiding it and not repaying.** A category you borrowed from needs refilling.
- **Making it too complicated.** Start with your five biggest irregular bills and add more later.

## Getting started

Spend 30 minutes with your last year of statements. List the irregular costs, estimate each yearly total, divide by 12, and add them up. Open or label a separate account, set up one automatic transfer for the total, and pay the next big bill from it. If the total feels too high, begin with the three largest bills and add the rest as your budget allows.
