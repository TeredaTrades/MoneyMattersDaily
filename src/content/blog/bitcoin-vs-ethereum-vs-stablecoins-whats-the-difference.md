Bitcoin, Ethereum and stablecoins all get called "crypto," but they are built for different things and fail in different ways. Mixing them up is one of the most common beginner mistakes, because the risks of one do not describe the others. This post explains each in plain language, compares them side by side, and covers what to check before putting any money near them.

It is education only. It does not recommend buying, holding or avoiding any of them, it does not name any coin to pick, and nobody can predict their prices. All three can lose value, and crypto holdings are generally not covered by bank deposit insurance.

## Who this is for, and who it isn't

**It's for:** people who keep hearing these three names and want to know how they differ.

**It isn't for:** anyone looking for a price call or a tip. If you are new to the basics, start with [what is cryptocurrency](/blog/what-is-cryptocurrency-how-it-works-and-the-risks/).

## Bitcoin: a digital money with a fixed supply

According to SoFi's comparison (January 2026), Bitcoin is mainly a digital currency for sending value directly between people. Its supply is limited to 21 million coins, and it uses a system called proof-of-work, in which miners use computing power to validate blocks of transactions.

The key points for a beginner:

- Its price is not set by a company or a government. It moves with buying and selling, and it can swing sharply.
- The fixed supply is a design feature. It does not guarantee any future value.
- It is not designed mainly for running programs on top of it.

## Ethereum: a platform for programs, with its own coin

SoFi describes Ethereum as a blockchain platform for building smart contracts and decentralized apps. A smart contract is code that runs automatically when set conditions are met. Ether is the coin used to pay for activity on the network.

Compared with Bitcoin:

- It has no fixed supply cap, but a portion of transaction fees is permanently removed from circulation, according to SoFi.
- It uses proof-of-stake, in which participants lock up ether to help validate the network, rather than proof-of-work.
- Because other apps and tokens run on it, a problem in an app or a contract can be a risk in itself.

## Stablecoins: tokens designed to hold a price

According to Kiplinger (updated June 2025), stablecoins are cryptocurrencies pegged to a stable asset, such as a fiat currency or gold, so they are meant to be less volatile than bitcoin or ether. They are used for payments, trading, lending and holding value. The article describes four kinds:

- **Fiat-backed:** backed by cash or equivalents, redeemable for the matching amount. This is the most popular type and generally considered the more secure one.
- **Crypto-backed:** backed by other crypto locked in smart contracts, usually over-collateralized. The article's example is about $150 of ether backing $100 of the token.
- **Algorithmic:** no reserves; supply is adjusted by an algorithm to push the price back toward the peg. Kiplinger calls these risky and points to the 2022 collapse of Terra.
- **Commodity-backed:** pegged to something like gold.

The main risks Kiplinger lists are reserve risk (does the issuer really hold enough assets to back the tokens?), technical risk for algorithmic designs, and counterparty risk, since platforms holding your coins can fail, as Celsius and BlockFi did.

### What US law now says about payment stablecoins

The US GENIUS Act, signed on July 18, 2025, sets rules for payment stablecoins. According to The Block's explainer (June 2026, updated August 2026), issuers must back each token one for one with liquid, dollar-denominated reserves, such as cash and short-term Treasuries, keep customer reserves separate from their own funds, let holders redeem each token for $1 on demand, and publish monthly reserve reports. The main provisions take effect on the earlier of 120 days after final rules or January 18, 2027. That source does not say the Act provides deposit insurance, and other sources note that crypto holdings are generally not FDIC or SIPC protected. Other countries have different rules, so check your own country's regulator.

## Side by side

| | Bitcoin | Ethereum (ether) | Stablecoin |
|---|---|---|---|
| Main purpose | Digital money transferred between people | Platform for smart contracts and apps, with its own coin | A token meant to track a price, such as one dollar |
| Supply | Fixed at 21 million | No fixed cap; part of fees removed | Set by the issuer or algorithm |
| How it is secured | Proof-of-work | Proof-of-stake | Depends on the design and the issuer |
| Price behavior | Volatile | Volatile | Meant to be steady, but can slip |
| Main risks | Price swings, scams, lost keys | Price swings, contract bugs, scams, lost keys | Reserve quality, depegging, issuer or platform failure |

## A worked example: when a "stable" coin slips

These numbers are invented. Suppose you hold 1,000 tokens of a dollar-pegged stablecoin, worth **1,000** at the peg of 1.00.

- If a scare pushes the market price to 0.97, your 1,000 tokens are worth 1,000 × 0.97 = **970**, a loss of 30, or 3%, on paper.
- If the issuer cannot meet redemptions and the price falls to 0.60, they are worth **600**.

A steady coin can look safe for months and fail in days. For algorithmic designs, Terra shows how fast that can go. The risk with a fiat-backed coin is whether the reserves exist and can be redeemed, which is exactly why the reserve and redemption rules above matter.

## Which one is "safest"?

That is the wrong question, because they carry different risks. A stablecoin has less day-to-day price movement, but it adds issuer and reserve risk, and it does not grow in value by design. Bitcoin and Ether can move a lot in either direction, with no promise of recovery. Nothing here suggests one is a better choice for you.

## How people use each (without any advice)

- **Bitcoin** is described as a digital currency, but many people simply hold or trade it as an asset.
- **Ether** is needed to pay for activity on Ethereum, and it is also held and traded.
- **Stablecoins** are most often used to move value or to sit in between trades, and in some countries people use dollar-pegged tokens to avoid local currency swings. How that interacts with local rules and bank access differs a lot by country. For wallets that handle several currencies, see [multi-currency wallets compared](/blog/best-multi-currency-wallets-compared-one-account-for-several-currencies/).

## Where people actually go wrong

- **Treating all crypto as the same.** The risks of a stablecoin are not the risks of bitcoin.
- **Assuming "stable" means "safe."** It means designed to track a price, not guaranteed.
- **Chasing yield on stablecoins without asking where the return comes from.** If a return looks far above normal savings, ask what risk pays for it.
- **Leaving large balances on a platform you have not checked.** See [how to store crypto safely](/blog/how-to-store-crypto-safely-wallets-exchanges-and-common-scams/).
- **Following headlines and social media.** Use the checklist in [how to read crypto news without getting burned](/blog/how-to-read-crypto-news-without-getting-burned/).
- **Skipping tax rules.** Taxes on crypto differ by country, and most places expect records of what you buy, sell and send.

## Getting started

1. **Learn what each thing is for** before you decide anything.
2. **Decide a limit you could lose entirely,** and do not borrow to buy.
3. **Check who issues and holds your assets,** and what protection applies where you live.
4. **Use unique passwords and two-factor authentication,** and never share a recovery phrase.
5. **Keep records** for tax purposes.
