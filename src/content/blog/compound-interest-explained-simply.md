Simple interest pays you on what you put in. Compound interest pays you on what you put in *plus* every dollar of interest that's already been added — which means the interest itself starts earning interest. That difference sounds small. Over enough time it isn't.

## The mechanic, without the formula first

Put $1,000 into an account paying 10% a year, simple interest. Year one, you earn $100. Year two, another $100. Year ten, another $100. Ten years, ten $100 payments, $2,000 total.

Now do the same $1,000 at 10%, compounded annually. Year one, you still earn $100 — same as before, because there's no prior interest yet to compound. But year two, the 10% applies to $1,100, not $1,000, so you earn $110. Year three, it applies to $1,210, earning $121. Nothing dramatic is happening yet. By year ten, compounding has grown the account to roughly $2,594 versus simple interest's $2,000 — a $594 gap that didn't exist because of anything you did differently, only because each year's interest became part of the base for the next year's interest.

## The formula, now that the mechanic makes sense

The standard compound interest formula is **A = P(1 + r/n)^(nt)**, where:

- **A** = the amount you end up with
- **P** = principal (what you start with)
- **r** = annual interest rate, as a decimal (7% = 0.07)
- **n** = how many times per year it compounds (12 for monthly, 1 for annually)
- **t** = number of years

**Worked example:** $5,000 invested at a 7% annual return, compounded monthly, for 20 years. That's P = 5000, r = 0.07, n = 12, t = 20. A = 5000 × (1 + 0.07/12)^(12×20) ≈ **$20,200**. The principal is $5,000. The other roughly $15,200 came from growth on growth — not from adding more money.

## Why the compounding frequency barely matters

It's a common assumption that monthly compounding beats annual compounding by a lot. It doesn't, once the rate is fixed. $5,000 at 7% for 20 years compounded annually comes to about $19,349 — compared to $20,200 monthly. The difference is under $1,000 over two decades, because more frequent compounding just breaks the same annual growth into smaller steps; it doesn't add extra growth. **The rate and the time matter far more than the compounding frequency.** Chasing an account that compounds daily instead of monthly for a fraction of a percentage point of difference isn't where the leverage is.

## Where time does almost all the work

**Worked example, same contribution, different start date:** Investor A puts in $200/month starting at age 25 and stops entirely at 35 — ten years of contributions, $24,000 total, then untouched until 65. Investor B waits until 35 to start, puts in $200/month every year until 65 — thirty years of contributions, $72,000 total. At a 7% average annual return, Investor A's account grows to roughly **$252,000** by 65. Investor B's — three times the money put in — grows to roughly **$227,000**. Investor A contributed less and still ends up ahead, purely because those first ten years had thirty extra years to compound instead of twenty.

This isn't an argument that contributing more doesn't matter — Investor B still ends with a substantial amount, and someone who did what Investor A did *and* kept contributing through 65 would end up well ahead of both. It's an argument that starting early is disproportionately powerful, because compounding needs time more than it needs a large initial amount.

## Common mistakes people make with this concept

- **Assuming the growth curve is a straight line.** Compounding graphs look flat for years and then bend upward sharply. Someone judging progress at year three or five, before the curve visibly bends, often concludes it isn't working and stops contributing right before the effect becomes visible.
- **Forgetting that fees compound too.** A 1% annual fee doesn't just cost 1% of the current balance once — it compounds against you the same way returns compound for you, quietly eating a meaningful chunk of total growth over decades. This is the same mechanism, working in reverse.
- **Ignoring that compounding cuts both ways on debt.** Credit card interest compounds too, typically daily. The same formula that grows an investment grows a balance you're carrying, which is part of why high-interest debt is worth clearing before prioritizing additional investing.
- **Treating average return as a guarantee.** The 7% used above is a long-run historical average for a diversified stock portfolio, not a promised annual number — actual years swing well above and below it, and compounding math assumes a smooth rate that real markets don't deliver year to year.

This is also the whole mechanical case for staying invested in something broad and low-cost for decades rather than trying to time entries and exits — see our [index funds explained](/blog/index-funds-explained-for-beginners/) post for what that actually looks like in practice.

## Who this matters most for

Anyone with a multi-decade time horizon — which in practice means anyone investing for retirement, even in their 40s or 50s, since "decades" here means the time until the money is needed, not necessarily an entire working life. It matters least for money needed within the next few years: compounding needs time to work, and short time horizons don't give it enough of that to matter, which is a separate reason (alongside volatility) that near-term savings goals generally don't belong in the stock market at all.
