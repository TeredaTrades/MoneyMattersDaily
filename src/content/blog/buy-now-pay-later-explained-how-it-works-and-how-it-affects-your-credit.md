Buy now, pay later, usually shortened to BNPL, is the "split it into four payments" button at checkout. It looks simple and often costs nothing if you pay on time. It can also quietly add up, and how it shows on your credit report is changing. This post explains how BNPL works, what it can do to your budget and credit, and how to use it without getting caught out.

It is general education, not a recommendation to use or avoid any company. Terms, fees and credit-reporting practices differ by provider and country, and change often, so this post quotes no current fees or interest rates.

## Who this is for, and who it isn't

**It's for:** anyone who has seen the BNPL option at checkout and wants to know what they are agreeing to.

**It isn't for:** people already behind on payments. If you are missing bills, talk to the lender early and look at a plan for [paying down debt](/blog/debt-payoff-calculator-explained/) before taking on anything new.

## How BNPL works

According to PBS NewsHour (June 2025), BNPL lets you buy something now and pay for it over time through a loan from a third-party company, such as Klarna, Afterpay or Affirm. Common plans split the cost into short installments, often every two weeks, while longer plans can run a year or more. Some plans require no credit check or only a soft one.

In practice there are two broad types:

- **Short "pay in four" plans.** You pay a quarter up front and the rest in installments over about six weeks. These are often interest-free if you pay on time.
- **Longer instalment loans.** These run for months and can charge interest. PBS says plans that charge interest can reach rates of up to 36%.

Either way, it is a loan, even when it does not feel like one.

## Where BNPL can go wrong

- **Late fees and interest.** Missing a payment can turn a free plan into a costly one. PBS notes that a regulatory protection against multiple fees for one missed payment is no longer being enforced by the current US Consumer Financial Protection Bureau, as of its June 2025 reporting. Rules change, so check the current terms.
- **Overspending.** One-click checkout makes small purchases easy, and PBS warns that repeated use can build into a deep hole.
- **Stacking.** Several plans at once can add up to a large fixed payment you did not plan for. The CFPB's 2022 data, as reported by PBS, found that about 60% of BNPL users had taken out multiple loans.
- **Spillover.** The same PBS article cites a Federal Reserve study linking BNPL use to more overdraft fees and higher credit card interest and fees.
- **Returns and disputes.** If you return an item, the refund and your installment schedule do not always line up. Keep paying until the lender confirms the plan is cancelled or reduced.

## A worked example: how small plans stack

These numbers are invented. You buy three things on pay-in-four plans: a 240 jacket, a 160 pair of shoes and a 120 gadget.

- Each installment is a quarter: 240 ÷ 4 = **60**, 160 ÷ 4 = **40**, 120 ÷ 4 = **30**.
- Every two weeks you owe 60 + 40 + 30 = **130**.
- Over the six weeks of the plans you pay 4 × 130 = **520**, which is the full price of all three items.

If you are paid every two weeks and take home 1,000, that is 13% of every paycheck committed for six weeks before you pay for anything else. None of the plans looks big, which is the problem.

Now a longer loan, also invented. You finance a 1,200 laptop over 12 months at 20% annual interest. The monthly payment is about **111.16**, and you repay about **1,333.94**, so the financing costs about **133.94**. At 0% the same laptop would cost 100 a month. The lesson is to look at the interest rate and total cost, not just the monthly figure.

## How BNPL affects your credit

This is the part that is changing. According to an Associated Press story (June 2025), BNPL loans usually do not raise credit scores today, but missed payments can lower them, and the three big credit bureaus have not adopted a standard way to report them. FICO announced a scoring model that counts BNPL loans, to be offered to lenders as an option. Affirm began voluntarily reporting its pay-in-four loans to Experian in April 2025. FICO's own study with Affirm, covering over 500,000 borrowers, found that people with five or more loans typically saw their scores rise or stay stable.

But adoption is expected to be slow. A Digital Transactions report (September 2025) says BNPL data may take years to have real impact on lending decisions, because providers are not required to report, some do not report their most popular product, and lenders often keep older score versions. An expert quoted by the AP does not expect a major change for people with established credit, since the average BNPL loan is about 135.

What that means for you:

- **Do not count on BNPL to build credit.** If you want to build credit, a [secured credit card](/blog/secured-credit-cards-explained-how-they-work-and-when-to-graduate/) used well is more established. See also [what makes up a credit score](/blog/what-makes-up-a-credit-score-and-what-doesnt/).
- **A missed payment can hurt.** Treat BNPL due dates like any other bill.
- **Lenders may see more of it over time.** Mortgage and car lenders can look at your debts, including BNPL, when deciding what to offer.
- **Your reports differ by country.** In the UK, for example, the way credit files work is covered in [how UK credit scores work](/blog/how-uk-credit-scores-work-experian-vs-equifax-vs-transunion/). Check how BNPL is treated where you live.

## Is BNPL ever a sensible choice?

It can be, for a planned purchase you could pay for anyway, where you want to smooth the timing and the plan really is interest-free. It is a poor choice for impulse buys, for things you cannot afford, or as a way to avoid looking at your budget.

A few questions help:

1. **Would I buy this if I had to pay for it today?** If not, the plan is the reason you are buying.
2. **Do I know every payment date and amount?** Put them in your calendar.
3. **What happens if I miss one?** Read the late fee and any interest terms first.
4. **Do I already have other plans running?** Add them up first.
5. **Could I save for it instead?** A [sinking fund](/blog/how-to-build-a-sinking-fund-for-irregular-expenses/) for known purchases avoids the lender entirely.

## Where people actually go wrong

- **Treating it as free money.** Interest-free only holds if you pay on time.
- **Stacking plans across several apps** and losing track of the total.
- **Paying installments from a low-balance account,** which can trigger overdraft fees.
- **Using a credit card to pay a BNPL installment.** That moves the cost onto a card that may charge interest or fees.
- **Ignoring returns.** Keep paying until the plan is closed.
- **Forgetting the paper trail.** Save receipts and confirmation emails in case of a dispute, and see [how to dispute an error on your credit report](/blog/how-to-dispute-an-error-on-your-credit-report/) if something appears that you do not recognize.

## Getting started

1. **List every BNPL plan you have,** with the next payment date and amount.
2. **Add the totals** and compare them with your next paycheck.
3. **Set reminders** a day or two before each payment.
4. **Check that your payment account** will have enough money on those dates.
5. **Pause new plans** until the current ones are cleared, if the total feels heavy.
