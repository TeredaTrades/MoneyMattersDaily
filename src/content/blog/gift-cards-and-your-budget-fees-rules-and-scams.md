Gift cards feel like the easiest present and the easiest budget line: you set an amount, hand it over, and you're done. They're also one of the most reliable ways to lose money without noticing, either quietly to fees and forgotten balances, or very loudly to a scammer. This post covers how gift cards work, what the rules say, how to treat them in a budget, and the single rule about gift cards that protects you from most of the damage.

## Who this is for, and who it isn't

**It's for:** anyone who buys, receives, or gets asked about gift cards, especially around the holidays, and wants to know what to check before paying.

**It's not for:** a full holiday budget or a gift-giving plan. For the underlying method, see [how to build a sinking fund for irregular expenses](/blog/how-to-build-a-sinking-fund-for-irregular-expenses/), which handles gifts as a predictable yearly cost. The rules below are US federal rules; other countries and some US states differ, so check your local consumer agency if you live elsewhere. This is general information, not personalized financial advice.

## How gift cards work

A gift card is a stored amount of money you pay for in advance. The details depend on the type:

- **Store or merchant cards.** Redeemable at one merchant or a group of affiliated ones. Federal rules call a fixed-amount version a gift certificate and a reloadable version a store gift card.
- **General-use prepaid cards,** often branded with a payment network. These are redeemable at many unaffiliated merchants and sometimes at ATMs. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB) notes that network-branded cards work wherever that network is accepted.
- **Digital gift cards.** Electronic codes. The federal rule says coverage isn't limited to plastic, so electronic codes that entitle you to redeem a specified amount are generally covered.
- **Loyalty, reward, and promotional cards.** Issued through a rewards or promotional program. These are treated differently: they must be labeled "Reward" or "Promotional" and carry certain disclosures, but several of the consumer protections below don't fully apply.

One more category trips people up: a reloadable prepaid card that isn't marketed or labeled as a gift card is generally outside the federal gift card rule. The CFPB notes that these can charge fees such as monthly service fees right away. [Best prepaid cards compared](/blog/best-prepaid-cards-compared-fees-limits-and-who-they-suit/) covers how to weigh those fees.

## What the US rules say

The main federal rule is Regulation E, section 1005.20, published by the CFPB. It applies to products sold on or after August 22, 2010. In plain terms:

- **Expiration.** Funds on a covered card must last at least five years from when the card was issued or last loaded. If a card carries an expiration date, a consumer must have a reasonable opportunity to buy one with at least five years remaining. A card can display an earlier expiration date than its funds, but then it has to say so, and you're entitled to a replacement for the remaining balance.
- **Replacement.** A replacement or remaining-balance card may not carry a fee unless the card was lost or stolen.
- **Inactivity and service fees.** These can only be charged if there's been no activity for the one-year period ending on the day the fee is charged. Only one such fee is allowed per calendar month, fees can't be stacked into one later charge, and the amount and frequency must be stated clearly on the card.
- **Disclosures.** Fee and expiration terms must be disclosed before purchase and can't change afterward. Required disclosures must appear on the card or code itself, not only on packaging, and the card must show a toll-free number (and a website if one exists) for fee information.
- **Exclusions.** The rule doesn't cover cards used only for telephone services, cards not marketed to the general public, paper-only gift certificates, cards redeemable only for event admission, and reloadable cards not marketed as gift cards, among others.

Some states add protections beyond the federal rule. The CFPB's own older guidance says as much and points readers to state law.

## The fees that still exist

The rule limits inactivity fees, but it doesn't make cards free. Watch for:

- **One-time fees.** The rule's guidance says one-time fees such as issuance or replacement fees are not "service fees," so a purchase or activation fee on a general-use card can be legal. Check the rack or the checkout screen before you pay.
- **Inactivity fees after a year.** After 12 months with no activity, a fee may start to reduce the balance.
- **Reloadable or non-gift prepaid cards,** which can charge fees from day one.
- **A retailer or issuer going out of business.** The CFPB cautions that a closure could leave a card unredeemable.

### A worked example

All amounts below are made up, to show scale. Say a 50 general-use card has a 5 purchase fee. You pay 55 to give 50 of spending power, so the fee adds about 10% on top of the card's value. Now say the recipient never uses it and the card charges 2 a month after a year of inactivity. After another year of sitting in a drawer, 24 is gone, nearly half the card. The rule limits how and when that fee is charged, but it doesn't prevent it.

## Gift cards in your budget

A gift card is cash with restrictions, so treat it like cash.

- **If you give one,** count the full price, including any purchase fee, as spending in your gift category. If gifts are a yearly cost, a sinking fund keeps them from landing on a credit card.
- **If you receive one,** use it soon, and for something you would have bought anyway, such as groceries, gas, or a household item. That turns it into real cash-flow relief instead of a treat that slips through the cracks. See [the envelope budgeting method](/blog/envelope-budgeting-method/) for a way to track small pots like this.
- **Keep a list.** Write down each card's issuer, balance, and any expiration or fee date. The CFPB suggests recording the card number, security code, and customer service number somewhere safe, and keeping the receipt, since some merchants refund to the card.
- **Use partial balances up.** Odd leftover amounts are the cards that get forgotten, so spend them first.
- **Don't count a gift card as savings.** It can only be spent at certain places, and it can lose value to fees or a closing store.
- **Lost cards may not be replaced.** The CFPB notes that some issuers, such as stores, may not replace a lost or stolen card, and others only if it was registered beforehand.

## The rule that protects you: gift cards are for gifts

The Federal Trade Commission (FTC) puts it simply: gift cards are for gifts, not payments. **Anyone who demands payment by gift card is a scammer.** That's not a figure of speech. Real businesses and government agencies don't take payment in gift cards.

Why scammers like them, per the FTC: gift cards are quick to collect and hard to reverse. A gift card number and PIN let them take the money even if you're still holding the physical card.

### The stories scammers use

The FTC lists these common setups:

- **Government impersonation.** A caller claims to be from the IRS, Social Security, or the FTC and demands taxes or fines paid by gift card.
- **Tech support.** Someone says your computer has a problem and asks for remote access and payment.
- **A family emergency.** A caller poses as a relative or friend in trouble, sometimes with a cloned voice, and asks you to keep it secret.
- **Prizes and sweepstakes.** You "won," but must pay fees first.
- **Utility threats.** Pay now or your service is cut off.
- **Romance scams.** Someone you met online asks for gift cards.
- **Fake check overpayment.** You're told to deposit a check and send back the difference on a gift card. The check is fake.

### Warning signs

- Pressure to pay right away.
- Instructions on which card to buy and where, sometimes across several stores.
- A caller who stays on the line while you load money onto a card.
- A request for the card number, the PIN, or a photo of the card.

If you're in a checkout line buying gift cards while someone on the phone tells you what to do, stop. The FTC says you may be being scammed.

### If you already paid with a gift card

The FTC's guidance (published January 2026) says to act fast:

1. **Contact the gift card company right away,** even if it happened some time ago. Have the card and the store receipt ready.
2. **Ask for your money back.** Some companies may refund you, and the FTC says Apple and Google can freeze a card if funds remain on it.
3. **Report it to the FTC** at ReportFraud.ftc.gov.

The FTC's page lists contact numbers and reporting options for many major card brands. If yours isn't listed, check the back of the card or the issuer's website.

## Buying gift cards safely

- **Inspect the card.** The FTC says to check that any protective stickers are in place and the PIN isn't exposed. If a card looks scratched or damaged, don't buy it, and report it.
- **Buy from sources you trust.** The FTC says to think twice about online auction sites, where cards may be fake or stolen. A deep discount on a card is a reason for suspicion.
- **Keep the receipt,** and a photo of the card.
- **Read the terms before paying,** especially for fees charged on each use or when the card sits unused.
- **Report a lost or stolen card to the issuer right away.**

## Common mistakes

- **Paying anyone with a gift card.** If someone asks, it's a scam.
- **Sharing the card number or PIN with a caller.** That's the same as handing over the cash.
- **Ignoring the terms on the card.** Purchase fees and inactivity fees are right there.
- **Buying discounted cards from unknown sellers.** They can be fake or stolen.
- **Letting a balance sit.** Forgotten cards lose value to fees or to a closed store.
- **Not counting the cost in a budget.** A gift card is spending, even if it feels like a favor.
- **Assuming every prepaid card is a gift card.** Reloadable cards not sold as gifts fall outside the rule.

## Getting started

Do three things this week. Gather every gift card you have, note each balance and any fee or expiration terms, and plan to use the oldest first. Before you buy cards as gifts, read the terms printed on the card and add any purchase fee to your gift category. And tell anyone in your family who might be targeted that a request to pay with a gift card means stop, hang up, and call someone you trust.
