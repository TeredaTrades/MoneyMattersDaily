A subscription is cheap to start and easy to forget. A trial that quietly became paid, a streaming service nobody has opened since spring, an app that renews every year: each one is small, and together they can take a real slice of a month's budget. The good news is that finding them takes about an hour, and cancelling them is one of the few budget changes that costs you nothing you were using.

This guide is general. The tools and legal details below are US-focused where noted, so check your own country's consumer agency for local rules.

## Who this is for, and who it isn't

**It's for:** anyone who suspects they are paying for things they do not use, or who has never listed their recurring charges in one place.

**It isn't** a reason to cancel everything. Plenty of subscriptions are worth their price. The aim is to pay for them on purpose.

## How big can it be?

Chime's blog (February 2026) cites a CNET 2025 survey finding that consumers spend nearly $200 a year on unused subscriptions. That is a company's summary of a survey, so treat it as a rough scale, not a rule. Chime's own example is simpler: three unused subscriptions at $10 a month cost $360 a year.

## Step 1: Find every charge

Use several routes, because no single one catches everything:

- **Review 12 months of bank and card statements** and mark every recurring charge, including price increases (Chime, AARP).
- **Search your email** for words like "subscription," "membership," "renewal," "free trial" and "receipt" (AARP, Chime). This also finds charges paid through a service like PayPal.
- **Check your phone's app subscriptions.** AARP (October 2026) gives these paths. On iPhone: Settings, tap your name, then Subscriptions. On Android: open Google Play, tap your profile icon, then Payments & subscriptions, then Subscriptions.
- **Look for bundles and duplicates,** such as streaming channels bought through another service, or a service your phone or internet plan already includes (AARP).
- **Optionally, use a tracker.** Chime lists apps such as Rocket Money and TrackMySubs. AARP notes some charge fees or show ads, and that you should check their privacy settings before linking an account. A spreadsheet works without sharing your bank data.

## Step 2: List them all in one place

Write each one down with its name, price, how often it bills, the renewal date and how you pay. Seeing the total per month, and per year, is often the motivation. See [free budgeting calculator templates compared](/blog/free-budgeting-calculator-templates-compared/) for simple options.

## Step 3: Decide keep, cut or pause

Ask of each one:

- **Did I use it in the last 30 to 60 days?** Chime suggests testing whether you still log in.
- **Does something else I pay for overlap with it?**
- **Is there a cheaper plan, a yearly discount or a free version?**
- **Would I sign up again today at this price?**

If you are unsure, cut it. Most services let you rejoin later.

## Step 4: Cancel properly, and keep proof

The US Federal Trade Commission's advice is to follow the company's cancellation instructions and keep a copy of your request, write notes about any conversations, and check your statements for charges after you cancel. Keep the service until the billing period ends if it lets you, and watch statements for a month or two (Chime). If you cannot cancel online, call customer service (AARP).

## Step 5: If you are still charged

The FTC says to file a **chargeback** (dispute) with your card issuer right away, either in your online account or by phone using the number on the back of the card, and to follow up in writing. It also says to report problems at ReportFraud.ftc.gov and to your state attorney general (the FTC page is dated September 2024 with a December 2025 update). Outside the US, your card issuer and national consumer agency are the starting points.

## Step 6: Guard against the next one

- **Free trials:** put the cancellation deadline in your calendar the day you sign up. The FTC says to read the terms, including how and when to cancel, and to uncheck any pre-ticked boxes.
- **Be careful with renewal notices.** The FTC says not to call the number or click the link in a notice you did not expect. Contact the company using a number you already know is real.
- **Use a separate card or virtual card for trials,** if your bank offers one. That is a general tip and not from these sources.
- **Re-run the audit** every six months. Put it on your calendar.

## A worked example

This is invented. Someone lists eight subscriptions:

| Service | Per month |
|---|---|
| Video streaming | 12 |
| Second streaming service | 9 |
| Music | 11 |
| Cloud storage | 3 |
| News | 15 |
| Fitness app | 10 |
| Language app | 8 |
| Photo editing | 6 |
| **Total** | **74** |

That is 74 × 12 = **888 a year**. They have not opened the second streaming service, the language app or the photo editor in months: 9 + 8 + 6 = 23 a month, or 23 × 12 = **276 a year**. Cancelling those three leaves 51 a month. They could put that 276 into a [sinking fund](/blog/how-to-build-a-sinking-fund-for-irregular-expenses/), an emergency fund or debt payments.

## Common mistakes

- **Cancelling without proof.** Keep the confirmation, in case it keeps billing.
- **Checking only your main card.** Subscriptions hide on a second card, in app stores and on PayPal.
- **Missing the annual ones.** A yearly renewal is easy to overlook, so check 12 months of statements.
- **Ignoring price rises.** A plan that went up since you joined may no longer be worth it.
- **Cancelling, then re-subscribing impulsively.** If you rejoin, set a reminder to re-check in a month.

## Getting started

1. **Pull 12 months of statements** and mark the recurring charges.
2. **Check your phone's subscription lists and your inbox.**
3. **Make one list** with price, billing date and payment method.
4. **Mark each one keep, cut or pause,** and cancel, keeping a record.
5. **Set a calendar reminder** for the next audit, and for any free-trial deadline.

You can also feed the savings into an automatic transfer. See [best ways to automate your savings](/blog/best-ways-to-automate-your-savings/).
