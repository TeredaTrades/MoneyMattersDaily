"Free" is table stakes now — Fidelity, Robinhood, Schwab, and most competitors all charge $0 commission on stock and ETF trades, so that alone doesn't separate them anymore. The real differences are in account minimums, what happens to money you haven't invested yet, how much hand-holding the app gives you, and what it's quietly trying to upsell you into. Pricing and features below are approximate and change; check each app's current site before opening an account.

## Fidelity — best overall for beginners

Fidelity pairs $0 commissions with genuinely deep educational content and 24/7 phone support, which is unusual among mobile-first competitors. Fractional shares start at $1, so you can own a slice of an expensive stock without needing hundreds of dollars up front.

- **Cost:** $0 commissions on stocks/ETFs, no account minimum, no account maintenance fees.
- **Good fit for:** Beginners who want one app they won't need to outgrow — it scales from a first brokerage account up through retirement accounts without switching platforms later.
- **Downside:** The interface is more utilitarian than flashy. If you want a slick, minimal design, this isn't the draw — the substance is.

## Robinhood — best for a simple, mobile-first start

Robinhood popularized commission-free trading and still has the cleanest, fastest mobile experience of the mainstream apps. Fractional shares start at $1, and there's no account minimum.

- **Cost:** $0 commissions on stocks, ETFs, options, and crypto. Robinhood Gold (roughly $5/month) adds research access and a match on IRA contributions.
- **Good fit for:** Someone who wants to start investing today with a small amount and doesn't want to wade through a complex platform to do it.
- **Downside:** The app is also built to keep you engaged — it surfaces options and crypto trading to users who don't need either. The educational content is thinner than Fidelity's or Schwab's, so it's a fine place to buy an index fund and a riskier place to learn investing from scratch.

## Charles Schwab — best for wanting more tools later

Schwab offers the same $0-commission, $0-minimum basics as Fidelity and Robinhood, but backs it with thinkorswim — a genuinely advanced trading platform most beginners won't touch on day one but might grow into.

- **Cost:** $0 commissions on stocks/ETFs, no account minimum.
- **Good fit for:** Someone who wants a beginner-friendly starting point without a ceiling — you can open a basic account now and use the advanced tools later without moving your money elsewhere.
- **Downside:** thinkorswim's depth is wasted on a first-time investor; you're paying no extra cost for it, but it can also feel like more app than you need at the start.

## Acorns — best for not having to think about it

Acorns' core feature is round-ups: it rounds your everyday purchases up to the nearest dollar and invests the difference automatically. There's no active decision-making required after setup.

- **Cost:** Roughly $3–$12/month depending on tier (Acorns doesn't use a free tier the way the brokerages above do — this is its actual cost model).
- **Good fit for:** Someone who has tried "I'll invest manually" and it hasn't stuck. The habit is the product here, not low fees.
- **Downside:** That flat monthly fee is a meaningfully higher percentage cost on a small balance than a $0-commission brokerage. On a $50 balance, a $3/month fee is over 70% a year — Acorns makes far more sense once your invested balance is large enough that the flat fee is a rounding error, not before.

## SoFi Invest — best for hands-off beginners already using SoFi

SoFi Invest bundles commission-free trading with SoFi's banking products and free access to human financial planners — a combination aimed at people who'd rather have someone else build the plan.

- **Cost:** $0 commissions on stocks/ETFs, no account minimum.
- **Good fit for:** Existing SoFi banking customers who want investing in the same app, or beginners who specifically want a free planning conversation rather than researching allocation themselves.
- **Downside:** Research tools and fund selection are narrower than Fidelity's or Schwab's. It's a reasonable place to start; less of a reason to stay once you outgrow "just get me invested."

## How to actually choose

Ask what you're optimizing for first: **learning, convenience, or being left alone.** Fidelity and Schwab are the pick if you want to learn and won't need to switch platforms as you grow. Robinhood and SoFi are the pick if you just want the simplest possible path to your first trade. Acorns is the pick if the honest answer is that you won't do this manually — but treat its flat fee as a small-balance tax and expect to graduate off it once your invested amount grows.

One thing that doesn't change across any of these: all are SIPC-member brokerages, meaning your securities are protected up to $500,000 (including $250,000 in cash) if the brokerage itself fails. That protects against the company going under — it does not protect against your investments losing value, which is a normal and expected part of investing, not a flaw in the app you picked.
