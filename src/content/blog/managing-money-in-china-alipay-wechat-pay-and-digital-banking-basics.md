For years, China was the place where a foreign credit card quietly stopped working. Everyday spending moved onto QR codes inside Alipay and WeChat Pay, and both apps used to expect a Chinese bank account behind them. That has changed: both now let you link an international card, so a visitor or short-stay remote worker can pay the way locals do without opening a Chinese account first. The catch is that "it works" hides a few details about verification, limits, fees, and fallbacks that are worth sorting out before you land.

## Who this is for — and who it isn't

**It's for:** travelers, students on short programs, and remote workers spending weeks or months in mainland China who want to know what to set up before arrival and what to carry as backup.

**It's not for:** people relocating long-term who need salary banking, residency paperwork, or tax planning. Those depend on your visa category and are outside the scope here, and nothing below is legal or tax advice. For the tax side of staying abroad, see [tax residency basics for digital nomads](/blog/tax-residency-basics-for-digital-nomads/).

## What changed, and why it matters

In March 2024, China's central bank said it would guide major payment platforms such as Alipay and Tenpay (the operator of WeChat Pay) to raise the limit for foreign users from US$1,000 to US$5,000 per transaction, and the annual cap from US$10,000 to US$50,000. Officials framed it as fixing payment problems for international visitors who relied on bank cards or cash in a country where mobile payment is the default. The same push said cash and bank cards should keep working as fallbacks, with penalties for refusing them.

Those limits apply to users who have verified their identity with a passport. Reports at the time put the allowance for unverified foreign users much lower, around US$2,000 a year on Alipay, so verification is worth doing before you need it.

## Setting up Alipay or WeChat Pay with a foreign card

The general flow is the same for both apps:

1. Install the app and register with your home phone number.
2. Verify your identity with your passport. Do this at home on reliable Wi-Fi; travelers regularly report SMS and verification steps timing out on spotty airport connections.
3. Add an international Visa, Mastercard, or other supported card. Support for American Express, Discover, and Diners Club is uneven between the two apps, so a Visa or Mastercard is the safest default.
4. Test it on a small purchase before you rely on it.

Setting up **both** apps is cheap insurance. Some merchants display only one QR code, and a card that one app rejects sometimes goes through the other.

## Fees and limits: where sources disagree

The US$5,000 per payment and US$50,000 per year figures come from the central bank's announcement and are the ceiling to expect. The fee picture is less settled. Many guides say foreign-card payments of ¥200 or less carry no platform fee while larger ones add roughly 3%, but some recent sources describe fee waivers, and rules differ by app and over time. Treat any single blog's number, including this one, as a starting point and check the terms shown inside the app before a big payment.

Two things do not depend on the fee rules. Your own card issuer may still charge a foreign-transaction fee, so a card with no such fee is the best one to link. And if a 3% fee does apply, it adds up quickly: a ¥600 dinner would add about ¥18, which is why many travelers keep larger purchases for a card or cash and use the apps for small, frequent spending.

Reports also say that foreign-card accounts generally can't send money to other people or to Chinese bank accounts, which means you can pay merchants but may not be able to split a bill by transfer the way locals do.

## Cash and cards are still your backup

Don't treat the apps as your only plan. Government guidance on payments for overseas visitors says foreign and domestic bank cards should be accepted at three-star-and-above tourist hotels and at higher-rated tourist attractions, and that cash must remain usable. In practice, small vendors and rural areas lean hardest on QR codes, and a phone with a dead battery or a failed verification can leave you stranded.

A sensible backup kit is a small amount of cash in yuan, one international card that works at a bank ATM, and a second card kept separately from your phone. Withdraw at large bank ATMs rather than standalone machines, and expect your own bank's fees and daily withdrawal limits to apply.

## When a local bank account is worth it

A Chinese bank account unlocks the full version of Alipay and WeChat Pay, but it is not a casual errand. Guides generally describe an in-person branch visit, a passport, and a local phone number, and many say a long-stay visa or residence permit is needed, though requirements vary by bank and branch and are applied inconsistently. If you're staying a few weeks, a linked foreign card is almost always the better trade. If you're staying many months and need things like local bill payments or P2P transfers, the account starts to make sense, and it's worth asking your specific bank what it requires before you show up.

## A worked example

A remote worker plans four weeks in Shanghai. Two weeks before leaving, they install Alipay and WeChat Pay at home, verify with their passport, and link a Visa card with no foreign-transaction fee. They test both with a small purchase on arrival day. Daily life runs on Alipay: metro, convenience stores, a few ride-hailing trips. For a ¥1,800 hotel deposit they use the physical card instead of the app to avoid any percentage fee. They keep ¥500 in cash and a second card in the hotel safe. Over the month they never need a local account.

## Where people actually go wrong

- **Setting up on arrival.** Verification is smoother at home with your own phone service.
- **Relying on one app or one card.** A single failed link or dead battery shouldn't leave you unable to pay.
- **Assuming the fee numbers they read are current.** Check in-app terms before large payments.
- **Skipping cash entirely.** Cards and cash are meant to remain accepted, but acceptance is uneven in small places.

## Getting started

Install and verify both apps at home, link a no-foreign-fee card, and carry a physical card and some yuan as backup. For general habits that apply in any country, see [how to manage money while traveling abroad](/blog/how-to-manage-money-while-traveling-abroad/).
