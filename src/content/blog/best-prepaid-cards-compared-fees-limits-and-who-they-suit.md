A prepaid card looks like a debit card, but it works differently. You load money onto it first and spend only what's there. It isn't linked to a checking account, so there is no overdraft to fall into and nothing to link to your bank. For people who want to cap their spending, can't open a bank account, or want a card for a single purpose, that's useful.

The catch is that prepaid cards are priced in a way that's easy to misjudge. Two cards can look alike on the shelf and cost very different amounts depending on how you use them. This post doesn't crown a winner, because the honest answer is that "best" depends on your pattern. It compares the main types, the fees that drive the cost, what protections apply, and who each kind suits.

## Who this is for, and who it isn't

**It's for:** people choosing between prepaid cards, people who get their pay or benefits on a card and want to understand it, and anyone who wants a spending-capped card for a teenager, a trip or a budget category.

**It isn't a product ranking.** Fees, limits and terms change often and differ by provider and country, so no current prices are quoted here. The goal is to teach you what to compare so you can read any card's fee chart yourself. For gift cards specifically, see [gift cards and your budget](/blog/gift-cards-and-your-budget-fees-rules-and-scams/), which covers a different set of rules.

## How a prepaid card works

You put money on the card (a reload), and you spend from that balance. The US Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB) points out that a prepaid card isn't linked to a checking or share draft account and that you generally can't spend more than you've loaded.

A few terms help:

- **Open-loop** cards carry a payment network logo and work anywhere that network is accepted. **Closed-loop** cards work only at certain places, like a single store.
- **Reloadable** cards accept more money after the first load. These are often called general-purpose reloadable (GPR) cards.
- **Registered** cards are tied to your identity. More on why that matters below.

## The main types, side by side

| Type | What it's for | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|
| **General-purpose reloadable** | Everyday spending and bills if you don't use a bank account | Monthly and per-use fees, reload fees |
| **Payroll card** | Receiving your pay without a bank account | Fees to withdraw cash or check balances, depending on the plan |
| **Government benefit card** | Receiving benefits | Rules depend on the program; check the card's own terms |
| **Travel or multi-currency prepaid** | Holding or spending foreign currency | Currency conversion costs and whether your country offers the card |
| **Crypto-funded card** | Spending from a crypto balance | Which parts are regulated and protected; see below |
| **Gift card** | A one-time gift | Usually outside the protections that apply to the other types |

The CFPB notes that payroll and government benefit cards are often open-loop, and that gift cards generally lack the protections of its prepaid rule.

## The fees that decide the cost

The CFPB lists the fee types prepaid cards may charge, and the amounts vary by card and by how you use it:

- Activation, monthly and per-purchase fees
- ATM withdrawal fees, which can differ in-network and out-of-network
- Balance inquiry and cash reload fees
- Paper statement and decline fees
- Inactivity fees and card-to-card transfer fees
- Bill payment, replacement-card and additional-card fees
- Foreign transaction fees and card cancellation fees

Not every card charges every fee. The CFPB says some cards have a monthly fee but no transaction fees, while others do the reverse. That's why the right question isn't "which card is cheapest?" but "which card is cheapest for the way I'll use it?"

### A worked example

These numbers are made up to show the logic, not to describe a real card.

- **Card A** charges $5 a month and nothing per purchase.
- **Card B** charges $0 a month and $1 per purchase.

| Purchases per month | Card A (monthly) | Card B (per purchase) |
|---|---|---|
| 3 | $5 | $3 |
| 5 | $5 | $5 |
| 20 | $5 | $20 |
| 40 | $5 | $40 |

At five purchases a month, the two cost the same. Below that, the per-purchase card is cheaper, and above it, the monthly-fee card wins by more every time. Over a year, Card A costs $60 however much you use it, while Card B costs $36 at three purchases a month and $240 at twenty. Add a $2 ATM fee and a $3 reload fee to the mix and the answer can flip again, which is why a quick count of your own habits beats any ranking.

## Read the fee box before you buy

Under the CFPB's prepaid rule, cards sold in stores and online must show key fees before you acquire them. The CFPB says store packaging carries a chart of key fees and points to a website with more detail. A 2016 summary of the final rule by the law firm Orrick says the short form lists the fixed fees (such as monthly, purchase, ATM, reload and inactivity fees) plus the two additional fee types that earned the issuer the most revenue, and that a long form lists all possible fees and the conditions for them. The CFPB's own page adds that the new packaging is being phased in, so some cards in stores may not show it yet.

If you're buying online, you should be able to see both before you pay. If you can't find a fee schedule, treat that as a warning sign.

## Registration and insurance: the part people skip

Two things are worth checking before you load much money.

**Register the card.** The CFPB says registration gives you more protection if the card is lost or stolen and is required for FDIC or NCUA insurance where it's offered. Some providers limit use until you register.

**Look for the insurance statement.** The CFPB says money on some prepaid cards can be FDIC-insured if the issuing bank fails, and that the fee disclosure should contain the sentence "Your funds are eligible for FDIC insurance." Funds are usually held in a pooled account at the issuing bank rather than in an individual account in your name, and the pool only qualifies if the bank has records identifying you, which usually means registering the card. If you can't find that sentence, don't assume the money is insured.

**Know which protections apply.** The CFPB says registered cards, payroll cards and certain government benefit cards have federal error-resolution rights, and that network-branded cards often add their own loss or theft protection, which varies by provider. The Orrick summary says the Regulation E protections for limited liability and error resolution apply to prepaid accounts, with details such as provisional credit differing for accounts the issuer hasn't verified. If a card is lost or you see a charge you didn't make, contact the issuer immediately.

## Prepaid and credit

A plain prepaid card is a payment tool, not a credit product. Because it only spends what you've loaded, it isn't the way to build a credit history. For that, see [secured credit cards explained](/blog/secured-credit-cards-explained-how-they-work-and-when-to-graduate/) and [how to build credit from scratch](/blog/how-to-build-credit-from-scratch/).

The Orrick summary also notes that the rule effectively limits overdraft on prepaid accounts. Any credit feature must be structured as a separate credit product with its own protections, including a waiting period before it can be offered, rather than a negative balance on the card. If a prepaid card offers a "credit" or "advance" feature, read it as a separate loan and compare the cost accordingly.

## Beyond the card rack: other options worth knowing

The cards in a US store aren't the whole picture, and for many readers they aren't the main one.

- **Multi-currency and travel accounts.** If you spend in more than one currency, a multi-currency account or a travel card may suit you better than a single-currency prepaid card. These come with their own fee structures, covered in [multi-currency wallets compared](/blog/best-multi-currency-wallets-compared-one-account-for-several-currencies/). If you're using a card abroad, also read [dynamic currency conversion](/blog/dynamic-currency-conversion-why-to-decline-it-and-how-to-avoid-it/) and [how to use ATMs abroad](/blog/how-to-use-atms-abroad-without-overpaying-in-fees/).
- **Local mobile-money wallets.** In many countries the role of a prepaid card is played by a mobile wallet instead. [Mobile money in Africa](/blog/mobile-money-in-africa-m-pesa-and-digital-banking-basics-explained/) explains how that works.
- **Cards funded from crypto.** Some products let you spend from a crypto balance through a card. These mix a regulated card with an asset whose value can swing and whose protections are usually different. Before you use one, find out who issues the card, what happens to your balance if the provider fails, and when the crypto is converted. Nothing in this post is a recommendation of any such product. For the risks, see [what is cryptocurrency](/blog/what-is-cryptocurrency-how-it-works-and-the-risks/).
- **A basic bank account.** The CFPB itself notes that a bank account may suit your needs better than a prepaid card, depending on how you plan to use it. If you can open one cheaply, compare it with the card before choosing; [best banking apps for digital nomads](/blog/best-banking-apps-for-digital-nomads/) is one place to start for mobile-first options.

## Who each kind suits

- **You want a hard spending cap** (a teenager's allowance, a vacation budget, a category you overspend): a reloadable prepaid card with a low or no monthly fee and few per-use fees.
- **You get paid on a card:** read the payroll card's fee list, especially cash withdrawals, and ask whether you can move your pay to an account elsewhere.
- **You spend rarely, but regularly:** a card with no monthly fee and low per-purchase fees may cost less than one with a flat monthly charge.
- **You spend often:** a flat monthly fee can cost less than paying per purchase. Do the count from the worked example above.
- **You spend abroad or in several currencies:** a multi-currency option is worth comparing before you load a single-currency card.
- **You want to build credit:** a prepaid card won't do that. Look at a secured card instead.

## Common mistakes

- **Choosing by the headline fee.** The monthly fee is only one line on the chart.
- **Not registering.** You can lose protection and insurance.
- **Assuming it's insured.** Look for the FDIC statement in the disclosure.
- **Treating a gift card like a reloadable one.** The rules differ.
- **Loading more than you need.** Keep the balance small and reload as you go, especially if the card is easy to lose.
- **Ignoring cash fees.** ATM, reload and withdrawal charges often cost more than the monthly fee.

## Getting started

1. **Write down how you'll use it:** how often, how much, where, whether you need cash.
2. **Find the fee chart** before you buy and add up your own usage.
3. **Check the insurance statement** and register as soon as you can.
4. **Set up balance and transaction alerts** if the provider offers them.
5. **Reassess after a few months.** If the card costs more than you expected, switch or compare it with a basic account.
