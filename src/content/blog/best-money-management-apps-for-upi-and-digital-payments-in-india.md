In India, "money management on UPI" is really two jobs, and no single app does both well. One job is paying: PhonePe, Google Pay, and Paytm handle that, and the best one is mostly whichever your friends, shops, and bank already work smoothly with. The other job is understanding where the money went, and that is where a separate tracker earns its place: Money View, Axio (formerly Walnut), or ET Money if you want hands-off tracking on Android, or Jupiter if you're willing to bank with a neobank. Pick one app from each job instead of hunting for a single winner.

## Why one app can't do both jobs

Every UPI payment is a bank-to-bank transfer. The app you tap is just the front door; your bank account is where the money, the limits, and the SMS confirmation all live. That has two practical consequences:

- **Limits belong to your bank account, not the app.** NPCI's standard ceiling for everyday person-to-person and merchant payments is ₹1 lakh per day, with a commonly applied cap of around 20 transactions in 24 hours, and individual banks can set lower limits (some sit anywhere from ₹25,000 to ₹1 lakh). Installing a second UPI app linked to the same bank account does not give you a second allowance.
- **Every UPI debit generates a bank SMS no matter which app you used.** That's why a tracker can see your PhonePe, Google Pay, and Paytm spending together without being connected to any of them.

The second point is the whole basis of the "pay here, track there" setup below.

## The payment apps compared

NPCI data compiled by Inc42 put August 2026 volume shares at roughly 45.9% for PhonePe, 32.4% for Google Pay, and 8.1% for Paytm, so these three cover the vast majority of UPI payments. For a consumer, the differences are small, and all three are free for ordinary UPI transfers and merchant payments.

**PhonePe — the default if you want the widest acceptance.** The largest by volume, which in practice means nearly every merchant QR code and every friend you'll need to pay works with it. Beyond payments it bundles bill payments, recharges, mutual funds, and insurance. The fees to watch are on the extras: it charges a convenience fee on certain utility bills paid by card, and historically a small fee on some recharges. Plain UPI from your bank account is free.

**Google Pay — the cleanest, most minimal app.** Free bank-to-bank UPI, straightforward bill reminders, UPI Lite for small PIN-free payments, and UPI Circle for letting a trusted family member pay from your account within set limits. Like PhonePe, the one recurring cost is a convenience fee of roughly 0.5% to 1% plus GST when you pay bills with a credit or debit card instead of UPI.

**Paytm — the widest range of features, the most fee lines to read.** Paytm does payments, bills, wallet, and more, and everyday UPI transfers are free. Its recharge and bill-payment services have carried a platform fee (reported at around ₹4 to ₹30 depending on the amount), so check the fee line on the confirmation screen before you pay a bill here.

**A practical rule for all three:** use UPI from your bank account for bills when the app offers it, and treat "pay by card" on a bill page as the option that may cost extra. You can keep two of these apps installed as a backup for when a merchant's QR or a bank server is having a bad day, but remember they share the same bank-level limits.

**UPI Lite** deserves a mention for small payments: it lets you make PIN-free payments of up to ₹1,000 per transaction from a small balance held in the app, and those payments sit outside the standard daily UPI limit. It's convenient for tea stalls and autos; it's not a substitute for checking your statement.

## The money management layer

This is where you actually see what you spend. The main options fall into two styles.

**SMS-reading trackers (Android only).** Money View, Axio (formerly Walnut), and ET Money read the bank SMS your phone receives and categorise each payment, which is how they capture UPI spending from every app at once without any bank login. The trade-offs are real: they need SMS access, which is a privacy decision you should make knowingly, several of them pair the tracker with loan or investment promotions, and iPhones don't allow apps to read SMS, so this approach mostly doesn't work on iOS. Of the three, Money View is the most commonly recommended for plain tracking with broader money tools; ET Money suits people who also invest in mutual funds through the same app.

**Neobank apps, like Jupiter.** Jupiter builds spend categories into the app from activity on its own account. The tracking is polished, but it's most useful once a meaningful share of your spending runs through that account, so it's a choice about where you bank, not just which app you install.

**Manual or budget-first apps.** If you'd rather set monthly limits yourself than review auto-categorised history, a manual tracker with envelopes or category budgets works, at the cost of typing. The general comparison of how those work is in [Best Budgeting Apps Compared](/blog/best-budgeting-apps-compared/); note that many US-first budgeting apps can't read Indian UPI activity automatically.

## What UPI actually costs you, and what changes on October 15

For consumers the honest answer is: nothing on UPI itself. RBI has said transactions remain free for users, and that is the basis for everything above. What changes from **October 15, 2026** is on the merchant side, per the Department of Financial Services' FAQ published on September 15:

- Person-to-person transfers stay free.
- Merchant payments up to ₹2,000 stay free.
- Above ₹2,000, merchants pay 0.4%, capped at ₹300 (the cap applies from ₹75,000 and up), with flat or lower rates for some categories such as railways, telecom, insurance, fuel, utilities, and securities purchases.
- Small merchants receiving up to ₹1 lakh a month are exempt.
- Merchants are not allowed to pass the charge on to you, and the government has told banks to make sure they don't. A petition against the framework is already before the Supreme Court, so details could still shift.

The practical takeaway: if a shop asks for an extra "UPI charge" on top of the price after October 15, that is not how the rule is meant to work. Keep the receipt and raise it with the merchant or your bank.

## A worked example: the bill-payment fee that isn't UPI

The fee you can actually avoid today is the card-on-a-bill-page one. Say you pay a ₹6,000 electricity bill every month using a credit card through a payment app that charges 0.5% to 1% plus GST (illustrative: real rates vary by app, biller, and card).

- At 0.5%: ₹30, plus 18% GST ≈ **₹35.40** per bill.
- At 1%: ₹60, plus 18% GST ≈ **₹70.80** per bill.
- Over twelve months: roughly **₹425 to ₹850** in fees.
- Paying the same bill by UPI from your bank account: **₹0**.

If your card returns about 1% in rewards on that spend, a 1% fee plus GST (about 1.18% all-in) leaves you slightly behind even before you count the interest risk of carrying a card balance, while a 0.5% fee (about 0.59% all-in) leaves a small net gain. The point isn't that cards are always bad; it's that the fee line is easy to miss and easy to avoid.

## Who each pick is actually for

- **PhonePe** — for anyone who wants the widest merchant and friend coverage and doesn't mind a busier app.
- **Google Pay** — for people who want the simplest interface, and for families who'll use UPI Circle.
- **Paytm** — for people who already use its wallet or services; read the fee line on each bill.
- **Money View, Axio, or ET Money** — for Android users who want zero-effort tracking of all UPI spending and are comfortable granting SMS access.
- **Jupiter** — for people willing to move a meaningful share of spending into that account in exchange for built-in categorisation.
- **Not a fit:** anyone on iOS expecting an SMS tracker to work the way it does on Android, or anyone who wants one app to be both the wallet and the analyst.

## Common mistakes

- **Assuming a second UPI app doubles your daily limit.** The ₹1 lakh and transaction-count limits apply to the bank account, so switching apps doesn't reset them.
- **Paying bills by card inside a payment app without reading the fee.** The UPI route from your bank account is usually free; the card route often isn't.
- **Granting SMS permission without reading what else the app does with it.** Check the app's privacy description, and revoke the permission if you stop using it.
- **Treating a payment app's transaction history as a budget.** History shows what happened; a budget is a limit you set beforehand. Pair the tracker with a simple monthly target, and once you know your real monthly surplus, see [EPF vs PPF: India's Retirement Savings Basics Explained](/blog/epf-vs-ppf-indias-retirement-savings-basics-explained/) for where long-term savings can go.
- **Approving a "collect" request you didn't expect.** You enter your UPI PIN only to send money, never to receive it; an unexpected request asking for your PIN is a scam pattern.
