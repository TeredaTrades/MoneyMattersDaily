It's easy to assume that fuel costs the same everywhere in a country. In some places it nearly does, because the government sets the price. In many others it doesn't, and two stations a few minutes apart can post noticeably different numbers. That raises a practical question every driver has asked: is it worth driving out of your way to the cheaper one? The answer is a short piece of arithmetic, and it often comes out closer than you'd think.

## Who this is for, and who it isn't

**It's for:** anyone who drives regularly, fills their own tank, and wonders whether chasing a lower pump price pays off. It also covers how fuel pricing works in a few other countries, since the rules differ more than most people expect.

**It's not for:** electric vehicle charging, or fleet and business fuel accounting. This is general information, not personalized financial advice, and the country details below can change, so check current rules where you live.

## Does the price depend on where you are?

It depends on who sets it. In some countries, stations choose their own prices and compete. In others, a government agency sets the price or a ceiling, and every station follows it. A few mix the two, letting stations set prices but regulating when or how they can change them.

Where stations set their own prices, the US Energy Information Administration (EIA) lists several reasons they differ. At regional scale: state and local taxes, how far fuel has to travel from refineries and terminals, supply disruptions, how many stations compete in an area, and environmental fuel programs that raise costs in some regions. EIA's 2025 annual averages for regular gasoline ranged from about $2.68 a gallon on the Gulf Coast to about $4.09 on the West Coast.

Even at neighborhood scale, EIA says prices reflect local conditions. Station operating costs such as wages, rent and insurance vary by location. So do traffic patterns, supply sources and the number of nearby competitors, along with the owner's own marketing approach. That's why two stations on the same road can be priced differently on the same day.

## How a few countries handle it

These examples show how different the systems are. All details are as of the dates given, and fuel rules have been moving quickly in 2026.

| Place | Who sets the price | What's notable (as of) |
|---|---|---|
| United States | Each station, in a competitive market | Regional averages in 2025 ranged from about $2.68 to $4.09 per gallon (EIA). Nearby stations can differ too. |
| Germany | Each station, with a timing rule | Since 1 April 2026, stations may raise prices only once a day, at noon, but can cut them any time. On 9 April, ZDF reported prices for regular petrol running roughly 5 to 10 cents per litre higher around midday, so mornings and evenings were cheaper. The ADAC motoring club said the rule makes prices more predictable but hasn't lowered them. |
| Western Australia | Each station, with a price lock | Under FuelWatch, retailers must notify next-day prices by 2pm. Those prices apply for 24 hours from 6am the next day, and are published by 2:30pm, so you can see tomorrow's prices today. Listed prices are standard cash prices and exclude supermarket discounts and card fees (Consumer Protection WA, page updated June 2026). |
| Quebec, Canada | Each station, with a public price map | Since 1 April 2026, covered fuel sellers must make their prices available on an official Régie de l'énergie map, which is updated continuously. Drivers can compare stations anywhere in the province. |
| Kenya | The regulator sets monthly maximums | EPRA sets ceilings that can differ by town. For 15 October to 14 November 2026, maximum super petrol was reported at KSh 226.96 per litre in Nairobi and KSh 223.81 in Mombasa. Stations can charge less. |
| Ethiopia | The government | Pump prices are announced by the government and change in steps, rather than station by station. Reports in 2026 described several increases, citing subsidy costs and supply disruption. Check the latest announced schedule rather than relying on an old figure. |

Two things stand out. First, "a country" isn't one pricing system, which is why advice from a driver in one place can be useless in another. Second, a rule that makes prices more visible or more predictable doesn't automatically make them lower. Germany's ADAC made that point about the noon rule, and the same logic applies elsewhere: transparency helps you pick the cheapest option, but it doesn't remove the gap between stations.

## The break-even math

You gain from the lower price and lose to the extra driving. The comparison is:

**Saving = price gap per litre × litres you buy**

**Detour cost = extra distance × litres your car uses per km × price per litre**

If you think in gallons and miles, the detour cost is extra miles divided by your miles per gallon, times the price per gallon. Either way, the detour is only worth it when the saving is bigger than the detour cost, plus whatever you value your time at.

The numbers below are made up, in a neutral currency, and assume a car that uses 8 litres per 100 km (0.08 litres per km) and a cheaper-station price of 1.74 per litre.

| Case | Price gap | Litres | Saving | Extra driving | Detour fuel | Net, fuel only |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Big tank-up | 0.06 | 40 | 2.40 | 6 km | 0.84 | +1.56 |
| Small gap | 0.02 | 30 | 0.60 | 5 km | 0.70 | -0.10 |
| Small top-up | 0.06 | 20 | 1.20 | 6 km | 0.84 | +0.36 |

In the first case, the fuel math says the detour pays, up to about 17 km of extra driving before it breaks even. In the second, a small gap and a short detour already cancel out. In the third, the same gap and the same detour are barely worth it because you're only buying half as much.

Now add your time. If the first case takes ten minutes and you value your time at 12 an hour, that's another 2.00, which turns +1.56 into a small loss. That's an illustration, not a recommendation: what your time is worth is your call.

A rule of thumb that follows from the math: a detour is most likely worth it when you're buying a lot of fuel, the gap is large, and the extra distance is short. If any one of those is missing, it often isn't.

## What the posted price doesn't show

The number on the sign isn't always the number you pay, so comparing signs can mislead.

- **Discounts and card fees.** FuelWatch in Western Australia lists cash prices without supermarket discounts or card charges, which is a reminder that the real price can be lower with a loyalty offer or higher with a card surcharge. Check what you'd actually pay.
- **Fuel grade.** Make sure you're comparing the same type, not regular at one station and premium at another.
- **The detour itself.** Wear, parking, a busy junction, or driving in the dark all have a cost that doesn't appear in the formula.
- **Running low.** Driving further on a nearly empty tank to reach a cheaper station is a bad trade if it risks running out.
- **Timing.** Where prices change on a schedule, as in Germany and Western Australia, when you fill up can matter as much as where.

## If your country sets one price

Where fuel prices are set by the government or by a regulator's ceiling, as in Ethiopia and, to a degree, Kenya, the gap between stations is small or nonexistent, so a detour for a cheaper price rarely makes sense. The savings are elsewhere: avoiding unofficial sellers who charge far above the official price during shortages (reports in Ethiopia have described exactly that), not letting the tank run empty before you refuel, and driving in ways that use less fuel.
## Common mistakes

- **Driving a long way to save a small amount.** A gap of a few cents per litre disappears quickly once the detour is counted.
- **Comparing signs without checking the grade or conditions.** Discounts, card fees and fuel type change the real price.
- **Ignoring how much fuel you're buying.** The same gap is worth twice as much on a full tank as on a half tank.
- **Forgetting your own time.** If it isn't worth it to you, it isn't a saving.
- **Assuming the rules in one country apply in another.** Some set prices, some limit when they change, and some publish them for comparison.
- **Treating old price figures as current.** Fuel prices have swung widely in 2026, so only a recent number is useful.

## Getting started

Work out your car's fuel use once, in litres per 100 km or miles per gallon, and keep it handy. Next time you see a cheaper station, run the two lines of math above before you drive there. If your area publishes prices, such as a regulator's map or a next-day price list, use it to compare before you leave. Over a year, a handful of good decisions beats a lot of small trips for tiny savings.
