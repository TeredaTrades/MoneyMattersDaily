Sending money across a border looks like one decision and is really four: **how much arrives, how fast, how safe it is and what you can do if it goes wrong.** A transfer with a zero fee can still leave the recipient with less than one that charges a visible fee, because part of the cost hides in the exchange rate. This guide compares the main types of service on the things that matter, explains how to work out the true cost, and ends with a checklist. It names example providers for each type but does not rank them or quote fees, because fees and country availability change often and differ by route.

The consumer-rights details below are for the United States. Other countries have their own rules, and some have fewer protections, so check your regulator's pages for your route.

## Who this is for, and who it isn't

**It's for:** anyone sending money to family, paying a supplier or school fees abroad, or receiving money from overseas and wanting to understand what the sender should be using.

**It isn't** a recommendation of one provider. The best option depends on the **route** (the sending and receiving countries), the amount, how the money must be received and how quickly.

## The real cost: fee plus exchange rate

Two things take money out of a transfer:

1. **The fee,** the amount the provider charges you.
2. **The exchange-rate margin,** the gap between the rate you get and the mid-market rate, which is the rate you see on a search engine or in a currency feed.

Some providers advertise "no fees" and earn their money in the rate. The only fair test is: **if I send this amount, how much does the recipient actually receive?** Compare that final figure, in the recipient's currency, across providers at the same moment. Also look for fees taken at the receiving end, such as a bank or agent charging to pay out.

## The main types of service

| Type | Examples (not rankings) | Where it tends to fit | Watch for |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Bank wire** | Your own bank | Large amounts, payments that need a bank paper trail | Higher fees, intermediary-bank deductions, slower, fixed cut-off times |
| **Specialist online transfer services** | Wise, Remitly, WorldRemit | Regular transfers, comparing rates, bank-to-bank or wallet delivery | Country and delivery-method availability, limits, identity checks |
| **Cash-pickup money transfer operators** | Western Union, MoneyGram | Recipients without a bank account or who need cash fast | The exchange-rate margin, pickup-location rules |
| **Multi-currency accounts and cards** | Revolut and similar | People who hold or spend several currencies | Plan limits, which currencies and countries are supported. See [multi-currency wallets compared](/blog/best-multi-currency-wallets-compared-one-account-for-several-currencies/) |
| **Mobile money and local wallets** | M-Pesa and similar, linked through partner services | Receiving in countries where wallets are the main way to hold money | Cross-border links vary by country pair. See [mobile money in Africa](/blog/mobile-money-in-africa-m-pesa-and-digital-banking-basics-explained/) |
| **Stablecoin and crypto transfers** | Wallet apps and exchanges | Some cross-border cases where both sides can use them | Conversion at both ends, price risk until converted, scams, tax and rules vary by country. See [bitcoin vs. ethereum vs. stablecoins](/blog/bitcoin-vs-ethereum-vs-stablecoins-whats-the-difference/) |

The names are examples of each type, not endorsements, and a provider on this list may not serve your route. Check each provider's own pages for your country pair on the day you send. The same goes for any provider you find elsewhere.

On the crypto row: it deserves the same caution as the rest of this site's crypto coverage. Prices can move between sending and converting, there is usually no way to reverse a mistaken or scammed payment, and recipients may find it hard to turn the money into local currency. It is an option some people use, not a shortcut.

## Your rights when you send from the US

The US Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB) publishes a fact sheet on its remittance transfer rule, which covers electronic transfers of more than $15 from US consumers to recipients abroad, with a small-provider exemption. Under it, the provider must give you, in writing and before you pay:

- the exchange rate,
- the fees and taxes it charges,
- fees charged by overseas agents and certain other institutions,
- and the amount expected to be delivered, plus a notice if foreign taxes or recipient fees could reduce it.

The fact sheet adds that you generally have **30 minutes** to cancel at no charge, can dispute an error for about **six months**, and that the provider has **90 days** to investigate. Scheduled transfers can be cancelled up to three business days before they are sent.

If you send from or to another country, the rights may be different or weaker. Ask the provider what applies, and look up your national regulator.

## A worked example

This uses invented numbers in a made-up currency. Someone sends 500 USD abroad. The mid-market rate is 100 local units per USD. Three providers quote:

| | Fee | Rate offered | Recipient gets |
|---|---|---|---|
| Provider A | 4 | 98.5 | (500 − 4) × 98.5 = **48,856** |
| Provider B | 0 ("no fee") | 96.5 | 500 × 96.5 = **48,250** |
| Provider C | 10 | 99.5 | (500 − 10) × 99.5 = **48,755** |

At the mid-market rate, 500 USD would be 50,000. Provider B's "no fee" transfer costs the recipient **1,750**, the most of the three. Provider A, which charges a visible fee, leaves them the most. The numbers are made up, but the pattern is real: the headline fee tells you very little.

## Common mistakes

- **Comparing fees only.** Compare what lands in the recipient's account or hand.
- **Not checking the receiving end.** Pickup fees, agent fees and bank deductions can reduce the amount.
- **Using the first provider shown on the app store.** Fake apps and impostors exist, so use official links.
- **Sending a large amount first.** Test with a small one.
- **Ignoring limits and identity checks.** A new account may have low limits or take days to verify.
- **Paying someone who contacted you first.** Requests to send money to a stranger by transfer, crypto or gift card are a common scam pattern.
- **Losing the proof.** Keep the confirmation and tracking number.

## Getting started

1. **Write down the route, amount and how the recipient needs the money:** bank, wallet or cash.
2. **Check two or three providers** for that exact route and compare the **final amount received**.
3. **Check the delivery time, limits and the recipient's costs.**
4. **Send a small test first** if it is a new provider or route.
5. **Keep the receipt and tracking details,** and know how to cancel or dispute.
