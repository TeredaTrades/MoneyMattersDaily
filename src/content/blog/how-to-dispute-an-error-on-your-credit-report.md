A wrong account, a payment marked late that wasn't, or a debt that belongs to someone else can drag down a score you've worked hard on. The good news is that US law gives you a clear, free process for challenging mistakes, and the bureaus have a fixed window to respond. The less good news is that the process works best when you're organized and put things in writing.

## Who this is for, and who it isn't

**It's for:** anyone in the US who spots something on a credit report that's wrong, outdated, or not theirs.

**It's not for:** accurate negative items. A dispute can't remove a late payment that really happened, and paying for a "credit repair" service to dispute everything rarely beats doing it yourself. If you suspect identity theft, that's a different and more urgent process (start at IdentityTheft.gov). Nothing here is legal advice.

## Step 1: Pull your reports and read them closely

Free reports are available from all three bureaus (Equifax, Experian, and TransUnion) at AnnualCreditReport.com. Since October 2023 the bureaus have offered these free reports weekly, on a permanent basis. Use that official site only, since the FTC warns that look-alike sites may charge you or be fraudulent.

Pull all three, because each bureau builds its own file and an error can appear on one and not the others. Then check:

- Personal details: name, addresses, employers you don't recognize
- Accounts you never opened, or that belong to someone with a similar name
- Wrong balances, credit limits, or account status (open vs. closed)
- Payments marked late that you paid on time
- The same debt listed twice

For what a normal report entry should look like and what actually moves a score, see [what hurts your credit score](/blog/what-hurts-your-credit-score/).

## Step 2: Gather proof

A dispute is stronger with evidence. Collect copies, never originals, of anything that backs you up: bank statements showing an on-time payment, a paid-in-full letter, a closure confirmation, or a police or identity-theft report. Mark each error on a copy of the report so it's obvious what you're challenging.

## Step 3: Dispute with the bureau and the company that reported it

The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau describes this as a two-part process. You contact the credit bureau showing the error, and you also contact the furnisher, meaning the lender or collector that supplied the information.

For the bureau, you can dispute online, by phone, or by mail. The CFPB lists these starting points:

| Bureau | Where to dispute |
|---|---|
| Equifax | equifax.com credit dispute page, or (866) 349-5191 |
| Experian | experian.com/disputes, or (888) 397-3742 |
| TransUnion | dispute.transunion.com, or (800) 916-8800 |

Written disputes sent by certified mail with return receipt requested give you a paper trail, and both the FTC and CFPB recommend that route. A good letter includes:

1. Your full name and address
2. Each error, described specifically with the account number
3. A plain explanation of why it's wrong
4. What you want done (corrected or removed)
5. Copies of your proof and the marked-up report

Keep it short and factual. Anger doesn't help and a clean, specific letter is easier to act on.

## Step 4: Understand the clock

Once the bureau receives your dispute, it generally has 30 days to investigate. In some cases, such as when you send additional information partway through, the law lets that stretch to 45 days. The bureau passes your evidence to the furnisher, which must investigate and report back. The CFPB notes that furnishers generally must respond within 30 days of receiving the dispute too.

Keep copies of everything and note the dates you mailed and received things.

## Step 5: Read the results

You'll get written results. Three outcomes are possible:

- **Corrected or removed.** The error is fixed. If anything changes, the bureau must give you a free copy of the updated report, and this doesn't count against your free annual report. You can also ask it to notify anyone who got your report in the past six months (two years for employment purposes).
- **Verified as accurate.** The furnisher says the information is right. If you still disagree, you can add a short statement of the dispute to your file, though bureaus may charge a fee for this.
- **Can't be verified.** Information that can't be verified has to come off your report.

If you're unhappy with how a dispute was handled, you can file a complaint with the CFPB, and you can send follow-up disputes with new evidence.

## A worked example

Maya pulls her three reports and finds a credit card on her Experian file showing a 30-day late payment from March. Her bank statement shows the payment cleared on time. She prints the statement, circles the entry, and sends a one-page letter to Experian by certified mail, with a second letter to the card issuer. She notes the delivery date on a calendar. A few weeks later, Experian writes back that the late mark was removed and sends an updated report. She checks that the other two bureaus never had the error, and sets a reminder to review her reports again in three months.

## Where people actually go wrong

- **Disputing only with the bureau.** The furnisher is part of the process, so contact both.
- **Vague letters.** "This is wrong" gives the bureau nothing to check. Name the account and the specific error.
- **Sending originals.** Always send copies.
- **Disputing everything.** Challenging accurate items wastes time and can slow the real fixes.
- **Not following up.** Check your report after the investigation to confirm the correction actually shows up.

## Getting started

Pull all three reports this week, circle only what's really wrong, and send your disputes in writing with proof attached.
