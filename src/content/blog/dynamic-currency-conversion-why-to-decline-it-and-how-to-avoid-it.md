You tap your card at a restaurant in Lisbon and the terminal asks: pay in euros, or in your home currency? Paying in your own currency sounds like the friendly option, because you see a familiar number. That choice is called **dynamic currency conversion (DCC)**, and in most cases the friendly number costs you more.

This post explains what DCC is, who earns money from it, how much it can cost, and how to decline it without any fuss. It builds on the short DCC sections in [how to manage money while traveling abroad](/blog/how-to-manage-money-while-traveling-abroad/) and [how to use ATMs abroad without overpaying in fees](/blog/how-to-use-atms-abroad-without-overpaying-in-fees/).

Figures here are examples from mid-2026 sources and from a worked calculation. Markups vary by machine and provider, so treat them as illustrations, not quotes.

## Who this is for, and who it isn't

**It's for:** anyone who pays by card or withdraws cash in a country with a different currency from their own, whether on a two-week trip or while living abroad.

**It's not for:** payments made in your home currency inside your home country, where no conversion happens. It also isn't a guide to choosing a travel card; for that, see [best multi-currency wallets compared](/blog/best-multi-currency-wallets-compared-one-account-for-several-currencies/).

## What DCC actually is

Visa describes it plainly: a merchant or ATM offers to convert your purchase or withdrawal into your home currency instead of the local currency, and the conversion includes an exchange rate plus additional fees.

There are really two ways to pay for the same item abroad:

1. **Pay in the local currency.** The shop charges you in euros, baht or pesos. Your own card network (Visa, Mastercard and so on) or bank converts the amount to your currency later.
2. **Pay in your home currency (DCC).** The shop's payment provider converts the amount on the spot, at a rate it chooses, and charges your card in your currency.

In the first case your card's side picks the rate. In the second, the merchant's side does. NerdWallet's comparison notes that card network rates are usually close to market rates, while DCC rates tend to be considerably worse.

## Why merchants and ATMs offer it

DCC isn't a favor. OneMileAtATime points out that the financial companies that sell DCC earn money from it, and so do the merchants and ATM operators who get a commission on each DCC transaction. That's why the prompt often appears at the moment you're most likely to tap "yes" without thinking, and why the home-currency option is frequently presented as the easy default.

For you, the one real advantage is certainty: you see the exact amount in your own currency before you pay. That's a small convenience set against a rate you can't compare in the moment.

## What it costs

The markup depends on the provider, so the sources give a range rather than one number:

- **At card terminals:** Card Ratings cites a markup of 3% to 5% on DCC purchases and says it's separate from, and added on top of, any foreign transaction fee your card charges. OneMileAtATime puts card DCC fees at about 1% to 5% in one place and "often more than 3%" in another, so the sources don't perfectly agree.
- **At ATMs:** the same site's July 2026 example showed a 12.95% conversion fee on the home-currency option, plus a separate transaction fee. A Wise guide shows a smaller gap in its examples, with DCC about 6% to 7% above the mid-market rate.

The **mid-market rate** is the midpoint between what buyers and sellers of a currency are quoting, the one you'd see in a search engine. It's the fair benchmark.

### A worked example

Say you buy something priced at 200 euros, and the mid-market rate is 1 euro = $1.15, so the fair price is **$230.00**. These numbers are illustrative, and the calculation ignores small rate changes between the purchase and the posting date.

| Scenario | You pay | Extra vs. $230 |
|---|---|---|
| Decline DCC, card with no foreign fee | about $230.00 | $0.00 |
| Decline DCC, card with a 3% foreign fee | $236.90 | $6.90 |
| Accept DCC at a 3% markup, no foreign fee | $236.90 | $6.90 |
| Accept DCC at a 5% markup, no foreign fee | $241.50 | $11.50 |
| Accept DCC at 5%, plus a 3% foreign fee on top | $248.74 | $18.74 |

Two things stand out. First, DCC can cost as much as a foreign transaction fee, and you can pay it even on a card that has no such fee. Second, the last row is possible: Card Ratings says the two charges stack, although some issuers may treat a home-currency charge differently, so check your card's terms.

At a 13% ATM markup on a $200 withdrawal, the same math gives about $26 lost to the conversion alone.

Card Ratings estimates DCC can add roughly $30 per $1,000 spent, which is a useful rule of thumb at the 3% end.

## What the networks say you're entitled to

Visa's traveler guidance sets out several expectations for merchants and ATMs:

- **You get to choose.** Visa says merchants and ATMs must not choose on your behalf, and that accepting or declining doesn't affect your ability to buy or withdraw.
- **They must show the numbers.** On screens and receipts, the amount should appear in both currencies, with the exchange rate and any additional fees or markup.
- **No nudging.** Visa says they must not use tactics like different font sizes or colors to steer your decision.

If the required details aren't shown, or you feel pressured to pick one currency, Visa recommends declining and reporting it to your card issuer.

In the EU, a separate rule, Regulation (EU) 2019/518, requires card-payment conversion charges to be expressed as a percentage markup over the European Central Bank's reference rates and communicated before payment, with application from December 2019 for most provisions. A summary of that rule doesn't spell out how it covers merchant-offered DCC at terminals or ATMs, so don't assume it protects you there.

## How to avoid DCC

1. **Choose the local currency every time.** OneMileAtATime's advice is to do this "just about always." If the prompt says pay in euros or dollars and you're in Europe, pick euros.
2. **Read the screen before you tap.** A prompt with two currencies, an exchange rate, or "we'll convert for you" language is DCC. Decline the home-currency option. On some machines that means pressing "No" or a back button, so read which currency each button refers to.
3. **Check the receipt.** If it shows your home currency, a conversion rate and a fee, DCC was applied. Ask the cashier whether they can void it and run it again in the local currency; some will, some won't, and a refusal is a reason to pay another way next time.
4. **At ATMs, decline the offered conversion** and let your own bank convert. Card Ratings notes the machine's own fee can still be high at tourist spots, so keep the number of withdrawals low.
5. **At hotels and rentals, ask for local-currency billing.** Card Ratings advises declining DCC when you give card details for a reservation and requesting local-currency billing before checkout.
6. **Do the same online.** A web shop that offers to show the price in your currency is making the same choice. Use the local-currency option when it's offered and the checkout shows the rate.

## When the prompt feels ambiguous

Not every screen is clear. If you can't tell which option is which, or the terminal doesn't show a rate, Visa's advice is simply to decline. A few practical habits help:

- **Look for currency symbols and codes.** A "$" or "USD" means your home currency if you hold a US card; "EUR" or "€" means local.
- **Ask the staff.** "Can I pay in euros, please?" is a normal request.
- **Walk away if pushed.** Being rushed is a reason to decline, not to accept.

## Does a multi-currency account or crypto card change this?

Partly. A multi-currency account lets you hold balances in several currencies, so spending in the local currency can draw on a balance you already hold, with no conversion at all. That avoids the issue only if you choose the local currency at the terminal; if you accept DCC, the merchant's rate still applies. Our [multi-currency wallets comparison](/blog/best-multi-currency-wallets-compared-one-account-for-several-currencies/) covers the main types, including local rails and stablecoin-based options.

Crypto and stablecoin cards usually still convert your holdings into the local currency at spend time under the provider's own terms, which can include fees, so read them before relying on one abroad. Neither a wallet nor a card makes DCC acceptable; it only changes who does the converting once you decline it.

## Common mistakes

- **Assuming your own currency is the safe choice.** It's the more expensive one.
- **Assuming a no-foreign-fee card avoids DCC.** It avoids your issuer's fee, not the merchant's markup.
- **Not checking the receipt.** Once the charge posts, undoing it is harder than declining it.
- **Accepting at the ATM because the screen looks official.** The ATM's operator sets that rate.
- **Skipping the issue at hotels.** Large bills magnify a 3% to 5% markup.
- **Comparing against a bad benchmark.** Compare with the mid-market rate, not with what the terminal shows.

## Getting started

1. **Before you go,** check your card's foreign transaction fee and foreign ATM terms, and note the mid-market rate for the currency.
2. **At the terminal,** choose the local currency and decline any offer to convert.
3. **After paying,** glance at the receipt and your card app to confirm the charge is in local currency.
4. **If DCC was applied,** ask the merchant to void and re-run it; if that fails, report it to your card issuer.
5. **Keep a lower-cost backup,** such as a multi-currency account or a card with no foreign transaction fee, for when your main card is a poor fit.
