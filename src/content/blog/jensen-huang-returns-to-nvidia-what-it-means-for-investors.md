On Monday, November 23, 2026, Nvidia's board announced that Jensen Huang was back as chief executive, president and board member, effective immediately. He had resigned eleven days earlier. Nvidia is the AI chip company that became one of the most valuable in the world, and its shares went from a steep fall to an unusually large one-day jump within the same fortnight.

This post is a snapshot **as of Wednesday, November 25, 2026 (UTC)**, and the latest market figures I have are from Tuesday, November 24. It is a follow-up to [Jensen Huang resigns from Nvidia: what it means for investors](/blog/jensen-huang-resigns-from-nvidia-what-it-means-for-investors/), which explains the background. Some details are still unsettled, and figures in news reports sometimes differ. It makes no predictions about the share price and recommends no trades.

## Who this is for, and who it isn't

**It's for:** anyone who owns Nvidia directly, owns it through an index fund or ETF, or wants to understand why one company's news moved the whole market.

**It isn't** investment advice, and it does not take a side on the political or legal questions around the story.

## What Nvidia says happened

These points come from Nvidia's own press release of November 23, which was filed with the US Securities and Exchange Commission and which I read directly:

- **The decision.** The board voted unanimously to restore Huang as CEO, president and director. It said it was "satisfied with respect to Mr. Huang's candor and ability to lead NVIDIA" after a series of discussions with him.
- **National security.** The board said that input from senior US government officials led it to conclude his return "poses no national security risk."
- **Who is in charge.** Harvey Jones remains chair and said, "The Board believes Jensen is the right person to lead NVIDIA." Colette Kress, who was interim CEO, continues as chief financial officer.
- **The investigation continues.** A committee of independent directors, advised by outside counsel, is still looking into reports that a foreign intelligence effort targeted Huang. The board says it will run to completion, and Huang says he supports it and will cooperate.
- **New oversight.** Nvidia created a National Security and Export Control Committee made up only of independent directors, which is hiring counsel with national security experience. It is also searching for a chief compliance officer, who would report to the board's Nominating and Corporate Governance Committee. It says it will keep cooperating with government inquiries.
- **Huang's statement.** He said, "I take this responsibility with the greatest humility and renewed commitment."

## What is reported, and what is not confirmed

- **The politics.** Several outlets report that President Trump publicly backed Huang's return, and some describe heavy pressure on the board. Al Jazeera calls it "unprecedented pressure." Nvidia's own release does not mention the President and refers only to input from "senior U.S. government officials." I could not read the NBC, CNBC or Bloomberg pieces whose headlines link the decision to Trump, so treat the extent of his role as reported, not established.
- **A reversal by the board.** TheWrap reports that Chair Harvey Jones had said on the previous Tuesday (November 17) that the company did not intend to bring Huang back. That is TheWrap's account, not something I could check in a primary source.
- **The Justice Department.** Fox News reports that Attorney General Todd Blanche said the department has found no evidence of criminal wrongdoing by Huang, and that Huang is not under investigation "at least not that I saw." Blanche declined to say whether an export-control probe involving Nvidia is active. Those are the Attorney General's words as reported by Fox, not a formal finding.
- **Huang's message to staff.** Fox News, citing Wired, reports that at his first all-hands meeting Huang said he had made "very serious personal mistakes," and that he will be subject to regular 360-degree reviews. I have not read the Wired article.
- **Criticism.** Democratic lawmakers have criticised the decision and are pushing export-control legislation, according to Fox News. These are political statements, not findings, and no charges have been reported.
- **Personal allegations.** The earlier reports about Huang's personal relationships and a person said to have ties to Chinese intelligence remain unresolved. This post does not repeat claims about named individuals, and the board's investigation has not published a finding.

Two other facts from that week:

- **Market value.** The Financial Times, via Al Jazeera, estimates that Nvidia lost $1.16 trillion in market value when Huang resigned, and had recovered $952 billion of it by Monday.
- **The wider market.** On Monday, ABC News reports the Dow rose 3.0%, the S&P 500 4.7% and the Nasdaq 6.5%, with Dell up 20%, Micron 15% and CoreWeave 22%. On Tuesday, chip stocks fell again, with AMD down more than 10% and Micron more than 8%, according to Al Jazeera, which linked the drop to reports of new US tariffs on China. ## The earnings in the middle

Nvidia reported its third-quarter results on November 17, in the middle of the saga. According to a market wire repost I read (not Nvidia's release, which I could not open), revenue was about $115.8 billion against about $108.7 billion expected, and data centers were about 93% of it. The company forecast about $130 billion for the next quarter, plus or minus 3%, with no China data-center compute revenue assumed, and a lower gross margin of 71% to 72%. The shares fell on the news anyway. One session cannot show how much of any move came from the results, the leadership story or the wider market.

## A worked example: why a 31% rise did not feel like a full recovery

Percentages work in a lopsided way. Using the prices above:

- From the November 12 close ($166.19) to November 20 ($125.53), the stock fell about **24.5%**.
- From November 20 to November 23 it rose about **31.5%**, to about $165.05.
- Net across both: about **-0.7%** against November 12. The 31.5% gain just got back what the 24.5% fall took.

The reason is that a fall is measured against a higher starting point than the rise that follows. After a 24% fall, you need about 31.6% to break even. An invented $10,000 position shows it: down 24% leaves $7,600, and up 31.5% leaves about $9,994. A big rebound is not the same as a gain.

The stock also remains about 32% below its October 6 record high of $243.37 (the high is from the same source).

## What happens next

- **The board investigation.** No timeline has been given.
- **Congress.** Fox News mentions an upcoming appearance by Huang with no date or format. Earlier reports varied between a closed-door briefing and testimony before Thanksgiving (November 26). I have not seen it confirmed.
- **Government inquiries.** Nvidia says it is cooperating. I found no report of charges.
- **Rates.** Traders are pricing a quarter-point Federal Reserve rate rise at the December 8 to 9 meeting at close to certain (Decrypt cited CME FedWatch at 96.2% on November 23; Fox News says it is fully priced in). That is market pricing, not a decision. See [what the Federal Reserve does and how rate decisions affect your money](/blog/what-the-federal-reserve-does-and-how-rate-decisions-affect-your-money/).
- **China trade policy.** The tariff reports that coincided with Tuesday's chip selloff remain unconfirmed in my sources.

## What this means for investors

- **One person can be a risk factor.** Investors call this key-person risk. A company's value can depend heavily on one leader, and a sudden change can move the share price a long way in either direction, as this fortnight showed.
- **Speed cuts both ways.** A one-day gain of about 31% in a trillion-dollar company is rare. Prices that move that fast can also move back, as Tuesday showed.
- **Indexes carry it too.** If you own an index fund or ETF, you probably own Nvidia. See [stock market indices explained](/blog/stock-market-indices-explained-sp-500-dow-jones-and-nasdaq/), [index funds explained for beginners](/blog/index-funds-explained-for-beginners/) and [ETFs vs mutual funds](/blog/etfs-vs-mutual-funds-whats-the-difference-and-which-fits-you/).
- **Headlines are not a plan.** Buying or selling because of a daily move is trading on information everyone already has. See [what stock market volatility means for your savings](/blog/what-stock-market-volatility-means-for-your-savings/).
- **Match the money to the timeline.** Money you need soon should not depend on a single stock. For the wider selloff context, including crypto, see [why tech stocks and crypto are falling together](/blog/why-tech-stocks-and-crypto-are-falling-together/).

## Common mistakes

- **Reading a rebound as a verdict.** A 31% day shows how fast sentiment can change, not what happens next.
- **Treating reported claims as confirmed.** Several parts of this story rest on unnamed sources or a single outlet.
- **Ignoring the percentage trap** above when you think about your own losses and recoveries.
- **Concentrating** a large share of your savings in one stock because it has recovered.
- **Acting on rumor.** Wait for filings, company statements and named sources.
