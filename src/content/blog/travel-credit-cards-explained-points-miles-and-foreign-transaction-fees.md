Travel credit cards promise free flights, hotel nights and no foreign fees. Some deliver. Others charge an annual fee that is larger than anything you will ever earn back. The difference is mostly arithmetic, and you can do it yourself before you apply.

This post explains how points and miles work, what a foreign transaction fee is and how to avoid it, and a simple way to test whether a travel card is worth carrying. It is general education, not a recommendation of any card. Card terms change often, so this post quotes no current offers, and the details differ by country. The examples lean on US cards because most of the public guidance is written about them.

## Who this is for, and who it isn't

**It's for:** people who travel abroad at least once or twice a year, pay with a card, and want to stop losing a few percent on every purchase.

**It isn't for:** anyone who carries a balance from month to month. Interest on an unpaid balance is usually far larger than any reward, and it cancels the benefit. If that is your situation, start with [what makes up a credit score](/blog/what-makes-up-a-credit-score-and-what-doesnt/) and [credit utilization explained](/blog/credit-utilization-explained/) before chasing points. If you are building credit from scratch, see [secured credit cards explained](/blog/secured-credit-cards-explained-how-they-work-and-when-to-graduate/).

## The first cost to understand: the foreign transaction fee

A foreign transaction fee is charged when you pay in a foreign currency. According to The Points Guy's guide to the fee, the standard charge is about 3%. Credit Karma, in an article updated on October 30, 2026, gives a range of roughly 2.7% to 3%, about $3 on every $100.

Where that fee comes from is worth knowing. The Points Guy says the card network (Visa or Mastercard) typically adds about 1%, and many US banks add another 1% to 2% on top, which together make up the total you see on your statement.

Two things that surprise people:

- **It can apply at home.** Credit Karma notes the fee can also hit some purchases made in your own country, such as certain online orders from overseas stores, because the payment is routed through a foreign bank.
- **It is only one of two costs.** The exchange rate itself is the other. Your card network's rate is generally close to the market rate, but any extra markup by a shop or ATM is not covered by a no-fee card, as the next section explains.

### Cards that avoid the fee

The Points Guy's guide says some issuers waive the fee across their whole lineup, naming Capital One and Discover as having none, and that most top travel rewards cards do not charge it either. It also says some everyday cash-back cards, including well-known ones, tend to charge it. Terms change, and the article is from 2025, so check your own card's current terms. The fee is usually listed in the card's rates and fees table.

### Always pay in the local currency

At a shop or ATM abroad, the terminal may ask whether you want to be charged in your home currency instead of the local one. That is called dynamic currency conversion. The Points Guy's advice is blunt: decline it, and pay in the local currency, since the rate is usually poor. A zero-fee card does not protect you from that markup. For the full explanation, see [dynamic currency conversion: why to decline it](/blog/dynamic-currency-conversion-why-to-decline-it-and-how-to-avoid-it/).

## How points and miles work

Rewards cards pay you back in a rewards currency, not cash. In general terms:

- **Points** are often flexible. A card's points may be redeemable for travel, statement credits or gift cards, and some cards let you transfer them to airline and hotel loyalty programs. Credit Karma notes that some cards offer transfers to well over a dozen partner programs.
- **Miles** usually belong to a single airline's program, or to a card that pays out in a travel currency.
- **Cash back** is the simplest: a percentage of your spending returned as money.

The sources I used do not explain the formal difference between points and miles, so treat that split as a general rule of thumb, not a legal definition. What matters is what your card's points are actually worth when you use them, and that depends on the program, the route and how you redeem. This post quotes no per-point values on purpose, since they change and vary by how you spend them.

## The part that costs money: annual fees

Annual fees on travel cards vary widely. Credit Karma lists cards from the low double digits up to premium cards with much higher fees, and says a high fee only makes sense if you travel often enough to use the perks. In its words, a card with a fee can cost more than it returns if you don't.

Perks that sound valuable only count if you would have bought them anyway. A lounge pass is worth nothing to someone who never flies. A statement credit for a travel purchase is only worth the face amount if you would have spent that money regardless.

## Welcome bonuses: read the spending requirement

Many cards offer a large bonus after you spend a set amount within a fixed window, often a few months. Credit Karma's advice is to take a bonus only when the spending requirement matches what you already spend. Otherwise, you may end up buying things you would not normally buy, which defeats the purpose.

## A worked example: does the card pay for itself?

The numbers below are invented to show the method. They are not any real card.

Say a card has a $95 annual fee, no foreign transaction fee, and gives 2 points per dollar. You expect to spend $4,000 a year on it, including $1,500 abroad.

- **Rewards:** $4,000 × 2 points = 8,000 points. If you redeem them at a value of 1 cent each, that is $80.
- **Foreign fees avoided:** a typical card with a 3% fee would have charged $45 on the $1,500 spent abroad, so the zero-fee feature is worth about $45 to you.
- **Total value:** $80 + $45 = $125.
- **Net after the $95 fee:** about $30.

Now change one input. If you only spend $2,000 on the card, the points are worth $40, the foreign-fee saving is still $45 if the same $1,500 is abroad, and the total is $85. That is less than the $95 fee, and the card has cost you $10.

Two lessons follow. First, your own spending decides whether a card works, not the headline offer. Second, a no-fee card with fewer rewards can easily beat a premium card you barely use. Compare it with a card that has no annual fee and no foreign fee before you decide.

## What to check before you apply

1. **Foreign transaction fee.** Is it zero, and does that apply to every card in the issuer's range?
2. **Annual fee.** Can you name the perks you would actually use, and add them up honestly?
3. **Rewards rate and how you redeem.** Flexible points, airline miles or cash back?
4. **Bonus requirement.** Does it match spending you were already going to do?
5. **Interest rate.** If you ever carry a balance, rewards will not cover it.
6. **Networks.** Credit Karma recommends carrying cards on at least two major networks, with Visa the best bet for acceptance, since no card works everywhere.
7. **Your country's rules.** Fees, consumer protections and card availability differ outside the US, so check local terms.

## Where people actually go wrong

- **Paying only the minimum.** Interest wipes out any reward.
- **Chasing a bonus with spending you would not otherwise do.**
- **Keeping a premium card for perks you never use.** Review the card each year before the fee posts.
- **Accepting the "pay in your home currency" prompt** at a terminal because it feels familiar.
- **Carrying one card only.** If it is declined, blocked or lost, you have no backup. Having two cards on different networks, as Credit Karma suggests, costs nothing.
- **Forgetting that points can lose value.** Programs change the rules, so do not stockpile far beyond what you can use soon.

## Getting started

1. **List how much you spend abroad in a typical year,** and how much at home.
2. **Check your current cards' foreign fee** in their terms.
3. **Do the break-even sum** from the worked example with your own numbers.
4. **Pick the simplest card that wins on that math,** not the one with the flashiest perks.
5. **Pay the balance in full every month,** or skip rewards cards entirely.
6. **Decline home-currency conversion** every time you are asked abroad.
