The Central Provident Fund, or CPF, is Singapore's compulsory savings scheme. Part of every paycheque, and a matching share from the employer, goes into personal accounts that can only be used for specific purposes: housing, healthcare and retirement. The money earns government-set interest and is largely locked until later in life. It is one of the best-known national retirement systems in the world, and a common comparison for people studying systems elsewhere, such as [India's EPF and PPF](/blog/epf-vs-ppf-indias-retirement-savings-basics-explained/), [Australia's superannuation](/blog/how-superannuation-works-australias-retirement-savings-explained/) or [Canada's TFSA and RRSP](/blog/tfsa-vs-rrsp-canadas-tax-advantaged-accounts-explained/).

This guide explains how it works at a general level, using the CPF Board's own pages as the source. CPF rates, ceilings and rules are updated regularly, so use the CPF website for the current numbers before you plan anything.

## Who this is for, and who it isn't

**It's for:** anyone curious about how CPF works, including people moving to Singapore, those reading about it, or readers comparing national retirement systems.

**It isn't** personal financial or retirement planning advice. The CPF Board's page names **Singapore Citizens and Permanent Residents** as the people CPF helps. It does not say whether foreign workers are covered, so if that applies to you, check the CPF Board directly.

## The four accounts

According to the CPF Board:

- **Ordinary Account (OA).** Used mainly to buy property and pay housing loans. The Board advises keeping some OA savings for emergencies.
- **MediSave Account (MA).** Used for hospital stays, day surgery and some outpatient costs, including for dependants, and to pay premiums for approved healthcare insurance such as MediShield Life and CareShield Life.
- **Special Account (SA).** Used for retirement savings. Members can top up the SA or Retirement Account with cash or CPF savings. The SA is closed at age 55.
- **Retirement Account (RA).** Created at age 55 when the SA closes. It holds the retirement sum used for retirement payouts and for joining CPF LIFE.

## How much goes in

Contribution rates range from 12.5% to 37% of monthly wages, depending on age. For monthly wages above S$750, the rates from 1 January 2026 are:

| Age | Employer | Employee | Total |
|---|---|---|---|
| 55 and below | 17% | 20% | 37% |
| Above 55 to 60 | 16% | 18% | 34% |
| Above 60 to 65 | 12.5% | 12.5% | 25% |
| Above 65 to 70 | 9% | 7.5% | 16.5% |
| Above 70 | 7.5% | 5% | 12.5% |

How each contribution is divided between the accounts depends on age, and there is a monthly wage ceiling above which wages are not counted. The CPF Board publishes the current allocation rates and ceiling, so check those. Self-employed people must contribute to MediSave.

## The interest

The CPF Board's 22 September 2026 release gives the rates for 1 October to 31 December 2026:

- **Ordinary Account:** 2.5% a year.
- **Special, MediSave and Retirement Accounts:** 4% a year.
- The government also extended a **4% floor** on interest for the Special, MediSave and Retirement accounts through 31 December 2027.

On top of this, the Board's December 2025 release describes **extra interest**:

- Members **below 55** get an extra 1% on the first S$60,000 of their combined CPF balances, with up to S$20,000 from the OA counting toward that limit.
- Members **55 and above** get an extra 2% on the first S$30,000 and an extra 1% on the next S$30,000, with the same S$20,000 OA limit.
- Extra interest earned on OA balances goes into the SA or RA.

Rates are reviewed every quarter and the rules change, so treat these numbers as correct for the dates given and check the current quarter's rates.

## Housing, healthcare and withdrawals

- **Housing.** OA savings can be used for property purchases and housing loan payments, within rules the Board sets.
- **Healthcare.** MediSave pays for approved medical costs, up to limits. The Board's Basic Healthcare Sum for 2026 is S$79,000 for members below 65 (up from S$75,500), and it is the level up to which MediSave balances earn the standard interest rules.
- **Retirement.** From age 55, members can withdraw savings above the applicable retirement sum, in full or in part, after setting that sum aside in the Retirement Account. After age 65, members can apply for **CPF LIFE**, which pays a lifelong monthly income.
- **Top-ups.** Members can top up their SA, RA or MA with cash. The Board's overview also describes government matching schemes for retirement and MediSave top-ups, with annual and lifetime limits, so check which apply to you.

## A worked example

This uses the rates above and is illustrative only. Someone aged 30 earns S$4,000 a month, below the wage ceiling.

**Contributions:** the employee pays 20% = **S$800**, the employer adds 17% = **S$680**, so S$1,480 goes in each month, which is 37%.

**Interest on a small balance:** suppose they have S$10,000 in the OA and S$10,000 in the SA.

- OA: 10,000 × 2.5% = **S$250**
- SA: 10,000 × 4% = **S$400**
- Extra interest: 1% on the combined S$20,000 (within the S$60,000 limit and the S$20,000 OA limit) = **S$200**
- Total: 250 + 400 + 200 = **S$850**, which is **4.25%** of the S$20,000.

This is a simplified view. In practice the Board credits interest monthly and compounds it yearly, and the extra interest is credited to the SA, so the real figures will differ slightly. The point is how the layers combine.

## What CPF means for people outside Singapore

CPF is a useful case study even if you will never use it. It shows one design for national retirement saving: compulsory contributions, purpose-limited accounts, government-set interest and a lifelong payout option. Other countries make different choices, such as employer pensions, individual tax-advantaged accounts or pay-as-you-go state pensions. See [UK ISAs explained](/blog/uk-isas-explained-how-tax-free-saving-works/) and [Japan's NISA program](/blog/japans-nisa-program-explained-tax-free-investing-basics/) for other approaches.

## Common mistakes

- **Treating the rates as fixed.** The Board reviews them every quarter.
- **Forgetting the accounts have different jobs.** Money in the OA, SA and MA is not interchangeable.
- **Counting on withdrawing everything at 55.** There is a retirement sum to set aside first.
- **Ignoring the wage ceiling and age bands** when estimating contributions.
- **Using OA money for housing without thinking about retirement.** Housing use can reduce what is left for later, so check the Board's guidance.
- **Relying on a blog (including this one) for current figures.** Always check CPF.gov.sg.

## Getting started

1. **Read the CPF Board's overview and the current quarter's interest rates** on its website.
2. **Check the current contribution and allocation rates** for your age, and the monthly wage ceiling.
3. **Decide how you want to use the OA:** housing, savings or a mix.
4. **Look into voluntary top-ups and matching schemes** if you are eligible.
5. **Plan around CPF LIFE** by understanding the retirement sum that will be set aside at 55.
