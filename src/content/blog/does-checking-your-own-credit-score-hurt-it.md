Plenty of people avoid looking at their credit because they're afraid the act of looking will cost them points. It won't. The confusion comes from the fact that "credit inquiry" covers two very different things, and only one of them can touch your score.

## Who this is for, and who it isn't

**It's for:** anyone who has put off checking their credit, or who is about to apply for a card, car loan, or mortgage and wants to avoid unforced mistakes.

**It's not for:** people looking for ways to remove accurate negative items, which inquiries are not. Nothing here is personalized financial advice.

## The short answer

**Checking your own score or credit report is a soft inquiry, and it has no effect on your score.** You can do it as often as you like. What can lower your score is a **hard inquiry**, which happens when you apply for new credit and a lender pulls your file to decide whether to approve you.

## Soft inquiries: no effect on your score

A soft inquiry means someone looked at your credit without you applying for new credit. The common ones:

- You checking your own score or report, including through a bank or card app
- Credit monitoring services
- Prequalified or preapproved offers, where a lender screens you before you apply
- Insurance quotes
- Employer background checks, which you authorize
- Your existing lenders reviewing your account

None of these change your score. Some reports may list them in a section that only you can see, and lenders don't use them when scoring you.

## Hard inquiries: a small, temporary dent

A hard inquiry happens when you apply for credit and give permission for the lender to check your file. Typical examples are a credit card, auto loan, personal loan, or mortgage application. Some rental, phone, and utility applications can also trigger one, depending on the company.

Here's what sources generally agree on:

- **The point cost is usually small.** FICO has said that for most people one additional inquiry takes fewer than five points off. A few consumer-finance sources put it at roughly 5 to 10, and the effect can be larger if you have a thin credit file or several inquiries bunched together. Treat these as ranges, not promises.
- **It affects your score for a limited time.** FICO generally counts inquiries from the past 12 months, and the effect typically fades within a few months.
- **It stays on your report longer.** A hard inquiry can remain on your credit report for up to two years, even though it stops counting toward the score sooner.
- **Inquiries sit in the "new credit" category,** which makes up about 10% of a FICO score. Payment history and utilization matter far more. See [what hurts your credit score](/blog/what-hurts-your-credit-score/) for the full weighting.

## Why several applications can hurt more than one

Scoring models read a burst of new applications as a sign you may be taking on more debt than you can manage. That's why opening several credit cards in a short window can cost more than a single application, and why [secured credit cards](/blog/secured-credit-cards-explained-how-they-work-and-when-to-graduate/) are best added one at a time.

## The exception: shopping for one loan

If you're comparing offers for a mortgage, auto loan, or student loan, scoring models usually treat multiple inquiries for the same type of loan, made within a short window, as a single inquiry. The window depends on the scoring model:

| Scoring model | Typical window |
|---|---|
| Newer FICO versions | About 45 days |
| Older FICO versions | About 14 days |
| VantageScore | About 14 days |

Because you can't always tell which version a lender uses, the safe move is to do all your loan shopping within **14 days**. This bundling generally doesn't apply to credit cards or personal loans, where each application is its own hard inquiry.

## Prequalification is not the same as applying

Many lenders let you check whether you'd likely qualify with a soft inquiry, then pull your credit for real only when you submit a full application. That's a good way to compare options without a dent. Remember that prequalification isn't a guarantee: the final application can still involve a hard inquiry and a different decision.

A credit limit increase request is a gray area. Some issuers use a soft check and others use a hard one, so ask before you request one, especially if a mortgage is coming up.

## A worked example

Priya wants to know where she stands before buying a car. She checks her score in her bank app three times in one month, and her score doesn't move, since each check is a soft inquiry. She then applies for auto loans with three lenders over ten days. Under most scoring models those three pulls count as one inquiry, so the impact is small. A month later she applies for three store credit cards in one afternoon to get discounts, and that creates three separate hard inquiries, which can cost more points and looks riskier to lenders.

## Where people actually go wrong

- **Avoiding their own credit report.** Not looking doesn't protect your score, and it means you won't spot errors or fraud.
- **Mixing up soft and hard.** "Check my rate" with a soft pull is not the same as submitting an application.
- **Spreading loan shopping over months.** That turns one inquiry into several.
- **Applying for many cards at once.** Each card application counts separately.
- **Ignoring a hard inquiry they don't recognize.** An inquiry for an account you never applied for can be a sign of identity theft. If you see one, follow the steps in [how to dispute an error on your credit report](/blog/how-to-dispute-an-error-on-your-credit-report/).

## Getting started

Check your own credit freely. AnnualCreditReport.com is the official source for free reports from Equifax, Experian, and TransUnion, and the bureaus have offered them weekly on a permanent basis since October 2023. Space out new applications, and keep any loan shopping inside a tight window. To build the habits that matter more than inquiries, start with [how to build credit from scratch](/blog/how-to-build-credit-from-scratch/) and [credit utilization explained](/blog/credit-utilization-explained/).
