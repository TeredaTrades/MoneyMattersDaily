A coin is "about to explode." A government is "banning" crypto. A famous person "just bought in." Crypto news travels fast, and a lot of what looks like news is really someone trying to move you to act: to buy, to join a group, or to click a link. Reading it well is less about being clever and more about slowing down and asking a few questions.

This post is about reading the news, not about whether to invest. It makes no predictions and recommends no coins.

## Who this is for, and who it isn't

**It's for:** beginners who see crypto headlines on social media or in group chats and want to know what to trust.

**It's not for:** anyone looking for tips on what to buy or when. This is general education, not financial advice, and crypto can lose value quickly.

## Start with who is talking, and why

Before you read the claim, ask who is making it and what they gain.

- **A news outlet** reporting an event should say where the facts came from.
- **A promoter** may own the coin, be paid to talk about it, or run a group that profits if you buy.
- **An anonymous account** gives you nothing to check.

Paid promotion is a long-standing problem. The SEC warned celebrities and social influencers back in 2017 that anyone promoting an investment must disclose "the nature, source, and amount of compensation paid, directly or indirectly, by the company," per a law firm's summary of that warning. In 2023 the SEC charged eight celebrities for promoting crypto on social media without disclosing they were paid, according to a Motley Fool report, and six agreed to pay $400,000 in settlements without admitting or denying the charges. The lesson isn't that every endorsement is a scam. It's that **a famous face is not research, and a missing disclosure is a warning sign**.

## Hype versus news: a quick test

| Looks like news | Often hype if... |
|---|---|
| "Coin X partners with a big company" | There's no statement from the company itself |
| "Price jumps 300% today" | It's presented as a reason to buy, not a fact to understand |
| "Experts say it's going to the moon" | The experts aren't named, or they own the coin |
| "Limited-time opportunity" | There's a countdown, a deadline, or "buy now" |
| "Guaranteed returns" | Always. Nothing in investing is guaranteed |

If a story is real, you can usually find it reported elsewhere, by outlets that don't benefit from your purchase, and the company or regulator involved will confirm it on its own website. If you can only find it in the same group chat or on accounts that all repeat each other, treat it as unconfirmed.

## The pump-and-dump pattern

One of the oldest tricks in crypto is the **pump-and-dump**. The US Commodity Futures Trading Commission (CFTC) describes it this way: organizers inflate the price of a thinly traded or new coin, then sell their own holdings at the peak, leaving remaining investors holding assets that may be nearly worthless.

The CFTC's advisory says these schemes often run through chat groups with thousands of members. The organizers announce a countdown, name the coin and the exchange, and urge members to promote it on social media and invite others. Some even use fake news stories about well-known companies supposedly partnering with the coin. The whole cycle can finish in minutes, and the organizers typically exit first.

The CFTC lists these red flags:

- A buy recommendation based on a single tip, especially on social media.
- A sudden price spike.
- Ads or sites promising quick wealth from specific coins or tokens.
- Countdowns, "buy now" urgency, or promotion through fake news.
- Claims of guaranteed returns or no risk of loss.

Its advice: research the coins, the companies behind them and the claims, don't buy on a single tip or social media hype, and don't take part in pump-and-dump trades, which it calls market manipulation and illegal. Notice who benefits when you read a "news" post that checks several of these boxes: if it needs you to hurry, someone else is probably already positioned to sell to you.

## Scams that arrive as friendly messages

Not every trap is a headline. A common one starts with a message from a stranger who seems friendly and slowly steers you toward a "great" crypto platform. The CFTC calls this a relationship confidence scam, also known as "pig butchering," and in a July 2024 advisory it warned that victims are often targeted again, by fake "fund recovery" services.

Its advice, in plain terms:

- Never send crypto or sensitive information to someone you only know online.
- Be cautious about unsolicited calls, texts or emails from people you haven't contacted.
- Check that a trading platform is registered where it claims to be. In the US the CFTC points to its own lookup tool, and you should check the regulator for your own country.
- Don't trade products you don't understand.
- Be wary of anyone who offers to recover lost money for an upfront fee.
- Treat "easy part-time work" that involves moving money through your accounts or wallets as a warning sign of money laundering.

The same pattern shows up in other payment scams; see [gift cards and your budget](/blog/gift-cards-and-your-budget-fees-rules-and-scams/) for how the "pay me in an unusual way" request works.

## Reading regulation headlines

Regulation is real news, and it's also easy to misread.

- **"Bill passes" is not "law in effect."** Legislation often has a delay before it applies, and agencies then write detailed rules. A US stablecoin law passed in 2025, for example, takes effect only on the earlier of 18 months after enactment or 120 days after final regulations, per a law firm's summary, and a September 2026 report said the rules had been delayed. See [multi-currency wallets compared](/blog/best-multi-currency-wallets-compared-one-account-for-several-currencies/) for how that looks in practice.
- **Look at the country.** A ban in one country, a license in another and a tax rule in a third are different stories. Check which country the headline is about, and which one you live in.
- **"Court case" is not "final verdict."** Charges, settlements and rulings are steps in a process. Look for what was actually decided, and what the party denied.
- **A regulator's own site beats a summary.** Where you can, read the announcement itself, or at least check who wrote the summary and whether they have a stake.

Rules differ by country, and what's allowed, taxed or banned changes. When a post tells you what the law is, look for the date and the source.

## A worked example

Here's a made-up headline in a group chat: *"BREAKING: FooCoin partners with a major bank. Price up 400% in an hour. Buy before it's gone."*

Run the checklist:

1. **Who's saying it?** An anonymous account, with no link.
2. **Is it confirmed?** Search for the bank's own announcement. Nothing there.
3. **What's the pressure?** "Before it's gone" is urgency, a red flag on the CFTC's list.
4. **What does the price spike mean?** A sudden spike is another listed red flag, and a price that has already jumped 400% means early holders are positioned to sell.
5. **What happens if you wait a day?** You lose nothing if it's real, and avoid a loss if it isn't.

The first four checks all fail. The right move isn't a clever trade. It's to do nothing.

## A calmer way to follow crypto news

- **Wait before acting.** Real news is still true tomorrow.
- **Confirm with a second, unrelated source,** ideally the company or regulator itself.
- **Separate facts from forecasts.** "The regulator approved X" is a fact. "This will make you rich" is a pitch.
- **Mute the hype accounts.** Following price chatter all day makes impulsive decisions more likely.
- **Decide in advance how much you could lose,** and never go past it. For the basics, see [what is cryptocurrency: how it works and the risks](/blog/what-is-cryptocurrency-how-it-works-and-the-risks/).
- **Remember that swings are normal in markets.** For a broader view of reacting to volatility, see [what stock market volatility means for your savings](/blog/what-stock-market-volatility-means-for-your-savings/).

## Where people actually go wrong

- **Trusting a famous name.** Endorsements can be paid and aren't research.
- **Acting on one tip.** The CFTC specifically warns against it.
- **Mistaking a price jump for proof.** A rise tells you what happened, not why or what's next.
- **Believing "guaranteed" or "risk-free."** Be skeptical of anyone who says so.
- **Joining a group to "get in early."** Those groups are where pump-and-dumps are organized.
- **Paying to "recover" lost money.** Upfront fees for recovery are a known second scam.
- **Treating a proposed rule as settled law.** Check whether it's in force, and where.

## Getting started

1. **Pick two or three sources** that report with named authors and links, and a regulator's site for your country.
2. **Use the checklist above** on any story that makes you want to act.
3. **Write down your limits first:** how much, if anything, you'd put in, and what would make you stop.
4. **Report suspected fraud** to your local regulator or police. In the US, the CFTC lists a complaint line and web page for this.
