The idea that investing requires a few thousand dollars to "get started" is left over from a version of brokerages that mostly doesn't exist anymore. Fractional shares mean $20 can buy a slice of an index fund or a single expensive stock. The real barrier for most people isn't the dollar amount — it's not knowing the order to do things in.

## The order that actually matters

1. **Employer match first, if there is one.** A 401(k) match is an immediate, guaranteed return — often 50%–100% on whatever you contribute up to the match — before a single dollar is invested in anything. No index fund return competes with that.
2. **A small emergency cushion before anything else.** Not a full 3–6 months yet — even $500–$1,000 set aside first means a car repair or a broken phone doesn't force selling investments at a bad time to cover it.
3. **High-interest debt before investing further.** A credit card at 22%–29% APR is a guaranteed negative return larger than almost any realistic investment gain — paying that down is the higher-return move, dollar for dollar.
4. **Then investing, in whatever's left.** This is the step most "how to invest" content starts at, skipping the three that come first — which is why so much of it doesn't hold up for someone with a small, irregular amount to work with.

## What "little money" actually buys now

**Worked example:** $20 a week, invested every payday into a broad index fund, is roughly $1,040 a year. At a long-run historical average stock market return (which varies year to year and isn't guaranteed), that grows meaningfully over a decade mostly through consistency, not the size of any single contribution. The mechanism that does the work here is showing up regularly, not timing a large lump sum correctly.

Two things make this realistic at small dollar amounts that didn't widely exist a decade ago:

- **Fractional shares.** Most major brokers now let you buy a dollar amount of a stock or fund instead of needing to afford a full share — a $20 order buys $20 worth, whatever the share price is.
- **No or low account minimums.** Many brokerage accounts and Roth IRAs have $0 minimums to open, and no-fee trading is now standard at most major brokers — the "$1,000 to start" account minimum common a decade ago is mostly gone.

## Where the money actually goes first

For most people starting small, a **Roth IRA** is worth prioritizing over a plain taxable brokerage account, once any employer match is captured: contributions grow tax-free, and — unlike a 401(k) — Roth IRA contributions (not earnings) can be withdrawn without penalty if there's ever a genuine emergency, which lowers the stakes of starting before a full emergency fund exists. Inside it, a low-cost broad index fund is a reasonable default for the same reason it is at any dollar amount — see our [index funds explained](/blog/index-funds-explained-for-beginners/) post for how that actually works.

## Common mistakes

- **Waiting to "have enough" to start.** The cost of waiting is lost time compounding, not lost money — a small amount invested for 10 years usually outperforms a larger amount invested for 5, even at the same return rate.
- **Picking individual stocks first.** Concentrated bets are the highest-risk way to invest a small amount, since a single bad pick has nothing to be offset by. A broad fund spreads that risk automatically.
- **Investing money needed within 1–2 years.** A market downturn at the wrong moment can force selling at a loss — short-horizon money belongs in savings, not the market, regardless of how small the amount is.
- **Stopping contributions after a market drop.** The instinct to pause when prices fall works against the strategy — buying consistently through a dip means each contribution buys more shares at a lower price, not fewer.

## Who this is for — and who should wait

This is squarely for someone with steady income, no high-interest debt outstanding, and at least a small cushion set aside — the $20-a-week approach works because it's consistent, not because it's small. It's a weaker fit for someone still carrying high-interest credit card debt, where paying that down is very likely the better-returning move first, or someone without any savings buffer at all, where a single emergency could force cashing out at a loss.
