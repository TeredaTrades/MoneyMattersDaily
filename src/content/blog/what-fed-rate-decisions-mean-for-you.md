Federal Reserve rate decisions make headlines several times a year, but the coverage usually stays at the "markets reacted" level. Here's what actually changes in a household budget.

## The short version

The Fed sets a target range for the rate banks charge each other overnight. That single number ripples out into almost every interest rate you deal with — it doesn't set your mortgage rate or credit card APR directly, but it's the anchor they're priced against.

## What moves quickly

- **Variable-rate debt.** Credit cards and home equity lines of credit are usually pegged to a benchmark that tracks the Fed's rate closely — expect changes to show up in your statement within a billing cycle or two.
- **Savings accounts and CDs**, especially at online banks that compete on yield — these tend to move in step with the Fed, sometimes within days.

## What moves slowly, or not at all

- **Existing fixed-rate mortgages** don't change — that's the whole point of locking a rate.
- **New mortgage rates** react more to longer-term bond markets and expectations about *future* Fed moves than to any single announcement, so a rate cut doesn't always mean cheaper mortgages the next day.

## What to actually do with this information

- If you're carrying variable-rate debt, a rising-rate environment is the moment to prioritize paying it down.
- If you're holding cash, shop your savings rate after a hike — legacy big-bank savings accounts often don't pass the increase on to you unless you ask (or switch).
- If you're mortgage shopping, track the trend over months, not the reaction to any single Fed meeting.

Rate decisions are one of the few financial news events that map onto real numbers in your own accounts — worth understanding the mechanism once, rather than re-parsing the headlines every time.
