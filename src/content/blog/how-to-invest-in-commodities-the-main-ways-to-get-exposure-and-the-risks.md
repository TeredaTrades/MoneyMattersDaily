Oil spikes, gold hits a headline, wheat gets expensive after a bad harvest. It's natural to wonder whether you could invest in the thing itself, instead of just paying more for it at the store or the pump.

You can, but "buying a commodity" is not as simple as buying a share. There are several different routes, and each one behaves differently from the price you see in the news. This post explains the main ways to get exposure to commodities, what each one actually holds, and the risks that catch beginners out.

This is education, not a recommendation. It names no commodity to buy, makes no price predictions, and commodity investments can lose a large part of their value.

## Who this is for, and who it isn't

**It's for:** beginners who keep hearing about oil, gold, metals or farm goods as "investments" and want to understand the options before touching one.

**It isn't for:** active traders looking for strategies. It also doesn't cover individual products, since fees and availability change and differ by country. For the broader fund basics, see [ETFs vs. mutual funds](/blog/etfs-vs-mutual-funds-whats-the-difference-and-which-fits-you/).

## What a commodity is

A commodity is a raw material that is bought and sold in standard grades, so one unit is much like another. The usual groups are:

- **Energy:** crude oil, natural gas.
- **Metals:** gold and silver (often called precious metals), plus copper and other industrial metals.
- **Agriculture:** wheat, corn, coffee, sugar and similar crops.

Each group is driven by different forces. The CFTC, the US regulator for futures markets, notes that energy risks often relate to supply and storage, agricultural risks to weather, and metals risks to industrial and economic conditions. That matters because "commodities" is not one asset behaving one way.

## The main ways to get exposure

### 1. Owning the physical commodity

This is the most direct route, and for most people it only makes sense for a few metals, usually coins or bars. You own the actual item.

- **What you get:** exposure to the metal's price, with no fund or company standing in between.
- **What it costs:** dealers sell at a premium and buy back at a discount, so there is a gap between the price you pay and the price you could sell at. You also need to store it safely and may want to insure it.
- **Not practical for:** oil, natural gas and crops. Nobody stores barrels or bushels in a spare room.

### 2. Futures contracts

A **futures contract** is an agreement to buy or sell a set quantity of something at a set price on a future date. Futures are how the commodity market itself works. They are also high-risk for individuals, because they can involve borrowed money and fast losses.

The CFTC points out that a futures contract doesn't give you ownership of the underlying commodity, and that contracts expire. That is why most people who want futures-style exposure use a fund instead of trading contracts themselves. Direct futures trading is outside the scope of this beginner post.

### 3. Commodity funds and exchange-traded products

Many commodity funds are traded like stocks, so they look easy to buy. Underneath, they come in a few different structures, and the differences matter:

- **Funds that hold the physical metal.** Some gold and silver funds hold bars in storage, so the fund's price is meant to follow the metal. This is the closest a fund gets to owning the commodity.
- **Futures-based funds (commodity pools).** Most oil, gas and farm-crop funds don't hold barrels or grain. They hold futures contracts. FINRA's investor-protection notice on oil-linked products explains that these funds usually track futures rather than the spot (immediate) price, because holding the commodity directly is often impractical.
- **Exchange-traded notes (ETNs).** An ETN is not a fund. According to the SEC's investor bulletin, it's an unsecured debt obligation of a financial institution that pays a return linked to an index. If the issuer fails, you may lose your entire investment, no matter what the index did.

### 4. Stocks of companies that produce commodities

Instead of the commodity, you can own shares in miners, oil companies or large farm businesses, often through a broad fund. This is a different bet. A company's share price depends on its management, debts, costs, taxes and the wider stock market, not just on the commodity price. A gold miner can fall while gold rises, and the reverse.

## Why a commodity fund may not follow the price in the news

This is the single most important thing to understand about futures-based products.

A futures contract expires. To keep their exposure, these funds close the contract that's about to expire and buy one that expires later. This is called **rolling**.

- When later contracts cost **more** than the expiring one (a market condition known as **contango**), each roll can lose money, because the fund sells low and buys higher.
- When later contracts cost **less** (known as **backwardation**), rolling can add to returns.

The CFTC's advisory says small increases at each roll can add up and cut into returns, especially once fees are included. FINRA's notice says that over longer periods these dynamics can cause large gaps between a fund's performance and the spot price, and that some investors mistakenly treated oil-linked products as a stand-in for the oil price.

**A simple illustration (made-up numbers).** Imagine a fund that must swap an expiring contract priced at $100 for the next one priced at $102 each month. Each swap costs about 2% of the position, before fees. After a year of the same pattern, that drag could be roughly 20% or more, even if the "spot" price you see on the news hasn't moved at all. Real markets change month to month, so this is only to show how roll costs compound, not a forecast.

So a headline like "oil is up 10% this year" does not mean a futures-based oil fund is up 10%. It can be up less or down.

## Leveraged and inverse products

Some products aim to deliver two or three times the daily move of a commodity, or the opposite of it. The SEC's bulletin on ETNs warns that leveraged and inverse products reset daily, so holding them for more than one day can produce returns very different from what the label suggests, and they're not generally designed for buy-and-hold. FINRA says leveraged and inverse oil products are especially unsuitable for buy-and-hold investors. If you're a beginner, treat them as out of scope.

## Costs and details to check before you buy anything

- **Structure:** is it physical metal, futures, an ETN, or company stock? The name alone often doesn't say.
- **Fees:** the fund's annual cost, plus the dealer or broker spread.
- **Tracking:** read how closely the product tries to follow the commodity, and over what period (a day, or longer).
- **Issuer risk:** for an ETN, who stands behind it?
- **Taxes:** treatment varies by country and by structure. In the US, for example, funds holding physical metal, futures-based funds and ETNs can each be taxed under different rules, and some futures-based funds issue a K-1 form instead of a standard tax form. Rates and rules change, so check your country's tax authority or a tax professional.
- **Registration:** the CFTC suggests confirming the fund operator's registration and reading the disclosure documents.

## Where people actually go wrong

- **Treating a commodity fund as a price tracker.** A futures-based fund is not the same as owning the commodity.
- **Buying after a big headline.** Prices that have already jumped can drop just as fast.
- **Holding a short-term or leveraged product for months.** These aren't built for it.
- **Ignoring the issuer in an ETN.** The credit risk is real.
- **Assuming commodities always protect against a downturn.** The CFTC says to be skeptical of claims that commodity pools will outperform during recessions.
- **Putting in money you may need soon.** Commodity prices can swing sharply.
- **Skipping the storage and spread costs of physical metal.**

## Is a commodity investment necessary?

No. Many long-term investors hold only broad stock and bond funds, and a diversified stock fund already holds many companies that use or produce commodities. Commodity exposure is an optional extra, not a requirement. ## Getting started

1. **Decide why you want it.** Curiosity, diversification, and a hunch about prices are very different reasons, and only the first two are worth considering.
2. **Set an amount you could lose without harm,** and keep it small relative to your savings.
3. **Pick the structure on purpose:** physical metal, a fund that holds the metal, a futures-based fund, an ETN, or company stocks.
4. **Read the fund's prospectus or the ETN's disclosure,** especially the sections on how it rolls contracts, its fees, and its risks.
5. **Check the tax treatment** in your country before buying.
6. **Be wary of anyone promising returns,** and of unregulated "commodity trading" offers that arrive by message or social media. Compare with the red flags in [what is cryptocurrency](/blog/what-is-cryptocurrency-how-it-works-and-the-risks/), which apply here too.
