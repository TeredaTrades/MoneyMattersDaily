Most people don't fail to save because they don't want to. They fail because "save more" has no start, no amount and no end. A savings challenge fixes that. It gives you a number to move, a schedule to follow and a finish line, which makes it easier to begin and easier to tell whether you're on track.

The best-known version is the 52-week challenge, which asks for $1 in week one, $2 in week two and so on up to $52, for a year-end total of $1,378. That's a long run for a first try. This post shows six-month versions instead, with the arithmetic worked out so you can see the real cost of each before you commit.

All amounts use a dollar sign for illustration. Swap in your own currency and scale the numbers to your income.

## Who this is for, and who it isn't

**It's for:** people who want to build a saving habit with a clear plan, or who have tried open-ended saving and let it fade.

**It isn't a substitute for a budget.** A challenge only works if the money you commit is actually free after rent, food and bills. If you don't have a budget yet, start with [how to make a budget for beginners](/blog/how-to-make-a-budget-for-beginners/), then pick a template.

## What a savings challenge does, and doesn't, do

The totals below are plain arithmetic. A challenge doesn't make your money grow faster or make a goal easier to afford. What it does is take a decision you'd otherwise remake every payday and make it once.

I couldn't find a trustworthy study that measures how many people finish these challenges, so treat any claim that they "double your savings" with suspicion. The honest case is simpler: a fixed, automatic plan beats a vague intention.

## Five templates, with the numbers

Six months is about 26 weeks, so the weekly templates run for 26 weeks. The monthly ones run for 6.

### 1. The weekly ladder (adds a little each week)

Save $2 in week one, $4 in week two, and so on, adding $2 each week until you reach $52 in week 26.

| Week | Deposit | Saved so far |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | $2 | $2 |
| 4 | $8 | $20 |
| 8 | $16 | $72 |
| 13 | $26 | $182 |
| 20 | $40 | $420 |
| 26 | $52 | $702 |

You can scale it by changing the step. A $1 step gives $351 at the end, with a peak week of $26. A $5 step gives $1,755, with a peak week of $130.

**The catch:** it starts easy and ends hard. The last four weeks alone cost $196 on the $2 step, against $20 for the first four. If your pay is tight in the second half of the challenge, this template is the one most likely to break.

### 2. The reverse ladder (hard first, easy last)

Flip it: start at $52 and drop $2 each week until you reach $2 in week 26. The total is the same $702, but you front-load the hard part while motivation is high, and the plan gets easier as you tire.

NerdWallet's guide to the 52-week version notes that the larger early deposits have more time to earn interest if the money sits in an interest-bearing account, so the reverse order may earn slightly more. Chase's version points out it can also keep big deposits away from the holiday season.

### 3. The flat weekly amount

The same amount every week, nothing to remember. Chase's 52-week guide notes the ladder's total can be matched with a fixed weekly amount ($26.50 a week for the year), and the same idea works here:

| Weekly amount | After 26 weeks |
|---|---|
| $20 | $520 |
| $30 | $780 |
| $40 | $1,040 |
| $50 | $1,300 |

This is the easiest template to automate and the easiest to fit around a fixed budget, because your cash flow never changes. It's also the least "fun," which matters for some people and not at all for others.

### 4. The monthly ladder

Save a base amount in month one, then double it, triple it, and so on. With a $50 base:

| Month | Deposit | Saved so far |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | $50 | $50 |
| 2 | $100 | $150 |
| 3 | $150 | $300 |
| 4 | $200 | $500 |
| 5 | $250 | $750 |
| 6 | $300 | $1,050 |

It suits people paid monthly, and it has only six decisions instead of 26. The same warning applies: the last month is six times the first, so check that you can afford the final deposit before you start.

### 5. The percentage ladder

Instead of a dollar amount, save a growing share of your take-home pay: 1% in month one, 2% in month two, up to 6% in month six. On $3,000 of monthly take-home pay, that works out to:

| Month | Share | Deposit |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 1% | $30 |
| 2 | 2% | $60 |
| 3 | 3% | $90 |
| 4 | 4% | $120 |
| 5 | 5% | $150 |
| 6 | 6% | $180 |

Total: $630. This one adjusts itself if your income changes, which makes it a good fit for [irregular income](/blog/how-to-budget-on-an-irregular-income/). It's also the gentlest on cash flow, and a natural stepping stone toward saving a fixed share every month.

## How to pick the one that fits

1. **Work out your free cash first.** Take your monthly take-home pay, subtract fixed bills and essentials, and see what's left. That's your ceiling.
2. **Check the hardest week or month, not the average.** For the ladders, the peak is the last deposit. If a $52 week would leave you with almost nothing, drop to a $1 step or use a flat amount.
3. **Match it to your pay schedule.** Paid weekly or fortnightly? A weekly template fits. Paid monthly? Use the monthly ladder or the percentage ladder.
4. **Choose by temperament.** Some people like watching a number grow; others want to set it up once and forget it.

## What makes any of them actually work

- **Automate the transfers.** Both Chase and NerdWallet suggest moving the money automatically from checking to savings, rather than relying on remembering. [Best ways to automate your savings](/blog/best-ways-to-automate-your-savings/) covers the options.
- **Keep it in a separate account.** A CFPB announcement of a pilot with a prepaid-card provider (2014) described how people who do their saving and spending from the same account find it hard to keep the two apart, and described moving savings into a separate sub-account that takes a deliberate transfer to withdraw from. The principle holds today: out of sight is easier than willpower.
- **Watch the fees.** NerdWallet recommends a fee-free account, because a monthly maintenance charge can eat a small balance. See [high-yield savings accounts explained](/blog/high-yield-savings-accounts-explained/) and [money market accounts explained](/blog/money-market-accounts-explained-how-they-differ-from-savings-accounts/) for where to park it.
- **Plan for a missed week.** NerdWallet's advice if you can't make a full deposit is to put in whatever you can rather than quit. Decide in advance that a miss means "catch up when you can," not "the challenge is over."
- **Give it a purpose.** A number with no name is easy to raid. Label the account for the goal: a starter emergency fund, a trip, a down payment.

## Where this money should go first

If you have no emergency fund, that's the most common first use. NerdWallet's guide says an emergency fund should come before the challenge if you don't have one, and Chase presents the challenge as a way to build one. [How much emergency fund do you need?](/blog/how-much-emergency-fund/) explains how to set the target. If you're carrying high-interest debt, compare what a challenge earns with what the debt costs before choosing between them, since the interest on debt is usually higher than any savings rate.

For bills that arrive on a schedule, such as insurance or holiday spending, a [sinking fund](/blog/how-to-build-a-sinking-fund-for-irregular-expenses/) is a better fit than a challenge, because it saves toward a known amount by a known date.

## Common mistakes

- **Choosing a ladder you can't afford at the top.** The first month tells you nothing about the last.
- **Treating the challenge as separate from your budget.** If the deposits aren't in the budget, something else will get squeezed.
- **Quitting after one missed week.** The total is a target, not a test.
- **Leaving the money in checking.** It will get spent.
- **Stopping at the finish line.** When the six months end, keep the transfer going, or roll it into the next goal.

## Getting started

1. **Set a goal** and name it.
2. **Check your free cash** and the hardest week or month in the plan.
3. **Choose a template** from the five above, scaled to fit.
4. **Open a separate, fee-free account** and set up the automatic transfers.
5. **Mark a catch-up rule** for missed weeks.
6. **At the finish line, decide what's next** before you get there.
