If you have no credit history, or a damaged one, lenders have little reason to trust you with an unsecured card. A secured card flips that problem around. You hand the issuer a cash deposit up front, and that deposit protects them if you don't pay. In return you get a real credit card that reports to the credit bureaus, which is how you build a track record.

## Who this is for, and who it isn't

**It's for:** people with no credit file, a thin one, or a score hurt by past mistakes, and anyone who can't get approved for an unsecured card yet.

**It's not for:** people who already qualify for a no-fee unsecured card, or anyone who can't spare a few hundred dollars that will sit locked up for a while. If you only want a way to spend money you've already loaded, that's a prepaid or debit card, and those generally don't build credit. Nothing here is personalized financial advice.

## How a secured card works

The mechanics are simple:

1. **You put down a deposit.** Experian's guidance says the minimum often starts around $200, though some issuers let you choose a larger or smaller amount.
2. **The deposit usually sets your limit.** Put down $300 and your limit is typically $300. Some issuers let you go higher than your deposit, but that's the exception.
3. **You use it like any other card.** You make purchases, get a statement, and pay the bill.
4. **The issuer reports your activity to the credit bureaus.** This is what makes the card useful. Your on-time payments and low balances become part of your credit file.

One point that trips people up: **the deposit is not your payment.** If you skip a bill, it's reported as late like any other card, and the issuer may use your deposit to cover what you owe. Treat the card as if the deposit weren't there.

## What to look for in a secured card

Cards differ, and a few details matter far more than the brand:

- **Reports to all three bureaus.** Not all secured cards report equally. Experian advises looking for an issuer that reports to Equifax, Experian, and TransUnion, because a card that reports to only one or two gives you less benefit.
- **Low or no annual fee.** Experian notes some secured cards charge nothing while others run roughly $35 to $49 a year. Paying a fee to build credit is avoidable.
- **A clear path to an unsecured card.** Some issuers review your account automatically after about six months, while others make you ask or apply for a new card. Ask before you apply.
- **A refundable deposit.** Confirm how and when you get it back.
- **A reasonable APR.** You shouldn't plan to carry a balance, but a sky-high rate punishes slip-ups.

## How to use it to build credit

The strategy is deliberately boring:

1. **Put one small, regular bill on the card**, such as a phone plan or streaming subscription.
2. **Set up autopay for the full statement balance**, so a missed payment can't happen by accident.
3. **Keep your balance low.** Experts generally suggest staying well under 30% of your limit, and lower is better. See [credit utilization explained](/blog/credit-utilization-explained/) for how the timing works.
4. **Don't open lots of other accounts at the same time.** New applications add inquiries and lower your average account age.

Payment history carries the most weight in scoring, which is why autopay does most of the work. For the full breakdown of what moves a score, see [what hurts your credit score](/blog/what-hurts-your-credit-score/).

## When to graduate off a secured card

TD's guidance puts the typical window at **6 to 12 months of responsible use**, though your timeline depends on your payment history, utilization, and overall credit profile. Signs you're ready:

- Every payment so far has been on time
- Your utilization has stayed low
- Your score has climbed into at least the fair range (many lenders look for roughly 580 to 669 or better, though it varies by issuer)
- Your issuer offers a review or conversion option

There are two ways to graduate. Your issuer may **convert** the account to an unsecured card, usually returning your deposit as a statement credit. Or you can **apply for a new unsecured card** elsewhere. If you switch issuers, the deposit comes back only after the secured account is closed.

Not every secured card offers a conversion path, and an issuer can say no. If that happens, keep the habits going and try again after your profile improves.

## Should you close the secured card after graduating?

Usually not, if it has no annual fee. Closing an old card can shorten your credit history and reduce your total available credit. If the card carries a fee, ask the issuer about switching to a no-fee version first, since that often keeps the account's age. This is general guidance, so check your own situation.

## A worked example

Sam has no credit history and opens a secured card with a $300 deposit, which gives him a $300 limit. He puts his $30 phone plan on it and sets autopay to pay the full balance each month. That's about 10% utilization. After eight months of on-time payments, he checks his score, sees it has moved into the fair range, and asks his issuer for a review. The issuer converts the card to unsecured, raises his limit, and returns his $300 deposit as a statement credit. He keeps the same account open, so his history stays intact.

## Where people actually go wrong

- **Treating the deposit as a payment.** A missed bill still gets reported as late.
- **Choosing a card that doesn't report to the bureaus.** Then you pay the deposit and build nothing.
- **Paying an annual fee for no reason.** No-fee options exist.
- **Maxing out the card.** A $300 limit fills up fast, and high utilization hurts your score.
- **Only paying the minimum.** Interest adds up quickly, and a balance doesn't help your score.
- **Applying for several cards at once.** Each application can leave an inquiry on your report.

## Getting started

Pick a card that reports to all three bureaus with a low or zero fee, deposit an amount you can comfortably leave untouched, put one small bill on it, and turn on autopay. Review your progress after six months.
