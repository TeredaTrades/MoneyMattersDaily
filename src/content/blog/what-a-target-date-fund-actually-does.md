A target-date fund is a fund that does the asset-allocation work for you. You pick a fund named for the year you expect to retire, such as "2060", and the fund holds a mix of shares and bonds that becomes more cautious as that year gets closer. It is popular in workplace retirement plans for a simple reason: it needs almost no maintenance. But "set and forget" does not mean "the same everywhere," and two funds with the same year can behave very differently. This guide explains what the fund does, the difference between funds, the fee layer to look for and the questions worth asking.

The main sources are the US Securities and Exchange Commission's investor education site (Investor.gov) and FINRA. Funds, fees and plan rules differ by country and provider, and similar products exist elsewhere under other names, such as lifecycle or lifestyle funds.

## Who this is for, and who it isn't

**It's for:** people who want a single diversified retirement investment that adjusts itself, or who have been placed in one by an employer's plan and want to understand it.

**It isn't** a guarantee of retirement income, and it is not automatically the right choice for everyone. If you already hold a mix of funds you are happy with, it may add nothing.

## How it works

Investor.gov describes a target-date fund as one that holds a mix of stocks, bonds and other investments and shifts that mix over time. The year in the name usually marks the year you expect to retire. For example, a "2060" fund is designed for people retiring around 2060. Most are **funds of funds,** meaning the fund holds shares of other funds instead of individual securities.

When retirement is far away, the mix holds more shares, which have higher expected growth and larger swings. As the date approaches, it shifts toward bonds, government securities and cash-like holdings, aiming to reduce the risk of a big loss near the time you need the money.

## The glide path

The **glide path** is the schedule for that shift. FINRA notes that glide paths differ significantly between funds, even funds with the same target year. One 2050 fund might hold far more in shares than another 2050 fund at the same point, and that decides how much it can gain or lose in a bad year.

## "To" versus "through"

This is the detail most people miss.

- A **"to" fund** reaches its most conservative mix on the target date and generally stays there.
- A **"through" fund** keeps shifting after the target date and may not reach its most conservative mix until well after age 65.

Investor.gov points out that, at many points, a "to" fund holds lower-risk and lower-return investments than a "through" fund. Which suits you depends on your comfort with risk and your finances. Some funds also merge into an income fund at the target date, so check what happens then.

## The fees, in two layers

Both Investor.gov and FINRA stress that fees matter. Because a target-date fund usually holds other funds, there can be a fee on the fund itself **and** fees on the funds inside it. Investor.gov says: "Even small differences in fees can translate into large differences in returns over time." The fee table in the prospectus shows both layers. Look at the **total** annual cost, not just the headline.

## What it does not do

- **It does not guarantee income.** Investor.gov and FINRA both say target-date funds can lose value, including after the target date.
- **It does not know your situation.** The automatic mix does not account for your other savings, your pension, your health or your appetite for risk.
- **Reaching the date does not mean you have saved enough.** The result still depends on how much you put in and how long.
- **It is not tax-free by itself.** Outside a tax-advantaged account, the fund can generate taxable dividends, interest and capital gains each year (FINRA).

## A worked example

This uses invented numbers. A 30-year-old plans to retire at 65, which is 35 years away. FINRA's rule of thumb is to pick the fund year closest to your retirement year. The nearest fund might be a 2060 or 2065 fund, depending on what is offered.

Now compare two funds with the same target year and the same gross return of 6% a year, one charging a total of 0.15% a year and one charging 0.75%, with 50,000 invested and left for 30 years:

- At 0.15% a year, the net return is 5.85%, and 50,000 grows to about **275,230**.
- At 0.75% a year, the net return is 5.25%, and 50,000 grows to about **232,078**.

That is a gap of about **43,150**, from a 0.6-point fee difference alone. The 6% return is an assumption for illustration only and not a forecast. Real returns vary, and so do fees. The point is how much a small, steady fee can matter over decades.

## Questions to ask before you pick one

Investor.gov suggests checking:

- **The glide path:** how much is held in shares at each stage, and when the fund reaches its most conservative mix.
- **To or through:** whether it keeps shifting after the target date.
- **Total fees,** including the funds inside it.
- **Whether the year fits you.** A 2050 or 2070 fund may suit you better than the one that matches your birth-date maths, if your plans or risk tolerance differ.
- **How it fits the rest of your money,** including pensions and other savings.
- **Whether you were enrolled automatically.** If an employer put you in one, check that the fund suits you.

## Common mistakes

- **Choosing by the year alone.** Two funds with the same year can differ widely in risk.
- **Ignoring fees,** especially the second layer.
- **Assuming it is guaranteed or low-risk.** It can lose value at any age.
- **Holding it and a lot of other funds** that already overlap, which can undo the mix.
- **Not rechecking.** FINRA suggests looking now and then at whether the fund's approach has changed.
- **Treating it as a substitute for saving enough.**

## Getting started

1. **Find out whether your plan offers target-date funds** and which providers.
2. **Pick the year closest to your planned retirement,** and think about whether you want a more or less cautious path.
3. **Check the glide path, "to" or "through," and the total annual fees** in the fund's documents.
4. **Compare with simple alternatives** such as a broad [index fund](/blog/index-funds-explained-for-beginners/) or an [ETF](/blog/etfs-vs-mutual-funds-whats-the-difference-and-which-fits-you/), which you would rebalance yourself.
5. **Review once a year,** and keep adding to it. See [compound interest explained simply](/blog/compound-interest-explained-simply/) and [how to start investing with little money](/blog/how-to-start-investing-with-little-money/).
