Gold has a reputation as a safe store of value, and it is also an asset whose price can swing hard and stay flat for years. It pays no interest and no dividend. Its price is set by what other people will pay for it, which makes it worth understanding what moves that price before putting money in. This guide covers what drives gold prices, the main ways to own it, what each costs, and where the risks are. It is educational only: it makes no prediction and no recommendation.

Gold sits alongside the other commodities covered in [how to invest in commodities](/blog/how-to-invest-in-commodities-the-main-ways-to-get-exposure-and-the-risks/). Like them, it can lose value, and the future price is unknown.

## Who this is for, and who it isn't

**It's for:** anyone curious about how gold is priced, or trying to compare physical gold with funds and mining stocks.

**It isn't** advice to buy or avoid gold. Whether any gold belongs in a portfolio depends on your goals, time frame and tolerance for risk.

## What moves the price of gold

A National Bank Direct Brokerage learning article (May 14, 2026) describes the main factors as ways of understanding what has moved gold in the past, "not predictions":

- **Real interest rates.** That is the interest rate minus inflation. When real rates are low or negative, holding gold, which pays nothing, costs less compared with holding cash or bonds. This is often described as the most-watched driver.
- **Geopolitical tension and confidence.** Worries about governments, currencies or the financial system can push money toward gold.
- **Central bank buying.** Central banks hold gold in their reserves, and their purchases are a large source of demand.
- **Government debt and currency worries.** Fears that a currency is losing value can support demand.
- **Supply and demand for the metal itself.** The same article says roughly 75% of supply comes from mining and 25% from recycled gold, and that jewellery is the largest end use.

No single factor works all the time, and the drivers can pull in different directions. The link between gold and inflation over short periods is inconsistent.

### A recent snapshot, with a date

The World Gold Council's third-quarter 2026 report, released November 3, 2026, gives a picture of one quarter: total demand of 1,369 tonnes, down 1% year-on-year, worth US$188 billion, up 22% because of higher prices. The average price was US$4,262 per ounce. Central banks added a net 321 tonnes, up 42% year-on-year. Gold ETFs saw inflows of 211 tonnes, with holdings at a record 4,256 tonnes, while jewellery demand fell 17% and bar and coin demand fell 19%. That is one quarter's data, and it says nothing about where the price goes next.

## The main ways to own gold

| Way to own it | What you hold | Main costs | Main risks and trade-offs |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Physical bars and coins** | The metal itself | Dealer markup over the market price, storage, insurance | Selling can be slower and costlier than selling a share; theft and storage risk |
| **Gold-backed ETFs** | Fund shares backed by gold held by the fund | Annual management fee, trading commissions where they apply | May not track the gold price exactly after costs; you do not hold the metal |
| **Gold mining stocks** | Shares in mining companies | Trading commissions where they apply | Company risks (delays, energy costs, politics) on top of the gold price; moves can be larger than gold's |
| **Futures and other derivatives** | Contracts on gold's future price | Margin, fees, rolling costs | Complex and leveraged, and usually aimed at experienced traders |

The first three rows come from the National Bank article. The futures row is general, and that article does not cover it. Local products vary by country: savings schemes, digital gold apps and jewellery all exist, and each has its own fees and its own protections, so check who holds the gold and what happens if the provider fails.

Mining stocks are not the same bet as gold. A mine's profit margin can amplify gains and losses, so the share price can fall even when gold rises.

## Why people hold gold, and the caveats

People mostly cite three reasons: a hedge against inflation or currency weakness, a diversifier that does not move exactly with shares, and a store of value in a crisis. The caveats are real:

- Gold pays no dividend or coupon, so your return depends entirely on the price.
- It can lag other assets for years, particularly during strong stock markets.
- It has its own large swings.
- A hedge that works in some periods may not work in the next one.

## A worked example

This uses invented numbers. Someone wants exposure worth 1,000 in gold at today's market price.

**A coin from a dealer.** The dealer sells at 4% above the market price, so they pay 1,040. A month later, they sell it back to a dealer who pays 2% below the market price, so they get 980, even if the market price did not change. That gap is 1,040 − 980 = **60**, or about **5.8%** of what they paid, before storage or insurance.

**A gold ETF.** Suppose the annual fee is 0.4% and the holding is 5,000. The fee is 5,000 × 0.004 = **20 a year**, or 200 over ten years if the fund's value stayed at 5,000.

The rates are illustrative, and real figures differ by dealer and fund. The takeaway is structural: the metal costs more to **trade**, while a fund costs more to **hold** the longer you stay in.

## Common mistakes

- **Buying because of a headline record.** A price that has already risen can fall just as easily.
- **Ignoring the dealer spread** on coins and bars, which makes quick in-and-out trades expensive.
- **Treating gold as guaranteed.** It is not insured, not income-producing and not protected from losses.
- **Confusing mining stocks with gold.** They carry company risks on top.
- **Putting in more than you can leave alone.** Gold can sit flat for long stretches.
- **Forgetting storage and insurance** when you hold the metal at home.
- **Not checking who holds the gold** in digital or savings products.

## Getting started

1. **Decide the goal first:** inflation hedge, diversifier or curiosity, and how much you could tolerate losing.
2. **Compare how to own it** on cost to buy, cost to hold and cost to sell.
3. **For funds,** read the fee and what backs the fund. See [ETFs vs. mutual funds](/blog/etfs-vs-mutual-funds-whats-the-difference-and-which-fits-you/).
4. **For physical gold,** use a dealer you can verify, get a written receipt, and plan the storage.
5. **Keep it a small, deliberate part** of a wider plan. See [financial order of operations](/blog/financial-order-of-operations/) and [how to protect your savings in a high-inflation country](/blog/how-to-protect-your-savings-in-a-high-inflation-country/).
